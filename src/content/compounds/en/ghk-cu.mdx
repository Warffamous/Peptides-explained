<Section id="what-it-is">

<Lead question="What is GHK-Cu?">A copper-carrying peptide found in blood. It is sold in skin creams and as injections.</Lead>

<Compare title="A skin cream and an injection with GHK-Cu are not the same thing." detail="What is known about each use, from the rules in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia and from published studies, checked on 23 and 24 October 2026.">

| | On the skin (cosmetic cream) | Injected |
|---|---|---|
| Can it be a cosmetic? | <Verdict is="yes" /> It is not a banned or restricted cosmetic ingredient in the US, the UK or Canada. For Australia, this could not be confirmed.<Cite id="fda-cosmetics-prohibited-restricted,uk-cosmetic-products-reg-gb-annexes,ca-hc-cosmetic-ingredient-hotlist,au-aicis-inventory-search-ghk-cu-2026-10-23,au-aicis-cant-find-chemical" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> In the UK and Canada, anything injected is not a cosmetic. No GHK-Cu medicine is approved in the four countries.<Cite id="uk-cosmetic-products-reg-gb-art-2,ca-hc-cosmetic-drug-interface-guidance,openfda-drugsfda-search-stubs-b-2026-10-23,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23" /> |
| Studied in people? | <Verdict is="yes" /> A few small trials, with mixed results.<Cite id="mulder-1994-ghk-diabetic-ulcers,bishop-1992-venous-ulcers-copper-cream,miller-2006-ghk-laser-resurfacing" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No published study.<Cite id="pubmed-ghk-cu-count-2026-10-23" /> |
| Known safety concerns? | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Little data; low skin irritation in cell tests.<Cite id="li-2016-copper-compounds-skin-irritation" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> The FDA warns of possible immune reactions from clumps and impurities.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" /> |

</Compare>

Loren Pickart found the factor in human blood in 1973; it was identified as glycyl-histidyl-lysine (GHK) in 1977, and shown to bind copper in 1980.<Cite id="pickart-1973-ghk-serum,schlesinger-1977-ghk-sequence,pickart-1980-ghk-copper" />

<InDetail title="The molecule, and a wound gel from the 1990s">

GHK-Cu is <Seq>Gly-His-Lys</Seq> holding one copper(II) ion. PubChem gives the complex as C14H23CuN6O4+, mass <Q>402.92 g/mol</Q>; the peptide alone is C14H24N6O4, <Q>340.38 g/mol</Q>.<Cite id="pubchem-ghk-cu" /> Other names include prezatide copper and, on cosmetic labels, copper tripeptide-1.<Cite id="pubchem-ghk-cu" />

In the 1990s a GHK-Cu gel, called "lamin Gel" in the trial report, was tested on diabetic foot ulcers.<Cite id="mulder-1994-ghk-diabetic-ulcers" /> A wound dressing named Iamin Gel was cleared in the US in 1996 as a medical device. That is a different route from approval as a medicine.<Cite id="fda-510k-k953853-iamin" />

</InDetail>

<Section id="mechanism">

<Lead question="How does it work?">In cells it carries copper and boosts collagen. Whether this matters in people is unclear.</Lead>

<Mechanism />

<InDetail title="What each step rests on">

The copper and collagen steps come from cells in a dish, the repair-tissue step from rats.<Cite id="pickart-1980-ghk-copper,maquart-1988-ghk-collagen,maquart-1993-ghk-rat-wounds" /> On the skin, GHK-Cu passes the waterproof outer layer poorly, and how much reaches deeper layers has been little studied.<Cite id="ogorek-2025-ghk-cu-skin-permeation,hostynek-2011-copper-tripeptide-skin" /> In 1980 the discoverers noted effects in cultured cells that ranged "from the stimulation of growth and differentiation to outright toxicity".<Cite id="pickart-1980-ghk-copper" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The United States: cosmetic or drug, and pharmacy compounding">

### Cosmetic or drug

In the US, cosmetics and their ingredients need no FDA approval before they are sold, and GHK-Cu is not among the ingredients that FDA rules ban or restrict.<Cite id="fda-cosmetics-prohibited-restricted" /> What decides whether a product is a cosmetic or a drug is its intended use. Claims that a product will, for example, "regenerate cells" make it a drug, even if it is sold as a cosmetic.<Cite id="fda-cosmetic-or-drug" />

### Pharmacy compounding

The FDA placed injectable GHK-Cu in category 2, for substances that may pose significant safety risks, citing possible immune reactions and "limited data in humans"; that nomination was withdrawn.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" /> GHK-Cu for other routes is in category 1, still under evaluation. While the FDA evaluates a substance in category 1, it does not intend to act against pharmacies that make medicines with it, if the conditions of its guidance are met.<Cite id="fda-503a-categories-2026-05,fda-503a-bulks-page" /> The FDA plans to ask its Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee about GHK-Cu before the end of February 2027.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2027-early-announcement" /> Such a vote is advice only. A listing would allow US pharmacies to compound it on prescription. It would not be an approval, would say nothing about whether it works, and would change nothing outside the US. GHK-Cu is not an approved medicine in the US, the UK, Canada or Australia.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The UK, Canada and Australia">

### United Kingdom

No medicine with a UK licence contains GHK-Cu.<Cite id="uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23" /> Under UK law, a product presented as treating disease, or used to change how the body works through a drug action, is a medicine. It needs a UK licence before it can be sold or supplied.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-2,uk-hmr-2012-reg-46" /> As a cosmetic ingredient, copper tripeptide-1 is neither banned nor restricted in Great Britain. But UK cosmetics law says that anything meant to be injected is not a cosmetic.<Cite id="uk-cosmetic-products-reg-gb-annexes,uk-cosmetic-products-reg-gb-art-2" /> We found no statement by the UK medicines regulator (MHRA) about GHK-Cu itself.<Cite id="uk-govuk-search-status-c-2026-10-23" />

### Canada

Health Canada has not authorised any medicine with GHK-Cu.<Cite id="ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-noc-search-status-c-2026-10-23" /> A skin cream sold to improve the look of the skin can be a cosmetic, and copper tripeptide-1 is not on Health Canada's list of banned or restricted cosmetic ingredients. Products given by injection are not cosmetics, and products sold with treatment claims are assessed as drugs.<Cite id="ca-fda-s2-definitions,ca-hc-cosmetic-ingredient-hotlist,ca-hc-cosmetic-drug-interface-guidance" /> In law, a drug may not be sold in Canada without a drug identification number (DIN), the eight-digit number on an authorised drug's label. A new drug also needs Health Canada's approval before it is sold or advertised.<Cite id="ca-food-and-drug-regulations,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> Health Canada says peptides are "generally regulated as prescription drugs". It lists GHK-Cu among unauthorised injectable peptides it has seized.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> In May 2026 Health Canada published a recall of GHK-Cu powder that had been sold to pharmacies, because it was not an authorised ingredient for medicines.<Cite id="ca-hc-recalls-database" />

### Australia

GHK-Cu is not on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG), the official list of goods that may be supplied in Australia.<Cite id="au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23" /> The TGA, Australia's medicines regulator, names it as an example of unapproved peptide products, "often supplied in injectable form".<Cite id="au-tga-2026-04-peptide-responsibilities" /> In June 2026 the TGA and Australia's Chief Medical Officer warned of liver damage and severe allergic reactions linked to unapproved peptides in general; GHK-Cu was one of the examples they named.<Cite id="au-tga-2026-06-cmo-peptide-statement" /> GHK-Cu is not named in the Poisons Standard, the national list that sorts medicines into schedules such as "prescription only".<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10" /> The TGA says some peptide products can be cosmetics if their ingredients, claims, presentation and intended use are only cosmetic.<Cite id="au-tga-peptide-products-hub" /> Whether GHK-Cu may be used in cosmetics in Australia could not be confirmed from public records.<Cite id="au-aicis-inventory-search-ghk-cu-2026-10-23,au-aicis-cant-find-chemical" />

</InDetail>

<Section id="risks">

<Lead question="What are the risks?">On skin, few problems are known. Injected, its effects are unknown and the product may be impure.</Lead>

| Possible harm | Shown in people? |
|---|---|

<InDetail title="Why the gaps matter">

GHK-Cu changes how cells take up copper. In early cell work, its effects ranged from more growth to outright toxicity, depending on the type of cell.<Cite id="pickart-1980-ghk-copper" /> We found no study that measured what an injection does to copper levels, the liver or other organs in people.<Cite id="pubmed-ghk-cu-count-2026-10-23" /> The FDA found "limited data in humans" to judge the safety of injections.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" /> See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

</InDetail>

### When to stop and get help

<Callout type="stop" title="Stop and get help if" id="stop-now">
- the injection site becomes hot, red, swollen or painful (possible infection);
- you get a fever or chills, or feel very ill, after an injection;
- you get hives, swelling of the face or throat, or difficulty breathing (an allergic reaction).

If it is severe, call your local emergency number.
</Callout>

<InDetail title="Legal routes, and reporting side effects">

### Legal routes

No approved medicine with GHK-Cu exists in the US, the UK, Canada or Australia.<Cite id="openfda-drugsfda-search-stubs-b-2026-10-23,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23" /> In the US, GHK-Cu for use other than by injection is in the FDA's category 1. While the FDA evaluates it, it does not intend to act against pharmacies that make medicines with it, if the conditions of its guidance are met. That is not an approval, and it does not cover injections.<Cite id="fda-503a-categories-2026-05,fda-503a-bulks-page" /> Our page on [treatment routes](/en/legal-routes/) explains each legal route and its limits: a [prescription](/en/legal-routes/#prescription), [off-label use](/en/legal-routes/#off-label), [US pharmacy compounding](/en/legal-routes/#us-compounding), [UK "specials"](/en/legal-routes/#uk-specials), [Canada's Special Access Program](/en/legal-routes/#canada-special-access), [Australia's Special Access Scheme](/en/legal-routes/#australia-sas), [importing for yourself](/en/legal-routes/#personal-import) and [clinical trials](/en/legal-routes/#trials). Each has strict conditions. A US advisory vote on compounding would change nothing in the UK, Canada or Australia.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2027-early-announcement,fda-503a-bulks-page" />

### Report side effects

You can report a side effect even if you are not sure the product caused it. In the US, report to [FDA MedWatch](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medwatch-fda-safety-information-and-adverse-event-reporting-program); in the UK, to the [Yellow Card scheme](https://yellowcard.mhra.gov.uk/); in Canada, to Health Canada's [Canada Vigilance Program](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medeffect-canada/adverse-reaction-reporting.html); in Australia, to the [TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/safety/report-problem/report-adverse-event-or-safety-problem).<Cite id="fda-medwatch,mhra-yellow-card,ca-hc-medeffect-reporting,ca-hc-canada-vigilance,au-tga-report-adverse-event" /> For Ireland and New Zealand, see [our notes page](/en/legal/ireland-and-new-zealand/).

</InDetail>
