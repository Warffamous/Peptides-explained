<Section id="what-it-is">

<Lead question="What is GHK-Cu?">A copper-carrying peptide found in blood. It is sold in skin creams and as injections.</Lead>

<Compare title="A skin cream and an injection with GHK-Cu are not the same thing." detail="What is known about each use, from EU law, the FDA and published studies, checked on 23 October 2026.">

| | On the skin (cosmetic cream) | Injected |
|---|---|---|
| Allowed in the EU? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Listed as a cosmetic ingredient, copper tripeptide-1.<Cite id="cosing-copper-tripeptide-1" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Anything injected is not a cosmetic, and no medicine with GHK-Cu is authorised.<Cite id="eu-regulation-1223-2009-cosmetics,ema-medicines-json" /> |
| Studied in people? | <Verdict is="yes" /> A few small trials, with mixed results.<Cite id="mulder-1994-ghk-diabetic-ulcers,bishop-1992-venous-ulcers-copper-cream,miller-2006-ghk-laser-resurfacing" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No published study.<Cite id="pubmed-ghk-cu-count-2026-10-23" /> |
| Known safety concerns? | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Little data; low skin irritation in cell tests.<Cite id="li-2016-copper-compounds-skin-irritation" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> The FDA warns of possible immune reactions from clumps and impurities.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" /> |

</Compare>

Loren Pickart found the factor in human blood in 1973; it was identified as glycyl-histidyl-lysine (GHK) in 1977, and shown to bind copper in 1980.<Cite id="pickart-1973-ghk-serum,schlesinger-1977-ghk-sequence,pickart-1980-ghk-copper" />

<InDetail title="The molecule, and a wound gel from the 1990s">

GHK-Cu is <Seq>Gly-His-Lys</Seq> holding one copper(II) ion. PubChem gives the complex as C14H23CuN6O4+, mass <Q>402.92 g/mol</Q>; the peptide alone is C14H24N6O4, <Q>340.38 g/mol</Q>.<Cite id="pubchem-ghk-cu" /> Other names include prezatide copper and, on cosmetic labels, copper tripeptide-1.<Cite id="pubchem-ghk-cu,cosing-copper-tripeptide-1" />

In the 1990s a GHK-Cu gel, called "lamin Gel" in the trial report, was tested on diabetic foot ulcers.<Cite id="mulder-1994-ghk-diabetic-ulcers" /> A wound dressing named Iamin Gel was cleared in the US in 1996 as a medical device. That is a different route from approval as a medicine.<Cite id="fda-510k-k953853-iamin" />

</InDetail>

</Section>

<Section id="mechanism">

<Lead question="How does it work?">In cells it carries copper and boosts collagen. Whether this matters in people is unclear.</Lead>

<Mechanism />

<InDetail title="What each step rests on">

The copper and collagen steps come from cells in a dish, the repair-tissue step from rats.<Cite id="pickart-1980-ghk-copper,maquart-1988-ghk-collagen,maquart-1993-ghk-rat-wounds" /> On the skin, GHK-Cu passes the waterproof outer layer poorly, and how much reaches deeper layers has been little studied.<Cite id="ogorek-2025-ghk-cu-skin-permeation,hostynek-2011-copper-tripeptide-skin" /> In 1980 the discoverers noted effects in cultured cells that ranged "from the stimulation of growth and differentiation to outright toxicity".<Cite id="pickart-1980-ghk-copper" />

</InDetail>

</Section>

<Section id="evidence">

<Lead question="Does it work?">For skin and wounds, small trials gave mixed results. Injections have never been tested.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="One gel trial on foot sores was positive" value="98.5%" label="median ulcer closure with the gel, against 60.8% with a dummy gel" who="Mulder 1994: multicentre, evaluator-blinded trial; patient numbers not given in the abstract" source="mulder-1994-ghk-diabetic-ulcers" level="human-controlled" />

<KeyNumber title="On leg ulcers, the copper cream did no better than an inert cream" value="86" label="patients with leg ulcers compared three creams" who="Bishop 1992: randomised, evaluator-blinded trial" source="bishop-1992-venous-ulcers-copper-cream" level="human-controlled" />

<KeyNumber title="After a skin laser, experts saw no change" value="13" label="patients finished; only they rated their skin better" who="Miller 2006: randomised trial after laser skin resurfacing" source="miller-2006-ghk-laser-resurfacing" level="human-controlled" />

Most research is in cells and animals, from many groups.<Cite id="pubmed-ghk-cu-count-2026-10-23" /> A trial of a GHK-Cu gel on small skin wounds in 60 healthy adults started in 2026.<Cite id="ctgov-nct07437586" />

<InDetail title="Every study in one table">

<StudyTable />

None of these trials used injections, and most are decades old. Claims about hair growth, wrinkles or whole-body "repair" rest on cell, animal or small cosmetic studies, not on controlled trials of injected GHK-Cu.<Cite id="pubmed-ghk-cu-count-2026-10-23" />

</InDetail>

<Section id="regulatory">

<Lead question="Is it legal?">In EU skin creams, yes. As an injection it is not an approved medicine.</Lead>

<Compare title="OK in skin creams. Not approved as a medicine." detail="Status in the Netherlands, the EU and the US, and in sport, checked on 23 October 2026.">

| | Netherlands and the EU | United States |
|---|---|---|
| Approved as a medicine? | <Verdict is="no" /> No EU or Dutch authorisation.<Cite id="ema-medicines-json,cbg-geneesmiddeleninformatiebank" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Not FDA-approved.<Cite id="openfda-drugsfda-search-stubs-b-2026-10-23" /> |
| Allowed in cosmetics? | <Verdict is="yes" /> As copper tripeptide-1, with no special limits listed.<Cite id="cosing-copper-tripeptide-1" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> We did not check US cosmetic rules. |
| Allowed in sport? | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Not named. It may fall under S0, the ban on unapproved substances.<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en,wada-2027-list-en" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> The same worldwide rule.<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en" /> |

</Compare>

<InDetail title="Status in each country, and in sport">

<RegulatoryTable />

All statuses were checked on 23 October 2026. Under EU law a cosmetic is put on the outside of the body, and anything meant to be injected is not a cosmetic.<Cite id="eu-regulation-1223-2009-cosmetics" /> Dutch law forbids supplying a medicine without a marketing authorisation, with narrow exceptions.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" />

In sport, GHK-Cu is not named on the 2026 or 2027 List. The S0 class bans substances that no government health authority has approved for treatment, and the 2027 List adds "peptides" to its examples. WADA notes that a peptide not named on the List may still be banned.<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en,wada-2027-list-en,wada-2027-list-explanatory-note" /> No medicine with GHK-Cu is approved, but a gel dressing was cleared as a device, and WADA has not ruled.<Cite id="fda-510k-k953853-iamin" /> Athletes should ask their anti-doping organisation before using any form.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The United States: pharmacy compounding">

The FDA placed injectable GHK-Cu in category 2, for substances that may pose significant safety risks, citing possible immune reactions and "limited data in humans"; that nomination was withdrawn.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" /> GHK-Cu for other routes is in category 1, still under evaluation.<Cite id="fda-503a-categories-2026-05" /> The FDA plans to ask its Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee about GHK-Cu before the end of February 2027.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2027-early-announcement" /> Such a vote is advice only. A listing would allow US pharmacies to compound it on prescription; it would not be a marketing authorisation and would change nothing in the EU.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" />

</InDetail>

<Section id="risks">

<Lead question="What are the risks?">On skin, few problems are known. Injected, its effects are unknown and the product may be impure.</Lead>

<Compare title="The main risks of injecting it come from what is unknown." detail="What the FDA and the published studies say, as of 23 October 2026.">

| Possible harm | Shown in people? |
|---|---|
| An immune reaction to the peptide, its clumps or impurities | <Verdict is="unknown" /> The FDA's concern for injected forms; not studied.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" /> |
| Infection from germs in a non-sterile product | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Not studied for GHK-Cu; a known risk of any unsterile injection.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis" /> |
| Skin irritation from creams | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Low in cell tests; no systematic human data.<Cite id="li-2016-copper-compounds-skin-irritation" /> |

</Compare>

<InDetail title="Why the gaps matter">

GHK-Cu changes how cells take up copper. In early cell work, its effects ranged from more growth to outright toxicity, depending on the type of cell.<Cite id="pickart-1980-ghk-copper" /> We found no study that measured what an injection does to copper levels, the liver or other organs in people.<Cite id="pubmed-ghk-cu-count-2026-10-23" /> The FDA found "limited data in humans" to judge the safety of injections.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" /> See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

</InDetail>

<Section id="harm-reduction">

<Lead question="How can someone lower the risk?">Check the product first, keep creams on the skin, and know when to stop.</Lead>

<ProductCheck extra="Check what the label says the product is for. Cosmetic GHK-Cu is made for the skin. Under EU law, nothing meant for injection is a cosmetic." extraSources="eu-regulation-1223-2009-cosmetics,cosing-copper-tripeptide-1" />

### When to stop and get help

<Callout type="stop" title="Stop and get help if" id="stop-now">
- the injection site becomes hot, red, swollen or painful (possible infection);
- you get a fever or chills, or feel very ill, after an injection;
- you get hives, swelling of the face or throat, or difficulty breathing (an allergic reaction).

If it is severe, call your local emergency number.
</Callout>

<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis,nhs-sepsis,nhs-anaphylaxis,fda-category-2-safety-risks" />

<WarningSigns only="general,injection,allergic" />

<InDetail title="Checks that make sense">

A doctor can help you decide which checks fit you (see [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/)). Tell them about any product you use, including creams. Watch the skin where you put it: a rash that spreads or does not settle is a reason to stop. For injecting, see [sterile technique](/en/safety/sterile-technique/).
