<Section id="what-it-is">

<SequenceView />

BPC-157 is a synthetic peptide: a chain of 15 amino acids, <Seq>Gly-Glu-Pro-Pro-Pro-Gly-Lys-Pro-Ala-Asp-Asp-Ala-Gly-Leu-Val</Seq>, with ordinary free ends. Its formula is C62H98N16O22 and its mass is <Q>1419.5 Da</Q>.<Cite id="pubchem-bpc-157" /> Early laboratory work reported that it resists breakdown in gastric juice.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> <Claim level="hypothesis">Four of its amino acids are proline, and proline-rich stretches resist many digestive enzymes, which may explain that stability</Claim>. In blood, by contrast, it is broken down within minutes.<Cite id="he-2022-pharmacokinetics" />

### Where the name comes from

"BPC" stands for Body Protection Compound. In 1993 a group at the University of Zagreb reported isolating a protein of about 40,000 daltons from human gastric juice. They named it BPC and described BPC-157 as a 15-amino-acid fragment "thought to be essential for its activity".<Cite id="sikiric-1993-bpc-origin" /> That origin rests on the group's own reports. The FDA describes the peptide as "reported to be" such a fragment, and an independent review notes that its sequence resembles no known gut peptide.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,jozwiak-2025-review" /> The peptide used in research is made by chemical synthesis.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,he-2022-pharmacokinetics" />

In the 1990s and 2000s the Croatian company PLIVA developed it under the codes PL 14736, PL-10 and PLD-116; the name bepecin also appears in databases.<Cite id="chembl-bpc-157,staresinic-2003-achilles,tkalcevic-2007-egr1" /> It has no official non-proprietary name. The FDA notes that "BPC-157" is only a common name, and that different chemical substances are sold under it.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

### How it is made

It is made by solid-phase peptide synthesis: amino acids are added one at a time to a chain anchored on resin beads, then the chain is cut free and purified. Each step can leave shortened or wrongly built chains, solvents and reagents behind. Commercial material comes as a free base or as an acetate salt, which the FDA treats as different substances; PubChem also lists a trifluoroacetate salt.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,pubchem-bpc-157" />

### Why people have heard of it

BPC-157 is promoted online for tendon, ligament, muscle and gut problems, with record search interest in 2024.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,vasireddi-2025-systematic-review" /> That popularity does not rest on human trials, because there are almost none.

</Section>

<Section id="mechanism">

Nobody knows how BPC-157 works, or whether it does anything in people. The FDA's 2026 review says its molecular targets "have not been identified and its mechanisms of action remain poorly understood".<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> What exists is a set of observations in cells and rats that no single study connects, and none was made in a person.

<Mechanism />

### Step 1: the missing receptor

A drug normally starts by binding one specific target, such as a receptor or an enzyme. That binding is what makes its effects predictable and dose-dependent. For BPC-157 no target is established.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> In 2026 a Chinese laboratory reported that, in cultured human endothelial cells, <Claim level="in-vitro">BPC-157 binds the protein FBXO22 and so protects the transcription factor BACH1 from breakdown, increasing cell growth and tube formation</Claim>.<Cite id="zhang-2026-fbxo22" /> It is the first specific partner proposed, from one laboratory, and not yet repeated.

There is also a timing puzzle. In rats and dogs, intact BPC-157 leaves the blood within minutes, cut into fragments and then amino acids,<Cite id="he-2022-pharmacokinetics" /> yet animal studies report effects that build up over days. Why is unknown.

### Steps 2 to 4: signals and cell behaviour

Most signalling data come from one laboratory at Chang Gung University in Taiwan, which has no link to Zagreb:

- In human endothelial cells, <Claim level="in-vitro">BPC-157 raised levels of VEGFR2, the main receptor for the vessel-growth signal VEGF, and activated the VEGFR2–Akt–eNOS pathway</Claim>.<Cite id="hsieh-2017-vegfr2" />
- In isolated rat aorta, <Claim level="in-vitro">it relaxed the vessel through nitric oxide made by the lining cells; blocking nitric oxide synthesis with L-NAME abolished the effect</Claim>.<Cite id="hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos" />
- In rat tendon fibroblasts, <Claim level="in-vitro">it increased activation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and paxillin, proteins that let a cell grip its surroundings and crawl, and the cells migrated faster and survived oxidative stress better</Claim>. They did not divide faster.<Cite id="chang-2011-tendon-fibroblasts" />
- In the same cells, <Claim level="in-vitro">it raised the number of growth hormone receptors, so added growth hormone made the cells multiply more</Claim>.<Cite id="chang-2014-ghr" />

The developer's laboratory reported that <Claim level="in-vitro">in a human gut-cell line PL 14736 switched on EGR-1, a gene cells turn on within minutes of injury, faster than the authorised growth factor becaplermin</Claim>.<Cite id="tkalcevic-2007-egr1" />

The laboratories do not always agree. The Taiwanese group saw more vessel-like tubes in culture; the Zagreb group reported "no direct angiogenic effect of BPC 157 on cell cultures".<Cite id="hsieh-2017-vegfr2,brcic-2009-angiogenesis" /> The nitric-oxide link is also untidy. The independent laboratory found eNOS activation, but in the Zagreb group's own rat experiments the protective effects largely persisted when nitric oxide synthesis was blocked.<Cite id="sikiric-1997-nitric-oxide,klicek-2008-fistula" /> These findings do not yet fit one causal story.

### Steps 5 and 6: tissue and healing in animals

<Claim level="animal">Injured rat muscle and tendon showed more blood vessels and stronger VEGF staining</Claim>, <Claim level="animal">blood flow recovered faster in rats with a blocked leg artery</Claim>, and <Claim level="animal">wounds in rats and diabetic mice organised their collagen earlier</Claim>.<Cite id="brcic-2009-angiogenesis,hsieh-2017-vegfr2,tkalcevic-2007-egr1" /> The Zagreb reports of faster functional healing are discussed under the evidence below.

### What the chain does not show

The chain has three weak joints. No study measured the early steps and the healing outcome in the same animals, so the link between them is inferred. Nobody knows what concentration reaches a human tendon, so the cell concentrations cannot be translated. And many animal studies report the same benefit across doses a million-fold apart.<Cite id="staresinic-2003-achilles,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> A real drug effect usually grows with dose up to a ceiling, so a flat response across that range weakens the causal inference.

</Section>

### Cells: one independent laboratory

The four cell studies in the table come from one group at Chang Gung University: independent of Zagreb, but not of each other, and not repeated elsewhere.<Cite id="chang-2011-tendon-fibroblasts,chang-2014-ghr,hsieh-2017-vegfr2,hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos" /> They show that the molecule can change a pathway in a dish, not that it helps a damaged tendon in a person.

### The originating group's animal studies

Most of the literature is animal work by Predrag Sikirić, Sven Seiwerth and colleagues in Zagreb: by our count, about 140 primary experimental papers.<Cite id="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" /> The history page [how one laboratory built the BPC-157 literature](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/) explains the count and why it matters.

Two studies show the typical design. In 2003 the group cut the Achilles tendon in rats and gave daily intraperitoneal BPC-157 at <Q>10 µg/kg</Q>, <Q>10 ng/kg</Q> or <Q>10 pg/kg</Q>, or saline, starting 30 minutes after surgery. <Claim level="animal">Treated tendons were stronger and stiffer, the rats walked better, and the defect closed over 14 days</Claim>.<Cite id="staresinic-2003-achilles" /> In 2008 the group created colon-to-skin fistulas in rats, and <Claim level="animal">BPC-157 by injection or in drinking water closed them</Claim>.<Cite id="klicek-2008-fistula" /> Similar results were reported for ligament, muscle, nerve and gut injuries.<Cite id="cerovecki-2010-ligament,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

The FDA's 2026 review made three criticisms.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> Low and high doses usually worked equally well, so no dose–response relationship was shown. Treatment usually started at the moment of injury, so it is unknown whether BPC-157 helps once damage is established, which is when a person would use it. And without a known target, biological plausibility is hard to judge. The abstracts of the studies cited here also do not say whether outcome assessors were blinded.

### Independent animal studies

Groups outside Zagreb have tested BPC-157 in their own rodent models. As early as 1995, an industry laboratory at Parke-Davis in the US found that <Claim level="animal">the peptide (then called BPC-15) reduced chemically induced colon damage in rats in a dose-dependent way</Claim>, one of the few reports with a dose–response.<Cite id="veljaca-1995-colitis" />

A direct repeat of the flagship tendon claim came only in 2026. A Turkish team repaired the Achilles tendons of 32 rats and gave BPC-157, TB-500, both, or nothing for four weeks. <Claim level="animal">BPC-157 gave higher load to failure and better tissue scores than control, but the differences were not statistically significant</Claim>; TB-500's were, and the combination added nothing.<Cite id="bicer-2026-achilles" /> With eight rats per group the study could miss a modest effect. Its result fits a benefit smaller than the "full recovery" the Zagreb studies describe, or none.

### Toxicology and pharmacokinetics

The only formal toxicology comes from the Fourth Military Medical University in Xi'an, China, which was developing BPC-157 for wounds and burns. <Claim level="animal">Across mice, rats, rabbits and dogs, single and 28-day repeated intramuscular doses caused no serious toxicity; genotoxicity tests were negative, and a rat embryo–fetal study found no malformations</Claim>.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety" /> The FDA's re-analysis of the same data flagged possible safety signals (see the risk profile below).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

The same laboratory studied 366 rats and 6 beagle dogs. Intact BPC-157 had a half-life of <Q>15.2 min</Q> in rats and <Q>5.3 min</Q> in dogs after intravenous dosing. Only part of an intramuscular dose reached the blood, and the peptide was degraded to fragments and then amino acids.<Cite id="he-2022-pharmacokinetics" /> There are no such data for subcutaneous, oral or nasal use in any species, and none in humans.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

### Human evidence

**The PL 14736 programme.** PLIVA developed the peptide as an enema for inflammatory bowel disease. According to the FDA's summary of conference abstracts, a placebo-controlled phase 1 study randomised 32 healthy men. Headache and flatulence were the commonest complaints, at similar rates with peptide and placebo, and the peptide was mostly undetectable in blood.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> In a randomised, double-blind phase 2 trial in 53 people with mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis, <Claim level="human-controlled">a disease-activity score fell by 3.2 points with the peptide and 1.6 with placebo, and the confidence interval for the difference included zero</Claim>.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> That trial appeared only as a 2005 conference abstract, which the FDA judged "inadequate to support the efficacy and safety" of BPC-157. No full report or later trial has been published since.

**Three small uncontrolled reports.** All three share one lead author, took place at private clinics in Florida, and used products from US compounding pharmacies.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

- In a retrospective chart review of knee-joint injections (some combined with thymosin beta-4), <Claim level="human-limited">14 of 16 people reached by phone months later said their pain had improved</Claim>.<Cite id="lee-2021-knee" />
- Twelve women with interstitial cystitis that had not responded to standard treatment had injections into the bladder wall; <Claim level="human-limited">10 rated their symptoms as fully resolved, and no adverse events were reported</Claim>.<Cite id="lee-2024-interstitial-cystitis" />
- In two adults given intravenous infusions on consecutive days, <Claim level="human-limited">blood tests and vital signs did not change</Claim>.<Cite id="lee-2025-intravenous" />

A 2026 systematic review found only these three studies, 30 people in total, and rated the certainty of evidence as very low.<Cite id="poolton-2026-human-sr" />

Uncontrolled reports cannot show benefit, for structural reasons. Knee and bladder pain come and go on their own. People seek treatment when symptoms are worst, so many improve afterwards whatever is done (regression to the mean). An injection from a confident clinician produces a real placebo response, and asking people months later adds recall bias. Only a comparison group given a dummy treatment separates these effects from a drug effect.

### Registered trials

As of 20 October 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov lists four studies: a 2015 phase 1 study of oral tablets in Mexico, status unknown, with no results;<Cite id="ctgov-nct02637284" /> a randomised, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial of subcutaneous BPC-157 for acute hamstring strain in 120 people in China, recruiting, with primary completion expected in February 2027;<Cite id="ctgov-nct07437547" /> a 30-person randomised pilot after rotator-cuff surgery at a US university, not yet recruiting;<Cite id="ctgov-nct07803250" /> and a completed single-arm study of an oral consumer product, run by its maker, with no results posted.<Cite id="ctgov-nct07752381" />

<Callout type="uncertain" title="What would change this page">
The hamstring trial is the first properly designed efficacy trial of BPC-157 in an injury: randomised, placebo-controlled, with imaging endpoints and an independent safety committee.<Cite id="ctgov-nct07437547" /> If it is published in full, whatever the result, it will tell us more than all existing human data. We will update the grades when it reports.
</Callout>

### Where the evidence is genuinely promising

This evidence is not worthless. Across many rodent models of tendon, ligament, muscle and gut injury the direction of effect is consistent, and laboratories outside Zagreb (in the US, Taiwan and Turkey among others) have reported effects in the same direction.<Cite id="veljaca-1995-colitis,hsieh-2017-vegfr2,bicer-2026-achilles" /> Animal toxicology so far shows no genotoxicity and no acute toxicity at high doses.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety" /> Independent reviewers in sports medicine describe the preclinical record as promising while stressing how little human data exist.<Cite id="vasireddi-2025-systematic-review,mcguire-2025-review" /> Tendon and ligament injuries heal slowly and few medicines speed that healing, so a properly tested healing agent would be valuable.

The gap has a real cost. In public records the developer's programme never went beyond a phase 2 trial that was not fully published, and two decades passed without a definitive trial. Into that gap came a grey market of unmonitored injections of products of unknown content. Promising animal data are a reason to run a good trial, not a substitute for one.

</Section>

<Section id="unknowns">

As of 20 October 2026, these are specifically unknown:

- **Whether it works in people.** No controlled human trial has been published in full.<Cite id="poolton-2026-human-sr" />
- **How it works.** No receptor or primary target is established.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **Human pharmacokinetics.** There are no data for subcutaneous, oral, nasal or transdermal use. After an enema it was mostly undetectable in blood.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **Dose–response** in any species.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **Long-term safety.** The longest repeat-dose animal study lasted 28 days, and no person has been followed systematically beyond two weeks of use.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **Cancer risk.** No carcinogenicity study exists, and effects on existing tumours are untested.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **Reproduction.** There is one rat embryo–fetal study, with no fertility, postnatal or human pregnancy data.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **Immune reactions.** Immunogenicity has never been formally studied.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **Interactions** with any other medicine.
- **Whether the salt forms** (free base, acetate, trifluoroacetate) behave alike.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **What products contain.** Anti-doping laboratories confirmed the peptide in confiscated vials,<Cite id="cox-2017-doping-detection" /> but we found no published survey of the content, impurities or sterility of products on the market.

</Section>

### European Union and member states

No BPC-157 product has an EU marketing authorisation. EMA's medicines data, which include refused and withdrawn applications, contain no record of it, and the European Pharmacopoeia has no monograph.<Cite id="ema-medicines-search,ema-medicines-json,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> The FDA found no approved product in any country.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

- **Netherlands.** The CBG database lists no BPC-157 product.<Cite id="cbg-geneesmiddeleninformatiebank" /> Under the Geneesmiddelenwet a substance is a medicine if it is presented as treating disease, wounds or pain, or is meant to change body functions through a pharmacological effect (art. 1). Selling, supplying, stocking or importing a medicine without a marketing authorisation is prohibited, with narrow exceptions (art. 40).<Cite id="nl-geneesmiddelenwet" />
- **Germany.** Finished medicines may be marketed only with a national licence or an EU authorisation (AMG § 21); BPC-157 has neither.<Cite id="de-amg-21,ema-medicines-json" />
- **Belgium.** There is no EU authorisation and no approved product anywhere.<Cite id="ema-medicines-json,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

### United Kingdom

Selling or supplying an unauthorised medicinal product is prohibited (Human Medicines Regulations 2012, regulation 46), and no BPC-157 product is authorised.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

### United States

BPC-157 is not FDA-approved, has no US Pharmacopeia monograph and is part of no approved drug. A pharmacy may therefore compound with it only once it is placed on the 503A bulks list through rulemaking.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> The FDA had earlier put BPC-157 in Category 2 of its interim policy, for substances that may present significant safety risks. When the nominators withdrew, it moved to a list of withdrawn substances; on the FDA's 14 May 2026 list it is in no category.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks,fda-503a-categories-2026-05" />

The FDA nevertheless evaluated BPC-157 on its own initiative, for ulcerative colitis only, and its staff concluded that it should not be listed: it is not well characterised, and effectiveness and safety are unsupported.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" /> What happened next is the most misreported fact about BPC-157:

- On 23 July 2026 the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) voted 8–6 with one abstention to recommend BPC-157 for the 503A bulk drug substances list.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-questions" />
- The committee considered seven peptides over two days and recommended six: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax and epitalon. Emideltide (DSIP) was not recommended.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" />
- The vote is advisory and non-binding, and it went against the FDA staff's own recommendation.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" />
- Legal compounding remains prohibited until the FDA formally adopts the recommendation and completes rulemaking.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" />
- None of these is an approved medicine. A 503A listing, if it ever happens, would allow pharmacy compounding on prescription in the US. It is not a marketing authorisation, says nothing about efficacy, and changes nothing in the EU or the Netherlands.

Even a listing would not bring product oversight. The FDA reminded the committee that a compounded drug is not FDA-approved, need not follow good manufacturing practice, and need not have its adverse events reported. Unless the listing sets limits, the substance can be compounded for any use and route.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1" />

The FDA documents confirm the date, the seven peptides, the voting questions and the staff recommendation. The FDA had not posted written minutes by 20 October 2026, so the vote counts and the six-of-seven outcome are as reported from the public meeting; we will check them against the minutes.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting" />

### Sport (WADA)

BPC-157 has been named on the World Anti-Doping Agency's Prohibited List since 1 January 2022, under S0 (non-approved substances), and is banned at all times.<Cite id="wada-2022-list-modifications,wada-2026-list-en" /> The 2027 List, in force from 1 January 2027, still names it and adds "peptides" in general to the S0 examples.<Cite id="wada-2027-list-en" /> (A 2025 review states that it is no longer listed; that is wrong.<Cite id="jozwiak-2025-review" />) S0 covers substances with "no current approval by any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use", so a US compounding listing, which is not an approval, would not by itself change that status.<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en" />

<Section id="risks">

### What has been reported in people

The published human studies reported no serious adverse events, but the FDA notes they were short, small and said little about how safety was monitored.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> The FDA's adverse-event database held three reports up to 4 December 2025, all after injections: nine days of redness and swelling at the injection site (in someone also injecting thymosin); shortness of breath leading to an emergency visit; and diffuse darkening of the skin and gums after a week of a combined BPC-157 and TB-500 product labelled "for research purposes only", which recurred on restarting and stopped on stopping.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> None proves that BPC-157 was the cause. US compounding pharmacies do not have to report adverse events, so the small number of reports says little about how often problems occur.

### What the animal toxicology flags

The Chinese toxicology paper called BPC-157 well tolerated. The FDA's re-analysis of the same data described "clinically relevant safety signals":<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

- **Clotting.** A shortened clotting time (aPTT) in male rats and a prolonged one in dogs. In humans a shortened aPTT is a known risk factor for clots.
- **Liver and blood fats.** Rises in a liver enzyme (ALT), glucose and triglycerides in female rats after treatment stopped, and raised triglycerides in female dogs.
- **Kidney marker.** Lower creatinine in dogs.

Each came from a single 28-day study, and longer studies would be needed to tell whether the signals are real.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

### Product quality and immune reactions

For most people the most immediate risk is the product. The certificates the FDA reviewed did not identify impurities or report tests for bacterial endotoxin or microbial contamination, both essential for anything injected.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> Peptides can also clump together, and synthesis leaves near-copies of the chain; both present the repeated, unfamiliar patterns the immune system is built to notice. The FDA concluded that, injected or sprayed into the nose, BPC-157 "may pose a significant risk for immunogenicity", made worse by aggregation and impurities and never formally studied.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> Consequences range from silent antibodies to allergic reactions. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

### Why an effect on cancer is theorised

The reasoning combines well-established cancer biology with findings about BPC-157 from cells and rats:

1. **Tumours need blood vessels.** Beyond a very small size a tumour can grow only by recruiting new vessels, which it does by releasing signals, chiefly VEGF. Inducing angiogenesis is a recognised hallmark of cancer.<Cite id="hanahan-2011-hallmarks" /> VEGF acts mainly through VEGFR2, and medicines that block this pathway are used to slow some cancers.<Cite id="carmeliet-2011-angiogenesis" />
2. **BPC-157 pushes on that pathway.** <Claim level="in-vitro">It increased VEGFR2 levels and signalling in human endothelial cells</Claim> and <Claim level="animal">increased vessel growth in ischaemic rat muscle</Claim>.<Cite id="hsieh-2017-vegfr2" />
3. **Cancer cells crawl with repair machinery.** FAK and paxillin let a fibroblast crawl into a wound. In many cancers FAK is over-active and helps tumour cells survive, migrate and invade; it is a target of experimental cancer drugs.<Cite id="sulzmaier-2014-fak-cancer" /> <Claim level="in-vitro">BPC-157 increased FAK and paxillin activation in tendon fibroblasts</Claim>.<Cite id="chang-2011-tendon-fibroblasts" />
4. **More growth hormone receptors amplify growth signals.** <Claim level="in-vitro">BPC-157 raised growth hormone receptor numbers in tendon cells</Claim>;<Cite id="chang-2014-ghr" /> whether it does so in other cells, including tumour cells, is untested.

So the worry is not that BPC-157 creates cancer: it was not genotoxic in standard tests.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety" /> The worry is that something which helps vessels sprout and cells migrate could help an existing, perhaps undiagnosed, tumour grow or spread. Genotoxicity tests detect the DNA damage that starts a cancer; they cannot detect promotion of one that already exists. That needs long-term carcinogenicity studies and human follow-up. No carcinogenicity studies of BPC-157 in the regulatory sense have been published.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

There are honest counter-arguments. The originating group argues that BPC-157 "controls" angiogenesis rather than simply driving it, and reports anti-tumour effects in some models.<Cite id="sikiric-2025-comment" /> An independent review found only one cancer-related study, a mouse model of cancer weight loss in which tumour volume did not differ significantly.<Cite id="jozwiak-2025-review" /> No published study shows tumour promotion. The risk is plausible but untested.

A precedent shows why the question matters. Becaplermin (Regranex), an authorised growth-factor gel for diabetic foot ulcers, "promotes cellular proliferation and angiogenesis". Its 2011 label carried a boxed warning about more cancer deaths in people using three or more tubes. The warning was removed in November 2018, when the label reported the signal in one of three post-marketing studies, but the label still warns about cancers and forbids use over a known tumour.<Cite id="fda-regranex-label-2011,fda-regranex-label-2018" /> Such signals only emerge, and are only resolved, with large human datasets. BPC-157 has none.

### Sport

BPC-157 is prohibited in sport at all times,<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en" /> and methods to detect it in urine have been published.<Cite id="cox-2017-doping-detection" />

<Section id="harm-reduction">

This section is for people who use BPC-157 or have decided to. It is not a recommendation, and it gives no amounts, schedules or sources.

### Talk to a doctor first

A doctor can check for the conditions where the theoretical risks matter most (cancer, pregnancy, clotting problems) and make sure the injury itself is properly diagnosed. A torn tendon or ligament may need imaging or surgery within weeks, and self-treatment can delay care that works. See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

### Know what you cannot know about the product

A vial labelled BPC-157 may hold a different salt, more or less peptide, near-copies of the chain, solvents or bacterial toxins, and nobody checked it ([product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/)). If you inject anything, follow [sterile technique](/en/safety/sterile-technique/): most immediate harm from injected products comes from infection and contamination.

### Checks that make mechanistic sense

Discuss these with a doctor, who decides what is appropriate:

<Checklist>

- **Cancer screening due for your age, and any unexplained lump, bleeding or weight loss checked first**, because of the theoretical effect on blood-vessel growth.
- **Liver enzymes and blood fats**, because these rose in female rats and dogs.
- **Clotting tests, especially if you take a blood thinner or have had a clot**, because clotting times changed in rats and dogs.
- **Kidney tests, telling the lab you use BPC-157**, because creatinine, the standard kidney marker, fell in dogs, and a falsely low value could hide a problem.

</Checklist>

### Stop and get help if

- the injection site becomes hot, red, swollen or painful, or you get a fever (infection or contamination);
- you get hives, swelling of the face or throat, or difficulty breathing (allergic reaction);
- your skin or gums darken, as in one reported case;
- you notice a new lump, unexplained bleeding or bruising, or pain and swelling in one leg.

### Plausible interactions (none has been studied)

- **Blood thinners and antiplatelet medicines**, because of the clotting changes in animals.
- **Medicines that lower blood pressure or act through nitric oxide** (such as nitrates or sildenafil), because BPC-157 relaxed isolated arteries through nitric oxide.<Cite id="hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos" />
- **Cancer medicines that block blood-vessel growth**, because BPC-157 pushes the same pathway the other way.
- **Combination products with other peptides**, such as TB-500 blends, which add a second unknown, make any reaction impossible to attribute, and gave no extra benefit in rats.<Cite id="bicer-2026-achilles,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

### Who should not use it at all

- **Anyone pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive**: there are no human data, only one rat embryo–fetal study, and new vessel growth is central to pregnancy.
- **Anyone with cancer, a history of cancer or an undiagnosed lump**, because of the angiogenesis and FAK mechanisms above.
- **Children and teenagers**, whose tissues are still growing and for whom there are no data.
- **Anyone with a clotting disorder or on anticoagulants**, because of the animal clotting signals.
- **Athletes subject to anti-doping rules**, because it is prohibited at all times.
