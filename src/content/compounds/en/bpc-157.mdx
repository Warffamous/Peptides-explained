BPC-157 is promoted online for tendon, ligament, muscle and gut problems, with record search interest in 2024.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,vasireddi-2025-systematic-review" /> That popularity does not rest on human trials, because there are almost none.

<InDetail title="The molecule, and how it is made">

BPC-157 is a synthetic peptide: a chain of 15 amino acids, <Seq>Gly-Glu-Pro-Pro-Pro-Gly-Lys-Pro-Ala-Asp-Asp-Ala-Gly-Leu-Val</Seq>, with ordinary free ends. Its formula is C62H98N16O22 and its mass is <Q>1419.5 Da</Q>.<Cite id="pubchem-bpc-157" /> Early laboratory work reported that it resists breakdown in gastric juice.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> <Claim level="hypothesis">Four of its amino acids are proline, and proline-rich stretches resist many digestive enzymes, which may explain that stability</Claim>. In blood, by contrast, it is broken down within minutes.<Cite id="he-2022-pharmacokinetics" />

### How it is made

It is made by solid-phase peptide synthesis: amino acids are added one at a time to a chain anchored on resin beads, then the chain is cut free and purified. Each step can leave shortened or wrongly built chains, solvents and reagents behind. Commercial material comes as a free base or as an acetate salt, which the FDA treats as different substances; PubChem also lists a trifluoroacetate salt.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,pubchem-bpc-157" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Where the name comes from, and who developed it">

"BPC" stands for Body Protection Compound. In 1993 a group at the University of Zagreb described a protein of about 40,000 daltons isolated from human gastric juice. They named it BPC and described BPC-157 as a 15-amino-acid fragment "thought to be essential for its activity".<Cite id="sikiric-1993-bpc-origin,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> That origin rests on the group's own reports. The FDA describes the peptide as "reported to be" such a fragment, and an independent review notes that its sequence resembles no known gut peptide.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,jozwiak-2025-review" /> The peptide used in research is made by chemical synthesis.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,he-2022-pharmacokinetics" />

In the 1990s and 2000s the Croatian company PLIVA developed it under the codes PL 14736 and PLD-116; the names PL-10 and bepecin also appear in databases.<Cite id="chembl-bpc-157,staresinic-2003-achilles,tkalcevic-2007-egr1" /> It has no official non-proprietary name. The FDA notes that "BPC-157" is only a common name, and that different chemical substances are sold under it.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

</InDetail>

The FDA's 2026 review says its molecular targets "have not been identified and its mechanisms of action remain poorly understood".<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> What exists is a set of observations in cells and rats that no single study connects, and none was made in a person.

<InDetail title="Step by step: the proposed chain and what each step rests on">

<Mechanism />

### Step 1: the missing receptor

A drug normally starts by binding one specific target, such as a receptor or an enzyme. That binding is what makes its effects predictable and dose-dependent. For BPC-157 no target is established.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> In 2026 the laboratory in Xi'an, China, that also ran the animal toxicology reported that, in cultured human endothelial cells (the cells that line blood vessels), <Claim level="in-vitro">BPC-157 binds the protein FBXO22 and so protects the transcription factor BACH1 from breakdown, increasing cell growth and tube formation</Claim>.<Cite id="zhang-2026-fbxo22" /> It is the first specific partner proposed, from one laboratory, and not yet repeated.

There is also a timing puzzle. In rats and dogs, intact BPC-157 leaves the blood within minutes, cut into fragments and then amino acids,<Cite id="he-2022-pharmacokinetics" /> yet animal studies report effects that build up over days. Why is unknown.

### Steps 2 to 4: signals and cell behaviour

Most signalling data come from one laboratory at Chang Gung University in Taiwan, which has no link to Zagreb:

- In human endothelial cells, <Claim level="in-vitro">BPC-157 raised levels of VEGFR2, the main receptor for the vessel-growth signal VEGF, and activated the VEGFR2–Akt–eNOS pathway</Claim>.<Cite id="hsieh-2017-vegfr2" />
- In isolated rat aorta, <Claim level="in-vitro">it relaxed the vessel through nitric oxide made by the lining cells; blocking nitric oxide synthesis with L-NAME inhibited the effect</Claim>.<Cite id="hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos" /> In 2026 a group in Ankara, Turkey, tested this in human tissue: in rings of chest artery left over from heart-bypass surgery in 12 men, <Claim level="in-vitro">BPC-157 relaxed the artery mostly through the vessel lining and nitric oxide, and L-NAME weakened the effect</Claim>. The authors note that their concentrations were far higher than the blood levels measured in rats.<Cite id="yildirim-2026-mammary-artery" />
- In rat tendon fibroblasts, <Claim level="in-vitro">it increased activation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and paxillin, proteins that let a cell grip its surroundings and crawl, and the cells migrated faster and survived oxidative stress better</Claim>. They did not divide faster.<Cite id="chang-2011-tendon-fibroblasts" />
- In the same cells, <Claim level="in-vitro">it raised the number of growth hormone receptors, so added growth hormone made the cells multiply more</Claim>.<Cite id="chang-2014-ghr" />

The developer's laboratory reported that <Claim level="in-vitro">in a human gut-cell line PL 14736 switched on EGR-1, a gene cells turn on within minutes of injury, faster than PDGF-BB, the growth factor in the wound gel becaplermin</Claim>.<Cite id="tkalcevic-2007-egr1" />

The laboratories do not always agree. The Taiwanese group saw more vessel-like tubes in culture; the Zagreb group reported "no direct angiogenic effect of BPC 157 on cell cultures".<Cite id="hsieh-2017-vegfr2,brcic-2009-angiogenesis" /> The nitric-oxide link is also untidy. The Taiwanese and Turkish laboratories found effects that depend on nitric oxide, although part of the artery relaxation remained when it was blocked.<Cite id="hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos,yildirim-2026-mammary-artery" /> In the Zagreb group's own rat experiments, by contrast, the protective effects largely persisted when nitric oxide synthesis was blocked.<Cite id="sikiric-1997-nitric-oxide,klicek-2008-fistula" /> These findings do not yet fit one causal story.

### Steps 5 and 6: tissue and healing in animals

<Claim level="animal">Injured rat muscle and tendon showed more blood vessels and stronger VEGF staining</Claim>, <Claim level="animal">blood flow recovered faster in rats with a blocked leg artery</Claim>, and <Claim level="animal">new tissue around sponges implanted in rats, and wounds in diabetic mice, organised their collagen earlier</Claim>.<Cite id="brcic-2009-angiogenesis,hsieh-2017-vegfr2,tkalcevic-2007-egr1" /> The Zagreb reports of faster functional healing are discussed under the evidence below.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Three weak links: what the chain does not show">

The chain has three weak joints. No study measured the early steps and the healing outcome in the same animals, so the link between them is inferred. Nobody knows what concentration reaches a human tendon, so the cell concentrations cannot be translated. And several of the Zagreb group's animal studies report the same benefit at a low and a high dose a thousand-fold apart.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,cerovecki-2010-ligament" /> A real drug effect usually grows with dose up to a ceiling, so a flat response across that range weakens the causal inference.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Cell and animal studies, one by one">

### Cells and isolated tissue: mostly one independent laboratory

Four of the laboratory studies in the table come from one group at Chang Gung University: independent of Zagreb, but not of each other.<Cite id="chang-2011-tendon-fibroblasts,chang-2014-ghr,hsieh-2017-vegfr2,hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos" /> They show that the molecule can change a pathway in a dish, not that it helps a damaged tendon in a person.

Other groups have since reported two of the same effects. The Xi'an laboratory saw more vessel-like tubes in cultured human endothelial cells.<Cite id="zhang-2026-fbxo22,hsieh-2017-vegfr2" /> And in 2026 an Ankara team put rings of chest artery, left over from bypass surgery in 12 men, in a lab bath. BPC-157 relaxed the rings more as its concentration rose, mostly through the vessel lining and nitric oxide, as in the Taiwanese rat aorta.<Cite id="yildirim-2026-mammary-artery,hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos" /> It is the first such test in pieces of human artery, but its limits are clear. The concentrations were far higher than blood levels measured in rats, the men had advanced heart disease, and no rings were given only the solvent over the same time. It shows an effect on artery tone in a dish, not on blood pressure or healing in a person.<Cite id="yildirim-2026-mammary-artery" />

### The originating group's animal studies

Most of the literature is animal work by Predrag Sikirić, Sven Seiwerth and colleagues in Zagreb: by our count, about 140 primary experimental papers.<Cite id="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" /> The history page [how one laboratory built the BPC-157 literature](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/) explains the count and why it matters.

Two studies show the typical design. In 2003 the group cut the Achilles tendon in rats and gave daily intraperitoneal BPC-157 at <Q>10 µg/kg</Q>, <Q>10 ng/kg</Q> or <Q>10 pg/kg</Q>, or saline, starting 30 minutes after surgery. <Claim level="animal">Treated tendons were stronger and stiffer, the rats walked better, and the defect closed over 14 days</Claim>.<Cite id="staresinic-2003-achilles" /> In 2008 the group created colon-to-skin fistulas in rats, and <Claim level="animal">BPC-157 by injection or in drinking water closed them</Claim>.<Cite id="klicek-2008-fistula" /> Similar results were reported for ligament, muscle, nerve and gut injuries.<Cite id="cerovecki-2010-ligament,brcic-2009-angiogenesis,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

The FDA's 2026 review looked closely at the group's gut, liver and fistula studies, the ones relevant to ulcerative colitis, and made three criticisms.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> Low and high doses worked equally well, so no dose–response relationship was shown. Treatment started before, with or soon after the injury, so it is unknown whether BPC-157 helps once damage is established, which is when a person would use it. And without a known target, biological plausibility is hard to judge. The tendon and ligament studies above also started treatment 30 minutes after surgery, and the ligament study reported the same benefit at both of its doses.<Cite id="staresinic-2003-achilles,cerovecki-2010-ligament" /> Their abstracts also do not say whether outcome assessors were blinded.

### Independent animal studies

Groups outside Zagreb have tested BPC-157 in their own rodent models.<Cite id="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" /> As early as 1995, an industry laboratory at Parke-Davis in the US found that <Claim level="animal">the peptide (under the name BPC-15) reduced chemically induced colon damage in rats in a dose-dependent way</Claim>, one of the few reports with a dose–response.<Cite id="veljaca-1995-colitis" /> As in many Zagreb studies, treatment started at or before the moment of injury: here, one hour before.

A 2025 study from Ankara, with one author in common with the artery study, used a similar design. It used 24 rats in four groups of six, and gave BPC-157 at the start of a 45-minute block of blood flow to the legs. <Claim level="animal">Two hours after blood flow returned, the liver, kidneys and lungs of the treated rats showed less damage and more antioxidant activity than those of rats with the same injury and no treatment</Claim>.<Cite id="demirtas-2025-ischaemia-reperfusion" /> Treatment at the moment of injury and a two-hour follow-up say nothing about recovery from an established injury.

The first independent test of the flagship tendon claim came only in 2026. A Turkish team cut and then surgically repaired the Achilles tendons of 32 rats and gave BPC-157, TB-500, both, or nothing for four weeks. (The Zagreb studies left the cut tendon unrepaired, so this is a related model, not an exact repeat.) <Claim level="animal">BPC-157 gave higher load to failure and better total tissue scores than control, but these differences were not statistically significant</Claim>; TB-500's were, and the combination added nothing. <Claim level="animal">One collagen measure, the staining of type I collagen, was significantly higher with BPC-157</Claim>.<Cite id="bicer-2026-achilles" /> The assessors did not know which treatment each tendon had received, a safeguard the abstracts of the Zagreb tendon and ligament studies cited here do not report. But each group's eight tendons were split, four for strength testing and four for tissue analysis, so the study could miss a modest effect, and a single positive result among many tissue measures is weak evidence.<Cite id="bicer-2026-achilles" /> Its result fits a benefit smaller than the "full recovery" the Zagreb studies describe, or none.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Toxicology, and how fast the body breaks it down">

The only formal toxicology comes from the Fourth Military Medical University in Xi'an, China, which was developing BPC-157 for wounds, severe trauma and burns.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety,he-2022-pharmacokinetics" /> <Claim level="animal">Across mice, rats, rabbits and dogs, single and 28-day repeated intramuscular doses caused no serious toxicity; genotoxicity tests were negative, and a rat embryo–fetal study found no malformations</Claim>.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety" /> The FDA's re-analysis of the same data flagged possible safety signals (see the risk profile below).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

The same laboratory studied 366 rats and 6 beagle dogs. Intact BPC-157 had a half-life of <Q>15.2 min</Q> in rats and <Q>5.3 min</Q> in dogs after intravenous dosing. Only part of an intramuscular dose reached the blood, and the peptide was degraded to fragments and then amino acids.<Cite id="he-2022-pharmacokinetics" /> The FDA found no animal or human data on how the body handles BPC-157 after subcutaneous, oral or nasal use. The only human blood measurements, after an enema, mostly found no peptide.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Studies in people, and the trials still running">

### Human evidence

**The PL 14736 programme.** PLIVA developed the peptide as an enema for inflammatory bowel disease. According to the FDA's summary of conference abstracts, a placebo-controlled phase 1 study randomised 32 healthy men. Headache and flatulence were the commonest complaints, at similar rates with peptide and placebo, and the peptide was mostly undetectable in blood.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> In a randomised, double-blind phase 2 trial in 53 people with mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis, <Claim level="human-controlled">a disease-activity score fell by 3.2 points with the peptide and 1.6 with placebo, and the confidence interval for the difference included zero</Claim>.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> That trial appeared only as a 2005 conference abstract, which the FDA judged "inadequate to support the efficacy and safety" of BPC-157. No full report or later trial has been published since.

**Four small uncontrolled reports.** Three of them share one lead author, took place at private clinics in Florida, and used products from US compounding pharmacies.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

- In a retrospective chart review of knee-joint injections (some combined with thymosin beta-4), <Claim level="human-limited">14 of 16 people reached by phone months later said their pain had improved</Claim>.<Cite id="lee-2021-knee" />
- Twelve women with interstitial cystitis that had not responded to pentosan polysulfate, an approved treatment, had injections into the bladder wall; <Claim level="human-limited">10 rated their symptoms as fully resolved, and no adverse events were reported</Claim>.<Cite id="lee-2024-interstitial-cystitis" />
- In two adults given intravenous infusions on consecutive days, <Claim level="human-limited">blood tests and vital signs did not change</Claim>.<Cite id="lee-2025-intravenous" />

The fourth report came from a group of US dentists in 2026. Three patients had numbness in the lip and chin after injury to a nerve in the lower jaw. Each had weekly injections into the gum, for four weeks, of a mixture of five drugs: BPC-157, TB-500, a third experimental peptide (ARA-290), the steroid dexamethasone and a local anaesthetic. <Claim level="human-limited">Feeling largely returned, and no adverse events were reported</Claim>. The authors themselves say that milder injuries of this nerve often recover on their own, and that the steroid and the anaesthetic given at the same time could also explain the recovery. With five drugs at once, no single one can be credited.<Cite id="froum-2026-nerve-cases" /> One of the six authors lists the American Academy of Anti-Aging Medicine (A4M) as their affiliation. The authors declared no funding and no commercial or financial relationships that could be a conflict of interest.<Cite id="froum-2026-nerve-cases" />

A 2026 systematic review, which searched the literature up to 1 June 2026, found the first three studies, 30 people in total, and rated the certainty of evidence as very low.<Cite id="poolton-2026-human-sr" /> The dental report was published after that search.<Cite id="froum-2026-nerve-cases" />

Uncontrolled reports cannot show benefit, for structural reasons. Knee and bladder pain come and go on their own, and many nerve injuries slowly heal by themselves. People seek treatment when symptoms are worst, so many improve afterwards whatever is done (regression to the mean). An injection from a confident clinician produces a real placebo response, and asking people months later adds recall bias. Only a comparison group given a dummy treatment separates these effects from a drug effect.

### Registered trials

As of 20 October 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov lists four studies: a 2015 phase 1 study of oral tablets in Mexico, status unknown, with no results;<Cite id="ctgov-nct02637284" /> a randomised, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial of subcutaneous BPC-157 for acute hamstring strain in 120 people in China, recruiting, with primary completion expected in February 2027;<Cite id="ctgov-nct07437547" /> a small randomised pilot after rotator-cuff surgery at a US university, not yet recruiting, whose record contradicts itself on the comparison group (saline injections in one place, no treatment in another) and on size (20 or 30 participants);<Cite id="ctgov-nct07803250" /> and a completed single-arm study of an oral consumer product, run by its maker, with no results posted.<Cite id="ctgov-nct07752381" />

<Callout type="uncertain" title="What would change this page">
The hamstring trial is the first trial of BPC-157 in an injury that is randomised, blinded and placebo-controlled and has started recruiting. It uses an MRI measure of the injury as one of its two main outcomes and has a data monitoring committee.<Cite id="ctgov-nct07437547" /> If it is published in full, whatever the result, it will tell us more than all existing human data. We will update the grades when it reports.
</Callout>

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Where the evidence is genuinely promising, and what the gap costs">

Two findings from the Taiwanese cell laboratory have since been reported by other groups, one of them in human artery tissue.<Cite id="zhang-2026-fbxo22,yildirim-2026-mammary-artery" /> Animal toxicology so far shows no genotoxicity and no acute toxicity at high doses.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety" /> Independent reviewers in sports medicine describe the preclinical record as promising while stressing how little human data exist.<Cite id="vasireddi-2025-systematic-review,mcguire-2025-review" /> Tendon and ligament injuries heal slowly and few medicines speed that healing, so a properly tested healing agent would be valuable.

The gap has a real cost. In public records the developer's programme never went beyond a phase 2 trial that was not fully published, and two decades passed without a definitive trial. Into that gap came a grey market of unmonitored injections of products of unknown content.

<InDetail title="What has been reported in people">

The published human studies reported no serious adverse events, but the FDA notes they were short, small and said little about how safety was monitored.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> The 2026 dental report, published after the FDA review, reported no adverse events.<Cite id="froum-2026-nerve-cases" /> The FDA's adverse-event database held three reports up to 4 December 2025, all after injections: nine days of redness and swelling at the injection site (in someone also injecting thymosin); shortness of breath leading to an emergency visit; and diffuse darkening of the skin and gums after a week of a combined BPC-157 and TB-500 product labelled "for research purposes only". US compounding pharmacies do not have to report adverse events to the FDA, so the small number of reports says little about how often problems occur.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="What the animal toxicology flags">

The Chinese toxicology paper called BPC-157 well tolerated. The FDA's re-analysis of the same data described "clinically relevant safety signals":<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

- **Clotting.** A shortened clotting time (aPTT) in male rats and a prolonged one in male dogs. In humans a shortened aPTT is a known risk factor for clots, and a prolonged one can be a risk factor for bleeding.
- **Liver and blood fats.** Rises in a liver enzyme (ALT), glucose and triglycerides in female rats after treatment stopped, and raised triglycerides in female dogs.
- **Kidney marker.** Lower creatinine in dogs.

Each came from a single 28-day study, and longer studies would be needed to tell whether the signals are real.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Product quality and immune reactions">

The most immediate risk may be the product itself. The certificates of analysis the FDA reviewed reported purity, but not specific impurities, clumps of peptide, or tests for bacterial endotoxin or microbial contamination. The FDA calls endotoxin testing critical for anything injected.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> Peptides can clump together during storage, and synthesis leaves near-copies of the chain; both present the repeated, unfamiliar patterns the immune system is built to notice. The FDA concluded that, injected or sprayed into the nose, BPC-157 "may pose a significant risk for immunogenicity", possibly made worse by aggregation and impurities. Neither the nominators nor the FDA found a study that formally tested this. Consequences of an immune response range from antibodies that cause no symptoms to severe, even life-threatening, reactions.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Why an effect on cancer is theorised">

The reasoning combines well-established cancer biology with findings about BPC-157 from cells and rats:

1. **Tumours need blood vessels.** Beyond a very small size a tumour can grow only by recruiting new vessels, which it does by releasing signals, chiefly VEGF. Inducing angiogenesis is a recognised hallmark of cancer.<Cite id="hanahan-2011-hallmarks" /> VEGF acts mainly through VEGFR2, and medicines that block this pathway are used to slow some cancers.<Cite id="carmeliet-2011-angiogenesis" />
2. **BPC-157 pushes on that pathway.** <Claim level="in-vitro">It increased VEGFR2 levels and signalling in human endothelial cells</Claim> and <Claim level="animal">increased vessel growth in ischaemic rat muscle</Claim>.<Cite id="hsieh-2017-vegfr2" />
3. **Cancer cells crawl with repair machinery.** FAK and paxillin let a fibroblast crawl into a wound. In many cancers FAK is over-active and helps tumour cells survive, migrate and invade; it is a target of experimental cancer drugs.<Cite id="sulzmaier-2014-fak-cancer" /> <Claim level="in-vitro">BPC-157 increased FAK and paxillin activation in tendon fibroblasts</Claim>.<Cite id="chang-2011-tendon-fibroblasts" />
4. **More growth hormone receptors amplify growth signals.** <Claim level="in-vitro">BPC-157 raised growth hormone receptor numbers in tendon cells</Claim>;<Cite id="chang-2014-ghr" /> whether it does so in other cells, including tumour cells, is untested.

So the worry is not that BPC-157 creates cancer: it was not genotoxic in standard tests.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety" /> The worry is that something which helps vessels sprout and cells migrate could help an existing, perhaps undiagnosed, tumour grow or spread. Genotoxicity tests detect the DNA damage that starts a cancer; they cannot detect promotion of one that already exists. That needs long-term carcinogenicity studies and human follow-up. No carcinogenicity studies of BPC-157 in the regulatory sense have been published.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

There are honest counter-arguments. The originating group argues that BPC-157 "controls" angiogenesis rather than simply driving it, and reports anti-tumour effects in some models.<Cite id="sikiric-2025-comment" /> An independent review found only one cancer-related study, a mouse model of cancer weight loss in which tumour volume did not differ significantly.<Cite id="jozwiak-2025-review" /> We found no published study that shows tumour promotion. The risk is plausible but untested.

A precedent shows why the question matters. Becaplermin (Regranex), a growth-factor gel for diabetic foot ulcers authorised in the US (its EU authorisation ended in 2012), "promotes cellular proliferation and angiogenesis".<Cite id="fda-regranex-label-2018,ema-medicines-json" /> Its 2011 label carried a boxed warning about more cancer deaths in people using three or more tubes. The warning was removed in November 2018, when the label reported the signal in one of three post-marketing studies, but the label still warns about cancers and forbids use over a known tumour.<Cite id="fda-regranex-label-2011,fda-regranex-label-2018" /> Such signals only emerge, and are only resolved, with large human datasets. BPC-157 has none.

</InDetail>

<Section id="harm-reduction">

<Lead question="How can someone lower the risk?">Talk to a doctor first, treat any vial as unchecked, and know when to stop.</Lead>

### Talk to a doctor first

A doctor can check for the conditions where the theoretical risks matter most: cancer, pregnancy and clotting problems. A doctor can also make sure the injury itself is properly diagnosed. A torn tendon or ligament may need a scan or surgery within weeks. Treating yourself can delay care that works. See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

<Callout type="stop" title="Stop and get help if" id="stop-now">
- the injection site becomes hot, red, swollen or painful, or you get a fever (infection or contamination);
- you get hives, swelling of the face or throat, or difficulty breathing (allergic reaction);
- your skin or gums darken, as in one reported case;
- you notice a new lump, unexplained bleeding or bruising, or pain and swelling in one leg.
</Callout>

<InDetail title="What you cannot know about the product">

A vial labelled BPC-157 may hold a different salt, or more or less peptide than the label says. It may also hold near-copies of the chain, solvents or bacterial toxins. No regulator has checked it (see [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/)). If you inject anything, follow [sterile technique](/en/safety/sterile-technique/). A contaminated product or needle can cause an infection.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Checks that make sense, and why">

Discuss these with a doctor, who decides what is appropriate:

<Checklist>

- **Cancer screening due for your age, and any unexplained lump, bleeding or weight loss checked first**, because of the theoretical effect on blood-vessel growth.
- **Liver enzymes and blood fats**, because both rose in female rats, and blood fats also rose in female dogs.
- **Clotting tests, especially if you take a blood thinner or have had a clot**, because clotting times changed in rats and dogs.
- **Kidney tests, telling the doctor you use BPC-157**, because creatinine, the standard kidney marker, fell in dogs. If it does the same in people, a low value could make the kidneys look healthier than they are.

</Checklist>

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Interactions, and who should not use it at all">

### Plausible interactions (none has been studied)

- **Blood thinners and antiplatelet medicines**, because of the clotting changes in animals.
- **Medicines that lower blood pressure or act through nitric oxide** (such as nitrates or sildenafil), because BPC-157 relaxed isolated rat and human arteries through nitric oxide.<Cite id="hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos,yildirim-2026-mammary-artery" />
- **Cancer medicines that block blood-vessel growth**, because BPC-157 pushes the same pathway the other way.
- **Combination products with other peptides**, such as TB-500 blends, which add a second unknown, make any reaction impossible to attribute, and gave no extra benefit in rats.<Cite id="bicer-2026-achilles,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

### Who should not use it at all

- **Anyone pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive**: there are no human data, only one rat embryo–fetal study, and new vessel growth is central to pregnancy.
- **Anyone with cancer, a history of cancer or an undiagnosed lump**, because of the angiogenesis and FAK mechanisms above.
- **Children and teenagers**, whose tissues are still growing and for whom there are no data.
- **Anyone with a clotting disorder or on anticoagulants**, because of the animal clotting signals.
- **Athletes subject to anti-doping rules**, because it is prohibited at all times.
