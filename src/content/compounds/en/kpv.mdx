KPV is promoted online for inflammatory bowel disease, wounds, skin problems, mast-cell and histamine problems, and general "gut health", as an injection, by mouth, on the skin and as a nasal spray, and in blends with other peptides such as [BPC-157](/en/compounds/bpc-157/) and [TB-500](/en/compounds/tb-500/).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> A 2026 review lists it among the peptides marketed to gym-goers.<Cite id="coutinho-2026-doping-review" /> None of this promotion rests on human results, because there are none (see the evidence section). No medicine containing KPV is authorised in the EU, the Netherlands or the US.<Cite id="ema-medicines-json,cbg-geneesmiddeleninformatiebank,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />

<InDetail title="The molecule, and where it comes from">

KPV is a synthetic peptide made of just three amino acids: <Seq>Lys-Pro-Val</Seq> (lysine, proline, valine), with ordinary free ends. Its formula is C16H30N4O4 and its mass is <Q>342.43 Da</Q>.<Cite id="kpv-pubchem" /> That makes it one of the smallest molecules on this site. A peptide this short is at the fuzzy border between a "peptide drug" and an ordinary piece of digested protein.

### Where it comes from

KPV is the tail of a natural hormone. The body makes a large protein called <Term id="proopiomelanocortin">proopiomelanocortin</Term> and cuts it into smaller hormones, one of which is <Term id="alpha-msh">alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone</Term> (alpha-MSH). Alpha-MSH is a 13-amino-acid chain. Its last three amino acids, positions 11, 12 and 13, are lysine, proline and valine. That is KPV.<Cite id="cody-1984-c-terminal-tripeptide,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />

Alpha-MSH does two very different jobs. One part of it controls skin and hair colour. A different part calms inflammation.<Cite id="brzoska-2008-review" /> Researchers found that the small KPV tail keeps most of the anti-inflammatory action but has none of the colour-changing action.<Cite id="brzoska-2010-terminal-signal,luger-2007-review" /> That is the whole reason KPV was studied: it was an attempt to keep the useful half of alpha-MSH and drop the half that darkens the skin. The part of a molecule responsible for a given action is called its <Term id="pharmacophore">pharmacophore</Term>, and alpha-MSH has separate ones for colour and for inflammation.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Natural does not mean harmless, and the naming problem">

### Natural does not mean harmless

Because KPV is a fragment of a human hormone, it is often described as "natural". That word is doing no work. The KPV in a vial was not taken from the body; it was built in a factory by solid-phase peptide synthesis, the same chemistry used for other synthetic peptides.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> A manufactured copy can carry shortened chains, near-copies, leftover solvents and bacterial contamination, and it is injected or sprayed in amounts and ways the body never meets naturally. The later risk section explains why "small and natural" says nothing about whether an injected product is safe.

### Naming and salt forms

"KPV" is a common name, not an official one. It is not a United States Adopted Name, and it follows none of the standards that identify a drug substance exactly (INN, IUPAC, USAN).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> The FDA points out that this matters for safety: without a standard name, a patient may be dosed with a different substance than the prescriber meant. Commercial material is sold as a free base or as an acetate salt, and the FDA treats these as two different substances even though most public references give the same identifier number for both.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,kpv-pubchem" />

KPV has a clearer proposed mechanism than many experimental peptides, and parts of it are reported by several independent groups. But the chain has never been shown in a person, and the very first link, how KPV first acts on a cell, is still missing.

<InDetail title="Step by step: the proposed chain and what each step rests on">

<Mechanism />

### The receptor that is not there

A drug usually starts by binding one specific target. KPV comes from alpha-MSH, so the obvious guess is that it uses alpha-MSH's own targets, the melanocortin receptors. <Claim level="in-vitro">In binding experiments KPV did not attach to the melanocortin-1 receptor and did not raise the internal messenger cAMP, unlike whole alpha-MSH</Claim>.<Cite id="mandrika-2001-nfkb-no" /> <Claim level="animal">In mice, KPV still reduced inflammation when the melanocortin-1 receptor was broken or blocked</Claim>.<Cite id="getting-2003-dissection,kannengiesser-2008-colitis" /> The FDA's 2026 review concluded that melanocortin receptors are unlikely to be KPV's target and that its true molecular target is unknown.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> So KPV's effects, where they are seen, happen through a route that has not been identified.

### A back door into the cell

One laboratory offered an answer to how KPV reaches the inside of a cell. <Claim level="in-vitro">KPV is carried into gut and immune cells by PepT1, a transporter that normally ferries small two- and three-amino-acid peptides from digested food</Claim>.<Cite id="dalmasso-2008-pept1" /> This is a tidy fit for a gut disease: <Term id="pept1">PepT1</Term> is scarce in a healthy colon but rises where the colon is inflamed, so it could carry KPV straight to the inflamed tissue. <Claim level="animal">In mice bred without PepT1, KPV no longer reduced colitis-driven bowel tumours, though a non-significant trend to a lower tumour burden remained</Claim>. The authors conclude that KPV acts at least in part through PepT1, and that another transporter may carry some of it.<Cite id="dalmasso-2008-pept1,viennois-2016-cac" /> Whether colitis itself still responds to KPV in those mice was not reported.

### Turning down the inflammation switch

Inside the cell, the recurring finding is that KPV quietens <Term id="nf-kb">NF-κB</Term>, a switch that turns on inflammation genes. <Claim level="in-vitro">KPV lowered NF-κB activity in gut cells, T cells, macrophages and airway cells, and the cells released fewer inflammatory messengers: less interleukin-8 and eotaxin from airway cells, less interleukin-1β from skin cells, less nitric oxide from macrophages, and less cytokine output from gut and T cells</Claim>.<Cite id="dalmasso-2008-pept1,mandrika-2001-nfkb-no,land-2012-bronchial,sung-2025-keratinocyte" /> One study pinned down a step: <Claim level="in-vitro">KPV blocked the carrier that normally pulls NF-κB's p65 part into the cell nucleus, so the switch could not reach the genes it controls</Claim>.<Cite id="land-2012-bronchial" /> KPV also appears to blunt the effects of interleukin-1β directly.<Cite id="getting-2003-dissection" />

### From cells to animals

<Claim level="animal">In mice with chemically induced colitis, KPV-treated gut showed less immune-cell infiltration, measured as lower myeloperoxidase, and less damage under the microscope, and the animals recovered and regained weight sooner</Claim>.<Cite id="kannengiesser-2008-colitis,dalmasso-2008-pept1" /> <Claim level="animal">Rabbit corneas scraped bare healed faster with KPV eye drops, an effect held back when nitric oxide production was blocked</Claim>.<Cite id="bonfiglio-2006-cornea" /> These whole-animal results are consistent with the cell findings, but they are models: a chemical colitis in a mouse is not ulcerative colitis in a person, and a scraped rabbit cornea is not a chronic human wound.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The weak joints in the chain">

Three gaps matter. First, no study has followed KPV from the missing receptor step through to healing in the same system, so the chain is stitched together from separate experiments. Second, the strongest animal evidence is for the gut, and comes mostly from one laboratory; a delivery study from that group found that plain KPV worked only at about <Q>12,000×</Q> higher concentration than KPV packed into targeted particles, which suggests free KPV reaches its target poorly.<Cite id="laroui-2010-nanoparticles" /> Third, and most important, none of this has been measured in a human.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Cell and animal studies, one by one">

### Cells: a reproducible anti-inflammatory signal

The cell evidence is KPV's strongest suit. Over more than two decades, unrelated groups have reported the same core finding: KPV lowers NF-κB activity and inflammatory-messenger output without acting through melanocortin receptors.<Cite id="mandrika-2001-nfkb-no,getting-2003-dissection,land-2012-bronchial,dalmasso-2008-pept1" /> That several independent laboratories see the same thing is genuinely reassuring about the basic biology. Cell studies answer "can this molecule change this pathway in a dish?" They cannot answer "does it help a person?", because dosing, delivery, breakdown and the whole immune system are missing.

### Animals: promising, but shallow and clustered

Two lines of animal work stand out. The older line, from the Lipton group and others, showed that the alpha-MSH tail reduces acute inflammation in mice: ear swelling from a skin-sensitising chemical, and inflammatory cells drawn into the abdomen.<Cite id="hiltz-1989-cooh-fragment,getting-2003-dissection" /> The FDA counted the 1989 ear-swelling study as a study of an N-acetylated form of KPV rather than the free peptide, and set it aside.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> The newer and stronger line is gut disease. <Claim level="animal">KPV, especially when delivered in targeted nanoparticles or gels, reduced colitis in mice and reduced colitis-associated bowel tumours in one model</Claim>.<Cite id="dalmasso-2008-pept1,laroui-2010-nanoparticles,viennois-2016-cac,xiao-2017-nanoparticles" /> The colitis-cancer result is worth noting precisely because it points the opposite way to a common fear about "growth" peptides: in that model KPV lowered tumour numbers rather than raising them.<Cite id="viennois-2016-cac" /> In the same paper, mice with a genetic bowel-tumour disease and little gut inflammation got no fewer tumours with KPV, so the effect appears to work through inflammation rather than on tumours directly.<Cite id="viennois-2016-cac" />

The weakness is who produced it. Most of the gut evidence comes from a single laboratory and its partners. One independent group (in Münster) did reproduce the colitis benefit in its own mouse models, which strengthens it.<Cite id="kannengiesser-2008-colitis" /> But no disease result has been repeated across many groups to the standard a medicine needs, and every one of these results is in rodents.

### Skin and wounds

<Claim level="animal">KPV sped corneal healing in rabbits</Claim>, and <Claim level="in-vitro">it protected human skin cells from pollution-induced damage in a dish</Claim>.<Cite id="bonfiglio-2006-cornea,sung-2025-keratinocyte" /> A practical problem sits under the skin claims: <Claim level="in-vitro">plain KPV barely crosses human skin, and needed an electric current or micro-needles to get through in a laboratory test</Claim>.<Cite id="pawar-2017-iontophoresis" /> The FDA noted that this cuts both ways: poor skin penetration limits any whole-body toxicity from a cream, but it may also stop a cream from reaching the living skin below the surface where it would need to act.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Studies in people: there are none">

This is the central fact. The FDA searched PubMed, Embase, trial registries, its own adverse-event database and more in 2026, and found no study of KPV given to people, by any route, for any condition, and no case reports.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> As of 20 October 2026 we found no registered clinical trial of KPV on ClinicalTrials.gov or the EU trials register either. Everything known about KPV is from cells and animals. There is no human dose, no human pharmacokinetic data, and no systematic human safety information of any kind.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Where the evidence is promising, and where it is not">

Reviewers have argued, reasonably, that KPV is a fair candidate for proper clinical testing in inflammatory bowel disease and in wounds.<Cite id="brzoska-2008-review,bohm-2019-wound-review,gravina-2023-melanocortin-ibd" /> The honest counterpoint is that this has been true for over fifteen years and no company or group has run a published human trial.

</InDetail>

</Section>

<InDetail title="All nine open questions, with their sources">

What is specifically not known about KPV, as of 20 October 2026:

- **Whether it does anything in people.** No human study of any kind has been published.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />
- **How it works at the first step.** No receptor or primary molecular target is established; the PepT1 route explains uptake but not what KPV binds once inside.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,dalmasso-2008-pept1" />
- **What happens to it in a human body.** There are no human pharmacokinetic data for any route. A tripeptide is also a natural food-digestion product, so an injected dose may be broken down or used as ordinary amino acids quickly, but this has not been measured in people.
- **Whether a cream, capsule, injection or nasal spray delivers anything useful.** Plain KPV barely crosses skin in the laboratory, and no route has been tested in a person.<Cite id="pawar-2017-iontophoresis,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />
- **Any formal toxicology.** The FDA found no acute, repeat-dose, genotoxicity, reproductive or carcinogenicity studies of KPV.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />
- **Immune reactions.** Immunogenicity has never been studied; the FDA could not rule it out.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />
- **The effect of long-term immune damping.** KPV's whole point is to quieten inflammation; what chronic use would do to normal defence against infection, or to immune surveillance, is untested.
- **Interactions** with any other medicine.
- **Whether the free base and the acetate salt behave the same**, and what is actually in products sold as KPV. The nomination held no certificate of analysis for the free base, and the ones the FDA found elsewhere mostly report purity only; the acetate certificate reported identity, assay and related substances, but nothing on clumping (aggregation) or germs.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Status in each country, and in sport">

<RegulatoryTable />

All statuses below were checked on 20 October 2026.

### Sport (WADA)

KPV is **not named** on the World Anti-Doping Agency's Prohibited List. It is still banned. Section S0 prohibits, at all times, "any pharmacological substance which is not addressed by any of the subsequent sections of the List and with no current approval by any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use".<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en" /> KPV has no such approval anywhere, so it falls under S0 by definition. The 2027 List, in force from 1 January 2027, makes this plainer still by adding "peptides" to the S0 examples.<Cite id="wada-2027-list-en" /> On that wording, a US compounding listing would not count as an approval and would not change the anti-doping status.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The European Union, the Netherlands, Germany, Belgium and the United Kingdom">

What we checked for this page: EMA's medicines data, which covers EU-level authorisations including refused and withdrawn ones; the Dutch medicines database; and the FDA's own review, which searched the European and Japanese pharmacopoeias and EMA's records. None of them contains KPV.<Cite id="ema-medicines-json,cbg-geneesmiddeleninformatiebank,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> We did not search the national registers of Germany, Belgium or the United Kingdom, so for those three countries the entries below give the law that applies, not the result of a register search. The FDA's KPV review, unlike its reviews of some other peptides, does not itself say that no country has approved KPV. The reason we still answer "approved nowhere" is the evidence section: no study of KPV in people has ever been published, and no regulator grants a marketing authorisation without clinical data.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />

### European Union

No medicine containing KPV has an EU marketing authorisation, and EMA records, which also list refused and withdrawn applications, contain no KPV entry. There is no European Pharmacopoeia monograph for it.<Cite id="ema-medicines-search,ema-medicines-json,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />

### Netherlands

The CBG medicines database lists no product containing KPV.<Cite id="cbg-geneesmiddeleninformatiebank" /> Under the Geneesmiddelenwet, a substance is a medicine if it is presented as treating or preventing disease, or is meant to change body functions through a pharmacological effect. Finished medicinal products may be placed on the market only with a national licence or an EU authorisation (Arzneimittelgesetz § 21). No KPV product holds an EU authorisation; we found no national licence, but did not search the German register.<Cite id="de-amg-21,ema-medicines-json" />

<InDetail title="The United States, and the July 2026 vote point by point">

KPV is not FDA-approved. It has no US Pharmacopeia monograph and is not part of any approved drug, so under federal law a pharmacy may compound with it only if it is placed on the 503A bulk drug substances list through rulemaking.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> The FDA had earlier flagged KPV among substances that may present significant safety risks, because it found no human exposure data and could not judge whether it would cause harm.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" />

The one nomination asking for KPV was later withdrawn. The FDA evaluated both the free base and the acetate salt anyway, on its own initiative, because it was unclear which of the two the nominator had meant.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> Its scientists assessed them for wound healing and inflammatory conditions and concluded that they are not well characterised, that there is no information on their use in humans, and that they should not be added to the list.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" />

What happened next is the single most misreported part of this story:

- On 23 July 2026 the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) voted to recommend KPV for the 503A bulk drug substances list.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-questions" />
- Over two days the committee considered seven peptides.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" /> As reported from the meeting, it recommended six: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax and epitalon. Emideltide (DSIP) was not recommended.
- The vote is **advisory and non-binding**, and it went against the FDA staff's own recommendation, which was not to list KPV.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" />
- Legal compounding remains prohibited until the FDA formally adopts the recommendation and completes notice-and-comment rulemaking.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />
- **KPV is not an approved medicine.** A 503A listing, if it ever happens, would only let US pharmacies compound KPV on prescription. It is not a marketing authorisation, says nothing about whether KPV works, and changes nothing in the EU or the Netherlands.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1" />

As reported from the public meeting, the KPV vote was 8 in favour, 6 against, with 1 abstention, the same split reported for BPC-157 and TB-500. The FDA's voting questions in fact put two separate questions for KPV, one for the free base and one for the acetate salt, so there were two votes.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-questions" /> As of 20 October 2026 the FDA had not posted written minutes, and its meeting page was still marked current as of 6 August 2026. The FDA documents confirm the date, the seven peptides, the voting questions and the staff recommendation; we will check the counts, and whether the two KPV votes differed, against the minutes when they appear.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-questions" />

<Lead question="What are the risks?">Its risks in people are unknown. Unchecked products and immune reactions are the main worries.</Lead>

<InDetail title="What has been reported in people">

Nothing, in either direction. Because there is no human study and no case report, there is no record of benefit and no record of harm. The FDA searched its adverse-event reporting system and its food-and-supplement complaint system through 3 December 2025 and found no reports for KPV.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> An absence of reports is not evidence of safety: US compounding pharmacies are not required to report adverse events, and a substance almost no one reports on will show almost no reports.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The product, and immune reactions">

### The product is the first risk

For most people the most immediate danger is not the molecule but the vial. The nomination the FDA reviewed held no certificate of analysis for the free-base form, and the certificates the FDA found elsewhere mostly reported purity and nothing else; the acetate certificate it did have showed no testing for peptide clumping or for germs. It also noted that KPV is not consistently named, so a buyer may receive a different substance than intended.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> An injected or sprayed product that is not sterile can cause infection, from a skin infection at the site to germs reaching the blood.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis,nhs-sepsis" /> Germs growing in the liquid before it is filtered also leave <Term id="endotoxin">endotoxin</Term> behind, and counting germs says nothing about how much endotoxin is there.<Cite id="fda-1993-lyophilization-guide" />

### Why an injected peptide can trigger the immune system

Peptides can clump together into aggregates, and synthesis leaves behind near-copies of the intended chain. The immune system is built to notice repeated, unfamiliar molecular patterns, and both aggregates and near-copies supply them. The FDA concluded that KPV could carry a risk of <Term id="immunogenicity">immunogenicity</Term>, raised further by aggregation and impurities, and found no study that assessed it.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> In the FDA's words, the consequences of setting off an immune response can range from antibodies with no visible effect to life-threatening reactions, and they are often unpredictable in the person given the product.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/) for how grey-market peptides differ from authorised medicines.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Why “small and natural” is not reassurance">

Three points are often used to argue KPV must be safe: it is tiny, it is a piece of a human hormone, and it had no adverse-event reports. None of them holds up. A manufactured peptide is not the fragment your body makes; it comes with impurities and may aggregate. Being small does not stop immune reactions, and peptides as short as two amino acids can clump.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> And "no reports" reflects the fact that no one studies or reports on it, not that it has been checked.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="A risk that follows from how it works, and other gaps">

### A theoretical risk that follows from the mechanism

KPV's intended action is to damp inflammation by turning down NF-κB and cytokine output inside immune cells. The same machinery defends the body against infection and helps the immune system watch for abnormal cells. So, in principle, broadly and chronically quietening it could blunt normal defence. This is a reasoning from mechanism, not an observed harm: it has not been tested in people, and in one mouse model KPV actually reduced inflammation-driven tumours rather than promoting them.<Cite id="viennois-2016-cac" /> The honest summary is that a theoretical immune cost is plausible and completely unstudied.

### Pregnancy, cancer and other gaps

There are no reproductive or developmental studies, no genotoxicity studies and no carcinogenicity studies of KPV at all.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> That does not mean it is dangerous in these situations; it means nobody knows.

</InDetail>

<Section id="harm-reduction">

<Lead question="How can someone lower the risk?">Talk to a doctor first, treat any vial as unchecked, and know when to stop.</Lead>

This section is for people who have decided to use KPV, or already do. It is not a recommendation, and it gives no amounts, schedules or sources.

### Talk to a doctor first, especially if you have a real disease

KPV is promoted for inflammatory bowel disease, Crohn's disease and chronic wounds.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> These are serious conditions, and there are approved treatments for inflammatory disease and for wounds that are given with monitoring.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> Using an unapproved peptide instead of, or on top of, proper care can let a treatable disease get worse unnoticed. A doctor can confirm the diagnosis, start treatment that has been tested, and watch for complications. See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/) for how to raise it without judgement.

<Callout type="stop" title="Stop and get help if" id="stop-now">
- the injection or application site becomes hot, red, swollen or painful, which points to infection or contamination;
- you get a fever or chills, or feel very ill, after an injection;
- you get hives, swelling of the face, lips or throat, or difficulty breathing (an allergic reaction).

If it is severe, call your local emergency number.
</Callout>

<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis,nhs-sepsis,nhs-anaphylaxis,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />

<WarningSigns only="general,injection,allergic" />

<InDetail title="What you cannot know about the product">

### Know what you cannot know about the product

A vial labelled KPV may hold the free base or the acetate salt, more or less peptide than stated, near-copies of the chain, solvents, or bacterial toxins. Nobody checked it before it reached you, and KPV is not even consistently named.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> The page on [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/) explains what this means in practice. If you inject or spray anything, follow [sterile technique](/en/safety/sterile-technique/): injecting carries a risk of infection whatever is in the vial, and that risk you can lower.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis,cdc-2024-safe-injection-practices" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Checks that make sense, and why">

### There is no useful monitoring test

With most medicines you can track a blood marker to judge the dose or catch harm early. For KPV there is nothing validated to measure, because it has never been studied in people. The practical checks are therefore about catching problems, not tuning a dose:

<Checklist>

- **Watch the injection or application site** for heat, redness, swelling or pain, which point to infection or contamination.
- **Watch for allergic reactions**, which are the most mechanistically plausible harm from an injected or sprayed peptide.
- **If you have inflammatory bowel disease, keep your specialist's monitoring going** (symptoms, inflammation markers, scope checks as advised). Do not read any short-term improvement as proof the disease is controlled.
- **Tell any doctor who treats you that you are using it**, so an allergic or infectious reaction is not mistaken for something else.

</Checklist>

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Who should not use it at all, and interactions">

### Who should not use it at all

- **People who are pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive.** There are no human data and no reproductive studies of any kind.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />
- **People with an active infection**, because KPV's whole purpose is to damp inflammation, which is also the body's defence.
- **People relying on it to treat a serious or worsening condition** such as active inflammatory bowel disease or a non-healing wound, instead of seeking care that has been tested.
- **Children and teenagers**, for whom there are no data at all.
- **Athletes subject to anti-doping rules**, because it is prohibited at all times under S0.<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en" />

### Plausible interactions (none has been studied)

- **Medicines that suppress the immune system** (such as those used for inflammatory bowel disease or after a transplant), because KPV also damps immune signalling and the combined effect is unknown.
- **Combination products with other peptides**, such as BPC-157 or TB-500 blends, which add a second unknown substance and make any reaction impossible to attribute.

</InDetail>
