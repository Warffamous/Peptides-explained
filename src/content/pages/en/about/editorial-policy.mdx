<Lead question="What is this page?">The rules we write by. You can use them to check our work.</Lead>

This site exists to inform, not to persuade. Many readers have already made a choice, or already have a product. We do not try to talk anyone out of it. We try to make the choice better informed and the use safer. That is called [harm reduction](/en/safety/).

## How we grade evidence

<Lead question="How do you judge how strong the evidence is?">We rate four things separately. We also label each claim with the kind of study behind it.</Lead>

<Diagram id="claim-levels-explained" caption="The six labels we put on a claim, from least to most support.">

| Label on the page | What it means | What it can tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothesis | Someone proposed it. No experiment has shown it. | An idea worth testing. Nothing more. |
| Cells | Shown in cells or tissue in a lab | That something can happen in a dish |
| Animals | Shown in animal studies | That it can happen in a living animal. People often react differently. |
| Humans, limited | Seen in people, in single cases or small studies without a comparison group | That a question is worth asking in people |
| Human trials | Shown in controlled trials in people | That it works or harms in the people who were studied |
| Established | Shown in large trials and in approved use | What a regulator has accepted, for a specific use |

</Diagram>

Each compound page also has an evidence profile. It answers four questions: has it been tested in people, how much lab and animal research exists, how many separate research groups did that research, and how well the harms in people are known. We never add these up. One number would hide important differences. The full method is on our page about [evidence grading](/en/evidence/evidence-grading/).

<InDetail title="The four scales, step by step">

| Question | The steps, from least to most |
|---|---|
| How far has it been tested in people? | None published → case reports only → phase I (small, about safety) → phase II (dose and early effect) → phase III (large controlled trials) → authorised by a regulator |
| How much lab and animal research exists? | Minimal (fewer than about 10 studies) → moderate (about 10 to 50) → extensive (more than about 50) |
| How many separate groups produced it? | One group dominates (more than about 7 in 10 studies) → a few groups → many groups, with key results repeated |
| How well are the harms in people known? | Uncharacterised (no organised data in people) → partial (short or small studies) → well characterised (large trials plus years of reports after approval) |

We count only studies that have been completed and published. A trial that is registered but has no results does not count yet.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Why we never give one overall score">

Take a compound with more than 50 animal studies, almost all from one laboratory, and no trials in people. A single score of "medium" would describe none of that. The four separate grades say exactly what is known and what is missing. That is why the grades sit side by side and are never added up. See [the Croatian lab](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/) for a worked example.

</InDetail>

## Where our facts come from

<Lead question="Where do your facts come from?">From the study, the regulator or the law itself. Each fact links to it.</Lead>

<Diagram id="source-order" caption="Which sources we use first, and which we never use.">

1. **First choice: the original.** The trial report, the regulator's decision or product label, the law itself.
2. **Then: systematic reviews.** A systematic review collects all studies on one question by a fixed method.
3. **Only for background: other reviews and expert statements.** We say when a fact rests on one.
4. **Never: anyone with something to sell.** No sellers, clinics, coaches, influencers, forums or marketing material.

</Diagram>

Every claim about a study says which species, how many subjects and what kind of study ("in a trial of 1,961 adults…"). Every legal or regulatory status carries the date we checked it. If we cannot check something, we leave it out, or we write that it is not known.

<InDetail title="How we check a source">

- **Studies.** We look each study up in PubMed, the database of the US National Library of Medicine.<Cite id="pubmed-about-nlm" /> We copy the authors, title, journal, year and DOI (the paper's permanent web address) from that record, never from memory. A wrong DOI is one of the worst mistakes we could make.
- **Numbers.** Every number on a page must appear in the source it cites. We report numbers as the source gives them.
- **Regulators and laws.** We open each official page, read it, and record the date. Laws and statuses change, so pages show when we last checked.
- **Links.** We link only to sites on a fixed list. It holds regulators, law databases, PubMed and science journals, universities, anti-doping bodies, patient and harm-reduction organisations, and a few non-profit services, such as the archive our photos come from. No one who sells medicines or peptides can be on that list. Every other link is blocked when the site is built.
- **Animal and lab results.** We never turn an animal result into advice for people. See [the translation gap](/en/evidence/the-translation-gap/).

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Who paid for the research">

Who pays for a study can affect its results. A Cochrane review of 75 papers found that drug and device studies paid for by the maker more often reported favourable results and conclusions than studies paid for by others. By the usual quality checks, the company studies were not worse designed. On one check, blinding, they did better.<Cite id="lundh-2017-industry-sponsorship" /> So we record who paid for each key study and whether it came from the group that first developed the compound. That does not make a study wrong. It tells you whose questions were asked. See [conflicts of interest](/en/evidence/conflicts-of-interest/).

</InDetail>

## What we never write, and what we always write

<Lead question="Is there anything you will not write?">Yes. No doses, no sellers, no promises. And we always say what is not known.</Lead>

<Compare title="We give practical safety facts. We never give doses or name sellers." detail="The rules every page on this site follows.">

| | On this site |
|---|---|
| Doses, schedules, mixing sums or combinations of products? | <Verdict is="no" /> For untested peptides nobody knows a safe or useful amount. For approved medicines, your prescriber decides. |
| Names of sellers, links to shops, or tips on choosing a seller? | <Verdict is="no" /> That could count as advertising, which the law forbids for unlicensed medicines where we publish, and in the UK, Canada and Australia.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-84-85-advertising,eu-directive-2001-83,uk-hmr-2012-reg-279,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tg-act-advertising" /> |
| Stories from users, before-and-after pictures, or promises? | <Verdict is="no" /> A story cannot show what a product does. A fair trial can. |
| Practical safety information for people who already have a product? | <Verdict is="yes" /> What to check, which tests matter, warning signs and when to get help |
| Species, number and type of study for every study claim? | <Verdict is="yes" /> So you can see how far a result reaches |
| What is not known? | <Verdict is="yes" /> In both directions: where evidence is weak, and where it is promising |
| The date we checked each legal or regulatory status? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Because these change |

</Compare>

We never assume where a product came from, and we do not lecture. We state each risk once, with its reason. We present a doctor as someone who can help: with check-ups, with tests, and when something goes wrong.

<InDetail title="Why the line is where it is">

This site is published from the Netherlands, so Dutch law decides what it may say. Dutch law forbids any advertising for a medicine that has no licence. It also forbids advertising prescription-only medicines to the public.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-84-85-advertising" /> The law defines advertising by its purpose: any attempt to get people to prescribe, supply or use a medicine.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-1-reclame" />
{/* residue-ok: publisher */}
The EU Court of Justice ruled that information from an independent writer can also count as advertising. What matters is the purpose of the message.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-damgaard" />

Our readers' countries have similar rules. In the UK, Canada and Australia, the law forbids advertising a medicine that is not licensed there.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-279,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tg-act-advertising,au-tg-regs-reg-7-advertising-prescribed-goods" /> The rules where you live are on [the law in your country](/en/legal/).

A link to a shop, a price or a tip about where to get a product would give our information that purpose. Without them, our information stays information. That is why we name no sellers and give no doses. See [funding](/en/about/funding/).

We do describe warning signs of a dangerous product, practice or seller, taken from regulators. These are always warning signs only. No sign can show that a product is good, and we say so.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Words we avoid">

- We never call an untested product safe, and we never say something has been shown when it has not.
- We avoid hype words, and words that make a compound sound like a cure.
- We do not rank compounds against each other. If a trial compared two medicines directly, we give its numbers, its design and who took part.
- For approved medicines we say what the official label and the trials show, and for which use.

</InDetail>

## Who checks our work

<Lead question="Has a doctor checked these pages?">Not yet. Review by a named doctor and by a lawyer is still pending. Each page says so.</Lead>

<Diagram id="review-status" caption="Who has checked the pages so far, on 24 October 2026.">

| Check | Status |
|---|---|
| Every fact checked against its original source, in a separate step after writing | Done before a page is published, with AI help, overseen by the editor |
| Independent medical review by a named doctor on a public medical register | Pending |
| Independent legal review | Pending |
| Review of each translation by a professional translator | Pending |

</Diagram>

We will not present any page as reviewed until a named person has signed it off. Until then, each page shows a notice near the title. When a reviewer signs off, the page shows their name, their registration and the date.

<InDetail title="What the review line on each page means">

- **Medical review pending.** The page was written and checked against its sources, but no outside doctor has checked it yet. Read it as carefully sourced editorial work, not as reviewed medical advice.
- **Legal review pending.** The page describes the law from the laws themselves and from official guidance. It is not legal advice.
- **"Reviewed by" a person.** Only a real, named person can appear here. A medical reviewer is a registered doctor. We will name their register and show their registration, so anyone can check it: for example a US state medical board licence, the UK's General Medical Council register, a Canadian provincial college of physicians and surgeons, or Australia's Ahpra register.
- **These About pages** describe the site, not medicine, so our editorial board signs them.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="How we use AI">

We prepare drafts with the help of AI tools. The tools also help us search PubMed, trial registers and official websites. After writing, a separate checking step compares every fact with its original source: the study record, the regulator's page or the law. That step also uses AI tools, and the editor oversees the work. A fact we cannot check does not go on the page.

AI tools can make mistakes that sound convincing. That is why every claim links to its source, so you can check it too. It is also why no page is presented as reviewed until a named person, a doctor or a lawyer, signs it off.

Some drawings on this site were made with an AI image tool. They contain no text, they are labelled "AI-generated illustration", and a person must check each one for errors before it is published. Photos come from open archives, with their licence and source listed under each one.

</InDetail>

## Translations

<Lead question="Is every language version the same?">The science is the same. Each language covers the laws and services of its own readers' countries.</Lead>

<Diagram id="translation-model" caption="How each part of a page reaches another language.">

| Part of the page | How it is translated |
|---|---|
| Facts and numbers: evidence grades, study details, legal statuses, dates | Stored once and shown in every language, so they cannot change in translation |
| Explanations of science and medicine | Translated, then checked by a person; a notice shows until that check is done |
| Laws, approvals and help services | Written for each language's own readers' countries. The English site covers the US, the UK, Canada and Australia, with notes for Ireland and New Zealand |
| Emergency advice | English pages say "call your local emergency number". A language read mainly in one country may give that country's number |

</Diagram>

A translation never changes how strong the evidence is. Until a professional translator has checked a page, it shows a notice, and the English page is the reference for the science.

## Corrections

<Lead question="What if you get something wrong?">We fix it, date it, and list every real change in a public log.</Lead>

<Diagram id="correction-steps" caption="What we do when you tell us about a mistake.">

1. **Report.** Someone tells us about a possible mistake.
2. **Check.** We check it against the original source.
3. **Fix.** We correct the page. A mistake that could affect someone's safety comes first.
4. **Log.** Every real change goes into the public log, with its date.
5. **Show.** The page itself says that it was corrected, and when.

</Diagram>

How to report, and the full log, are on the [corrections page](/en/about/corrections/).

## Facts and opinion are kept apart

<Lead question="Do you take sides?">On policy, yes, on one signed opinion page. The fact pages do not depend on it.</Lead>

<Diagram id="facts-and-opinion" caption="Fact pages tell you what is known. One page gives our opinion.">

| Reference pages (all other pages) | Opinion page |
|---|---|
| Describe evidence, law and safety | Argues what should change |
| Every claim sourced | Factual claims sourced; the argument is ours |
| Unsigned; checked as reference work | Signed by the editorial board and dated |

</Diagram>

We think some rules should change. We say so in one place: [our position on reform](/en/the-case-for-reform/). It is marked as opinion. No compound, safety or legal page relies on it.
