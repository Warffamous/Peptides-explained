This page explains what a peptide physically is, from first principles. It is the foundation for the rest of the site: how peptides act on cells, how long they last in the body, and why most are injected.

## From amino acid to chain

<Lead question="What is a peptide made of?">Amino acids, joined end to end like beads on a string.</Lead>

<Diagram id="amino-acid-to-protein" caption="Amino acids join into chains. Short chains are peptides. Long, folded chains are proteins.">

1. **One amino acid.** A small molecule with an amino end, an acid end and a side chain. The side chain is what makes each of the 20 kinds different.
2. **Two join.** The amino end of one reacts with the acid end of the next. The link is called a peptide bond.
3. **A short chain.** More amino acids are added, one after the other. A chain of a few to about 50 is usually called a peptide.
4. **A long chain.** Longer chains are called polypeptides. They fold into a fixed shape and form proteins.

</Diagram>

<Cite id="nobel-1984-merrifield-press,nhgri-glossary-peptide,nhgri-glossary-amino-acids" />

Every peptide medicine on this site is such a chain. What it does depends on which amino acids sit where, and on the shape the chain takes.<Cite id="ema-2025-synthetic-peptides-guideline" />

<InDetail title="The chemistry of the link, and why order matters">

Every amino acid carries two reactive groups: an acid (carboxylic) group and a basic (amino) group. When the amino group of one amino acid reacts with the acid group of another, the bond between them makes a dipeptide.<Cite id="nobel-1984-merrifield-press" /> Chemists call this joining a condensation, because a molecule of water is given off. Digestion runs the same reaction backwards: enzymes in the gut cut the peptide bond by adding water back, which is called hydrolysis.<Cite id="zhu-2021-oral-delivery,santiago-2021-core-concepts" />

A chain therefore has two different ends: the amino end (N-terminus) and the acid end (C-terminus). A sequence is written as a row of three-letter codes that follow international naming rules, and regulators ask makers to mark both ends and any changes to them.<Cite id="ema-2025-synthetic-peptides-guideline" /> BPC-157, for example, is written <Seq>Gly-Glu-Pro-Pro-Pro-Gly-Lys-Pro-Ala-Asp-Asp-Ala-Gly-Leu-Val</Seq>: fifteen amino acids in a row.<Cite id="pubchem-bpc-157" />

There are 20 different amino acids in proteins, and in a natural protein their order is written in a gene.<Cite id="nhgri-glossary-amino-acids" /> Order is everything. It has long been accepted that the sequence of amino acids is what gives each protein its own chemical and physiological properties.<Cite id="nobel-1958-sanger-speech" /> Two chains with the same amino acids in a different order are different molecules.

</InDetail>

## Where "peptide" ends and "protein" begins

<Lead question="When is a chain a peptide, and when is it a protein?">There is no sharp line. Different experts draw it in different places.</Lead>

<Diagram id="peptide-or-protein-line" caption="Is insulin a peptide or a protein? It depends on who draws the line.">

| Who | Where they draw the line | Insulin (51 amino acids, two chains) is called |
|---|---|---|
| US genome institute (NHGRI) glossary | Peptide: a chain of typically 2 to 50 amino acids. A chain of 51 or more: polypeptide | Not named; the glossary counts one chain at a time |
| US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) rule | Protein: more than 40 amino acids, counting all chains together | A protein, regulated as a biological product since 23 March 2020 |
| Nobel Prize presentation speech, 1958 | No number given | A protein |
| A 2022 review of peptide medicines | Usually 500–5000 daltons | A peptide |
| A 2013 review of peptide medicines | Fewer than 50 amino acids and under 10,000 daltons | Unclear: too many amino acids in total, but light enough |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="nhgri-glossary-peptide,ecfr-21-600-3-protein,fda-2020-insulin-transition,nobel-1958-sanger-speech,wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides,diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics" />

The line is a convention, not a property of nature. A review of oral peptide medicines notes that insulin, at 51 amino acids, is described as either.<Cite id="zhu-2021-oral-delivery" /> The word "peptide" therefore tells you about size, not about what a molecule does or how risky it is.

<InDetail title="Why the line matters to regulators, and not to your body">

In the United States the line has legal weight. A federal rule defines a protein as an amino acid chain of more than 40 amino acids, and counts chains that belong together as one.<Cite id="ecfr-21-600-3-protein" /> Insulin, with 51 amino acids in two chains, therefore moved from the drug rules to the rules for biological products on 23 March 2020.<Cite id="fda-2020-insulin-transition" /> The molecule did not change; only the paperwork did.

Your body does not use either word. How well a molecule binds its target depends on its chemistry and its shape, whatever it is called.<Cite id="santiago-2021-core-concepts" /> In the same way, "peptide" is not a quality mark. It covers tested medicines such as semaglutide, experimental molecules such as BPC-157, and deadly natural toxins (see the last section of this page).

</InDetail>

## Shape: from chain to folded molecule

<Lead question="Does a peptide have a shape?">Yes. The chain twists and folds, and its shape decides what it can bind to.</Lead>

<Diagram id="levels-of-structure" caption="A chain has a sequence, then local twists, then an overall fold.">

| Level | What it is | Held together by |
|---|---|---|
| Primary structure | The order of the amino acids | Peptide bonds |
| Secondary structure | Local twists and pleats of the backbone, such as a helix or a flat sheet | Mainly hydrogen bonds |
| Tertiary structure | The whole chain folded in three dimensions | Contacts between side chains, sometimes sulfur bridges (disulfide bonds) |
| Quaternary structure | Two or more chains working as one unit | Contacts or bonds between the chains |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="ema-2025-synthetic-peptides-guideline" />

Large proteins fold into stable shapes. Many short peptides are floppy: they lack the support of a stable fold, which leaves their bonds exposed to enzymes.<Cite id="wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" /> Acid can also unfold some proteins, which is one reason the stomach is hostile to them.<Cite id="zhu-2021-oral-delivery" />

<InDetail title="Examples of shape in medicines">

- **Insulin** is two chains held together. The Nobel committee described in 1958 how Frederick Sanger worked out the order of its 51 amino acids and showed that it has two different chains, joined by two bridges of sulfur atoms.<Cite id="nobel-1958-sanger-speech" />
- **Dulaglutide** (Trulicity) is built from two identical chains linked by disulfide bonds. Each chain carries a GLP-1-like peptide joined to part of an antibody.<Cite id="ema-epar-trulicity" />
- **Ring-shaped peptides.** Chemists can close a chain into a ring. A ring helps a short peptide hold a helix or a turn that it could not keep on its own.<Cite id="wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" />

</InDetail>

## Size: why the weight of a molecule matters

<Lead question="Why does size matter?">Size decides if a molecule can pass the gut wall, the kidney filter and the walls of cells.</Lead>

<Diagram id="molecule-size-ladder" caption="Peptide medicines are far bigger than most pills.">

| Molecule | What it is | Weight (daltons) |
|---|---|---|
| Orforglipron | A small-molecule medicine (not a peptide) that acts like GLP-1 | 883 |
| BPC-157 | A chain of 15 amino acids | 1,419.5 |
| Semaglutide | A changed version of the gut hormone GLP-1, with a fatty side chain | about 4,114 |
| Insulin | 51 amino acids in two chains | about 5,808 |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="pubchem-orforglipron,pubchem-bpc-157,pubchem-semaglutide,pubchem-insulin-human" />

One dalton is roughly the weight of one hydrogen atom. A widely used rule of thumb from drug chemistry says that poor absorption is more likely when a molecule weighs more than 500 daltons.<Cite id="lipinski-2001-rule-of-5" /> Every molecule in the table is above that.

<InDetail title="Size, the kidneys and the immune system">

### Getting through barriers

Many pills are small molecules, and small size helps a medicine pass through cell membranes.<Cite id="lipinski-2001-rule-of-5" /> Natural peptides are typically large, water-loving and poorly able to cross membranes, and they are cleared quickly.<Cite id="di-2015-peptide-adme" /> That is why most peptides work on receptors on the outside of cells (see [how signalling works](/en/basics/how-signalling-works/)) and why most are injected (see [why not a pill](/en/basics/why-not-a-pill/)).

### The kidney filter

The kidney's filter (the glomerulus) holds molecules back by size and electrical charge. It lets only small amounts of albumin, a large blood protein, through.<Cite id="haraldsson-2008-glomerular-barrier" /> Peptides are generally small enough to be filtered freely.<Cite id="diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics" /> Fast clearance by the kidneys, together with breakdown by enzymes, is a common reason why peptides have short half-lives.<Cite id="werle-2006-half-life" /> Chemists use this: a peptide that clings to albumin, or that is fused to a large protein, becomes too big to pass the filter easily (see [pharmacokinetics](/en/basics/pharmacokinetics/)).<Cite id="wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" />

### The immune system

Medicines can provoke antibodies against themselves. Small peptides usually provoke fewer antibodies than larger protein medicines, but antibodies against the drug have been reported for some peptide medicines.<Cite id="wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides,diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics" /> Clumps of protein (aggregates) can make an immune response more likely.<Cite id="rosenberg-2006-aggregates-immunology" /> Makers design against this: dulaglutide was engineered to reduce its immune potential, and its European product information credits its close likeness to the body's own molecules.<Cite id="ema-epar-trulicity" /> {/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}

</InDetail>

## How chemists make peptides last

<Lead question="How do chemists stop a peptide being cut up?">They change the chain so enzymes cannot grip it, or make it too big to filter.</Lead>

<Diagram id="peptide-stabilising-tricks" caption="Chemists use a small set of tricks to make a peptide last longer in the body.">

| Trick | What it does | Example in an authorised medicine |
|---|---|---|
| Swap in a mirror-image amino acid (D-form) | Enzymes are built to fit the normal L-form, so a D-form at a cutting site blocks them | Leuprolide carries a D-leucine; octreotide carries D-phenylalanine and D-tryptophan |
| Close the chain into a ring | Fewer loose ends for enzymes; a more rigid shape | Octreotide (a ring held by a sulfur bridge) |
| Cap the ends | Resists enzymes that cut from the end of a chain | Leuprolide's modified end group |
| Swap the amino acid an enzyme targets | The enzyme DPP-4 can no longer cut the chain | Semaglutide (Aib at position 8) |
| Add a fatty-acid chain | The peptide clings to albumin, which the kidney filter mostly holds back | Liraglutide, semaglutide |
| Fuse to part of an antibody, or attach polyethylene glycol (PEG) | The molecule becomes too big to filter and is shielded from enzymes | Dulaglutide (antibody part) |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="lupron-depot-label,bauer-1982-octreotide,pubchem-octreotide,werle-2006-half-life,diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics,lau-2015-semaglutide-discovery,ema-epar-wegovy,ema-epar-victoza,haraldsson-2008-glomerular-barrier,ema-epar-trulicity,harris-2003-pegylation,wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" />

Each trick trades something. A D-amino acid can protect a peptide but often weakens its effect.<Cite id="wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" /> Semaglutide binds its receptor about three times less tightly than its predecessor liraglutide, a loss its designers accepted for stronger albumin binding.<Cite id="lau-2015-semaglutide-discovery" />

<InDetail title="Mirror-image amino acids, and two worked examples">

### Left- and right-handed amino acids

Except for glycine, every natural amino acid exists in two mirror-image forms, like a left and a right hand (the L-form and the D-form). Natural amino acids have the L-form.<Cite id="ema-2025-synthetic-peptides-guideline" /> The body's protein-cutting enzymes are shaped to fit L-chains, so putting a D-form at the spot where an enzyme cuts is an effective way to extend a peptide's life in the blood.<Cite id="wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" />

### Octreotide: four tricks in eight amino acids

The natural hormone somatostatin has a half-life of only a few minutes in the blood.<Cite id="werle-2006-half-life" /> Octreotide is a shortened copy of eight amino acids. It carries two D-amino acids, is closed into a ring by a sulfur bridge, and ends in an altered amino acid.<Cite id="bauer-1982-octreotide,pubchem-octreotide" /> <Claim level="in-vitro">In laboratory tests it resisted pure enzymes and tissue extracts</Claim>, and <Claim level="animal">in rats and rhesus monkeys it acted much longer than somatostatin</Claim>.<Cite id="bauer-1982-octreotide" /> A review gives its plasma half-life as about 1.5 hours, against a few minutes for somatostatin.<Cite id="werle-2006-half-life" />

### Semaglutide: three changes to a natural hormone

Natural GLP-1 has a half-life of about 1.5 to 2 minutes in the blood.<Cite id="ema-epar-trulicity" /> Semaglutide changes two amino acids (one to block the enzyme DPP-4) and adds a fatty-acid side chain that binds albumin; in people its half-life is about one week.<Cite id="lau-2015-semaglutide-discovery,ema-epar-wegovy" /> How half-life shapes everything else is explained on the [pharmacokinetics](/en/basics/pharmacokinetics/) page.

</InDetail>

## How peptides are made, and what each method leaves behind

<Lead question="How are peptide medicines made?">Most are built by chemists one amino acid at a time. Some are made by microbes.</Lead>

<Diagram id="two-ways-to-make-a-peptide" caption="Each way of making a peptide leaves its own kind of impurity.">

| | Chemical synthesis on a solid support | Made by microbes (recombinant) |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | The first amino acid is fixed to tiny resin beads. Protected amino acids are added one at a time, with washing between steps. The finished chain is cut free. | A gene for the chain is put into bacteria or yeast, which build it. The chain is then purified. |
| Typical leftovers | Chains with an amino acid missing (deletion sequences), cut short or doubled; mirror-image errors; leftover chemicals and solvents | Material from the host cells, such as their proteins and DNA; toxins from bacteria (endotoxin) |
| Examples | Most synthetic peptides | Human insulin (the first medicine of this kind); the semaglutide backbone (made in yeast) |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="ema-2025-synthetic-peptides-guideline,wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides,dhondt-2014-peptide-impurities,schneier-2020-endotoxin-review,johnson-1983-recombinant-insulin,fda-label-ozempic,ema-epar-wegovy" />

A regulated maker purifies the product and tests it against set limits before release.<Cite id="ecfr-21-211-165,ema-2025-synthetic-peptides-guideline" /> A product from outside the legal supply chain comes with no such guarantee; what labs have found in real vials is on the [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/) page.

<InDetail title="Why mistakes build up, and which impurities each method leaves">

### Why long chains are hard to make

<KeyNumber title="Small losses at each step add up over a long chain" value="61%" label="overall yield after 100 steps, even when each step works 99.5 times in 100" counted="A worked example in the Nobel Prize press release of 1984; at 90% per step, the overall yield is only 0.003%" source="nobel-1984-merrifield-press" />

Solid-phase synthesis was developed by Bruce Merrifield, who received the 1984 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. Fixing the growing chain to a solid lets chemists wash away by-products after each step instead of purifying every intermediate.<Cite id="nobel-1984-merrifield-press" /> The arithmetic in the key number explains the main weakness: a step that does not finish leaves some chains with an amino acid missing.<Cite id="ema-2025-synthetic-peptides-guideline" /> Making peptides of fewer than about 50 amino acids is now routine; longer chains remain hard to make on a large scale.<Cite id="wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" />

### Impurities from chemical synthesis

{/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}
A 2025 guideline of the European Medicines Agency lists the impurities chemists must control:<Cite id="ema-2025-synthetic-peptides-guideline" />

- **Deletion sequences**: chains with one or more amino acids missing, from steps that did not finish.
- **Truncated and insertion sequences**: chains whose growth was stopped early, or with an amino acid added twice.
- **Mirror-image errors** (racemisation or epimerisation): an amino acid that flipped to its D-form during the reaction.
- **Process chemicals**: leftover reagents, solvents and metals.

A review of impurities in peptide medicines adds unwanted counter-ions (salt partners) such as trifluoroacetate and, in poorly made products, contamination with other, unrelated peptides.<Cite id="dhondt-2014-peptide-impurities" /> Peptides also break down over time, for example by oxidation or by reacting with water (hydrolysis).<Cite id="ema-2025-synthetic-peptides-guideline,manning-2010-protein-stability" />

### Impurities from recombinant production

Microbes build the chain from its gene, but the raw product contains material from the cells themselves, such as enzymes, DNA and RNA fragments and unrelated proteins.<Cite id="wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" /> When bacteria are used, endotoxin is a particular concern: it is part of the outer wall of some bacteria and a major cause of fever reactions to contaminated medicines.<Cite id="schneier-2020-endotoxin-review" />

Recombinant production made human insulin the first commercial health-care product of this technology, in the early 1980s.<Cite id="johnson-1983-recombinant-insulin" /> The US label for semaglutide states that its peptide backbone is produced by yeast fermentation.<Cite id="fda-label-ozempic" /> The European product information names the yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) and the method, recombinant DNA technology.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy" /> {/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}

</InDetail>

## "Natural" says nothing about safety

<Lead question="If a peptide is natural, is it harmless?">No. What a peptide does depends on the molecule, the amount and where it goes.</Lead>

<Diagram id="natural-is-not-harmless" caption="Some natural peptides keep you alive. Some kill. One became a medicine.">

| Natural peptide | Where it comes from | What it can do |
|---|---|---|
| Insulin | Your own pancreas | Keeps you alive. Too much makes blood sugar fall dangerously low |
| Alpha-amanitin | The death cap mushroom | A ring-shaped peptide that destroys the liver; it survives cooking and digestion |
| Exendin-4 | Gila monster venom | The starting point for exenatide, which became an authorised diabetes medicine |
| GnRH (a brain hormone) | Your own brain | Given in pulses, it keeps the sex-hormone system going. Given without a break, it shuts it down |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="nhs-low-blood-sugar,garcia-2015-amanita,le-dare-2021-amanitins,eng-1992-exendin-4,wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides,dailymed-byetta-label,belchetz-1978-gnrh-pulses,lupron-depot-label" />

Effects follow from the dose, the timing and the tissue reached, not from where a molecule was first found.<Cite id="santiago-2021-core-concepts,belchetz-1978-gnrh-pulses" /> A product sold as a natural peptide is also a manufactured product, with the impurities described above.

<InDetail title="Three reasons natural is not the same as harmless">

- **Amount.** Insulin is the body's own hormone, yet too much causes low blood sugar, with shaking, confusion and, if severe, unconsciousness.<Cite id="nhs-low-blood-sugar" /> A 2026 case report describes two people who injected semaglutide obtained without a prescription and came to hospital in a coma with very low blood sugar: the product contained insulin.<Cite id="stammers-2026-insulin-falsified-semaglutide" />
- **Pattern.** <Claim level="animal">In a study in rhesus monkeys whose own hormone signal had been switched off, hourly pulses of GnRH restored the pituitary's hormone release, while a constant infusion of the same hormone did not; switching from pulses to a constant infusion could desensitise the pituitary again</Claim>.<Cite id="belchetz-1978-gnrh-pulses" /> Leuprolide, a synthetic, modified nine-amino-acid version of GnRH, works this way in people: it first raises sex hormones and then, given continuously, lowers testosterone to castrate levels within two to four weeks. Medicine uses this on purpose, in prostate cancer, for example.<Cite id="lupron-depot-label" /> The same natural signal, delivered in a different pattern, has the opposite effect. The [signalling page](/en/basics/how-signalling-works/) explains why.
- **Nature makes poisons too.** The death cap mushroom is involved in most fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide, and alpha-amanitin, a double ring of eight amino acids, is mainly responsible.<Cite id="garcia-2015-amanita,le-dare-2021-amanitins" /> It resists heat, acid and enzymes, so cooking and digestion do not destroy it.<Cite id="le-dare-2021-amanitins" />

Nature is also a rich source of medicines: exenatide was developed from a peptide found in Gila monster venom.<Cite id="eng-1992-exendin-4,wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides,muttenthaler-2021-peptide-trends" /> The point is not that natural peptides are dangerous or good. It is that the origin of a molecule tells you neither.

</InDetail>
