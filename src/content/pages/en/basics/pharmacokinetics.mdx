Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to a medicine. It decides how often a medicine is given, how long it takes to work fully, and how long its effects last after stopping. This page explains the principles with examples from authorised medicines. It gives no doses and no calculations for use.

## ADME: four things the body does to a medicine

<Lead question="What happens to a medicine after it enters the body?">It is absorbed, spread around, broken down and removed. Experts call this ADME.</Lead>

<Diagram id="adme-for-peptides" caption="The body does four things to every medicine. For peptides, two of them matter most.">

| Step | What it means | What it looks like for a peptide |
|---|---|---|
| **A**bsorption | Getting from where it was given into the blood | After an injection under the skin, it seeps into small blood vessels or lymph |
| **D**istribution | Spreading through the body | Mostly stays in the blood and the fluid around cells; some cling to the blood protein albumin |
| **M**etabolism | Being changed or broken down | Enzymes cut the peptide bonds, in the blood and in tissues |
| **E**xcretion | Leaving the body | Small peptides and their pieces are filtered out by the kidneys into the urine |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="santiago-2021-core-concepts,supersaxo-1990-lymphatic,diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics,di-2015-peptide-adme,fda-label-wegovy" />

For a natural peptide, breakdown and kidney filtering are fast. That is why many natural peptide hormones last only minutes in the blood.<Cite id="di-2015-peptide-adme,werle-2006-half-life" /> Almost everything chemists do to turn a peptide into a medicine aims at slowing those two steps.

<InDetail title="The words pharmacologists use">

- **Bioavailability**: the share of a dose that reaches the bloodstream unchanged. It is measured against an injection straight into a vein, which counts as 100%.<Cite id="santiago-2021-core-concepts" />
- **First-pass metabolism**: breakdown before a medicine reaches the general circulation, for example in the gut wall or liver after swallowing.<Cite id="santiago-2021-core-concepts" />
- **Clearance**: the volume of plasma (the liquid part of the blood) that is cleared of the medicine per unit of time. Total clearance adds up what the liver, the kidneys and other routes remove.<Cite id="santiago-2021-core-concepts" />
- **Volume of distribution**: a theoretical measure of how far a medicine has moved from the blood into tissues. Binding to blood proteins and tissues affects how a medicine moves through the body.<Cite id="santiago-2021-core-concepts" />
- **Elimination** covers both breakdown and excretion; excretion is only the physical removal.<Cite id="santiago-2021-core-concepts" />

Semaglutide shows how these fit together. More than 99% of it in the blood is bound to albumin. It is broken down by enzymes that cut its peptide backbone and by breaking down its fatty side chain, and only about 3% leaves in the urine unchanged.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" />
{/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}
The European product information names one of these enzymes, neutral endopeptidase.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy" />

</InDetail>

## Half-life: the number that drives everything

<Lead question="What is a half-life?">The time it takes for the amount of a medicine in the blood to fall by half.</Lead>

{/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}
<KeyNumber title="The natural hormone is gone in minutes" value="1.5–2 minutes" label="half-life of natural GLP-1 in the blood" who="The body's own GLP-1; European product information for Trulicity (dulaglutide)" source="ema-epar-trulicity" level="established" />

<KeyNumber title="The changed copy takes about a week to fall by half" value="about 1 week" label="half-life in the blood, in people" who="People with overweight or obesity; US label for Wegovy (semaglutide)" source="fda-label-wegovy" level="established" />

The two molecules act on the same receptor. The difference in how long they last is engineered: semaglutide resists the enzyme that cuts GLP-1 and clings to albumin, so the kidneys clear it slowly and enzymes reach it less.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" /> Half-life then sets how often a medicine is given, how long it takes to build up and how long it lingers.

<InDetail title="What half-life controls, step by step">

### Each half-life closes half the gap

When a medicine is used repeatedly, it builds up until the amount removed between doses equals the amount given. This balance is called steady state.<Cite id="santiago-2021-core-concepts" /> Because the body removes a fixed share in each half-life, each half-life closes half of the remaining gap to that balance. The same arithmetic runs in reverse after the last dose.

<Diagram id="half-life-staircase" caption="Each half-life closes half the gap. After four or five, little changes.">

| Half-lives passed | With repeated use: how close to steady state | After the last dose: how much is left |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50% of the way | 50% |
| 2 | 75% | 25% |
| 3 | about 88% | about 13% |
| 4 | about 94% | about 6% |
| 5 | about 97% | about 3% |

</Diagram>

This is why the US label for semaglutide tablets, with a half-life of about one week, states that steady state is reached after four to five weeks of daily use.<Cite id="fda-label-rybelsus-ozempic-tablets" /> It is also why semaglutide stays in the circulation for about five to seven weeks after the last dose.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" />

### Why a long half-life cuts both ways

- **Fewer injections and steadier levels.** A long half-life allows weekly use, and daily tablets with a long half-life give fewer ups and downs from day to day.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy,ema-epar-rybelsus" />
- **Side effects last long too.** After an overdose of semaglutide, the US label warns that a prolonged period of observation and treatment may be needed because of its one-week half-life.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" /> There is no specific antidote.<Cite id="ema-epar-rybelsus" />
- **Slow to start, slow to stop.** It takes weeks to reach steady levels, and weeks for them to fade. Because of the long half-life, the US label for Wegovy says to stop it at least two months before a planned pregnancy.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" />

</InDetail>

## How the body clears a peptide

<Lead question="How does the body get rid of a peptide?">Enzymes cut it into pieces, and the kidneys filter small peptides out of the blood.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="BPC-157 was gone in minutes in rats and dogs" value="5.3–15.2 minutes" label="half-life of BPC-157 after a shot into a vein: 5.3 in dogs, 15.2 in rats" counted="One study in rats and dogs (He 2022), as summarised by the FDA in 2026; no human value has been published" source="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" level="animal" />

Enzymes that cut peptide bonds (proteases) are found throughout the body, in the blood and in tissues such as the liver and kidneys.<Cite id="diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics,werle-2006-half-life" /> Most peptides are also small enough to pass the kidney filter, and filtering and breakdown in the kidney together remove many of them.<Cite id="diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics" /> BPC-157 resisted gastric juice in a lab test, yet vanished quickly from the blood of rats and dogs, probably through enzymes in blood and tissues.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

<InDetail title="Enzymes, kidneys and the evidence behind them">

### Enzymes everywhere

The amide bonds that link amino acids are easily cut by enzymes in the body when nothing protects them.<Cite id="wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" /> Proteases in blood, liver and kidney each prefer particular amino acids, so where they cut can be predicted from the sequence.<Cite id="werle-2006-half-life" /> <Claim level="in-vitro">In lab tests with human kidney preparations and liver fractions, the synthetic peptide TB-500 was broken down actively, by cleavage of all its peptide bonds</Claim>.<Cite id="zvereva-2016-peptide-metabolism" />

### The kidney filter

The kidney's filter holds back molecules by size and charge, letting only small amounts of albumin through.<Cite id="haraldsson-2008-glomerular-barrier" /> Peptides are generally filtered freely, and filtration plus breakdown in the kidney remove many peptide medicines.<Cite id="diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics" /> Exenatide (Byetta) is an example: in animal studies it was cleared mainly by kidney filtration followed by breakdown, and its half-life in people was 2.4 hours.<Cite id="dailymed-byetta-label" />

### Where the BPC-157 numbers come from

The half-lives above come from one study in which rats and dogs received a single injection into a vein. The FDA notes that BPC-157 resisted gastric juice in a lab test, so its short half-life in the body is probably due to enzymes in the blood or tissues.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,he-2022-pharmacokinetics" /> Animal half-lives do not translate directly to people, and no human measurement has been published (see the last section of this page).

</InDetail>

## How chemists stretch the half-life

<Lead question="How do you make a peptide last a week?">Stop enzymes from cutting it, and make it too big for the kidney filter.</Lead>

<Diagram id="glp1-half-life-ladder" caption="The same hormone, engineered in different ways, lasts from minutes to a week.">

| Molecule | What was changed | Half-life in people | How often the label says it is used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural GLP-1 | Nothing: cut by the enzyme DPP-4 and filtered by the kidneys | 1.5–2 minutes | Not a medicine |
| Exenatide (Byetta) | A related peptide from lizard venom | 2.4 hours | Twice a day |
| Liraglutide (Victoza) | Fatty-acid chain that binds albumin; molecules cluster under the skin; more stable against enzymes | about 13 hours | Once a day |
| Dulaglutide (Trulicity) | Fused to part of an antibody; resists DPP-4; too big to filter easily | 4.7 days | Once a week |
| Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) | DPP-4 blocked at position 8; fatty-acid chain that binds albumin | about 1 week | Once a week (injection) |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="ema-epar-trulicity,dailymed-byetta-label,eng-1992-exendin-4,ema-epar-victoza,fda-label-wegovy,lau-2015-semaglutide-discovery,wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" />

Three strategies dominate. Clinging to albumin hides a peptide from the kidney filter and from enzymes. Fusing it to a large protein does the same by size. Changing the amino acid an enzyme targets removes the cutting site.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy,ema-epar-trulicity,wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" />

<InDetail title="Albumin, antibody parts, PEG and reactive hooks">

- **Albumin binding through a fatty acid.** Albumin is a large blood protein that the kidneys hardly filter. A fatty-acid chain makes the peptide cling to it; bound peptide escapes kidney filtration and is protected from breakdown.<Cite id="haraldsson-2008-glomerular-barrier,fda-label-wegovy,wang-2022-therapeutic-peptides" /> Liraglutide adds a second trick: its molecules cluster under the skin and are absorbed slowly.<Cite id="ema-epar-victoza" />
- **Fusion to an antibody part (Fc).** Dulaglutide joins two GLP-1-like chains to part of a human antibody. Its large size slows absorption and reduces kidney clearance.<Cite id="ema-epar-trulicity" />
- **PEG chains.** Attaching chains of polyethylene glycol (PEGylation) increases a molecule's size and shields it from protein-cutting enzymes.<Cite id="harris-2003-pegylation" />
- **A reactive hook for albumin.** CJC-1295, an experimental growth-hormone-releasing peptide, carries a reactive chemical group that bonds to albumin after injection. <Claim level="animal">In the developer's own study in rats (numbers not given in the abstract), it was found bound to albumin 15 minutes after injection and was still in the blood beyond 72 hours</Claim>.<Cite id="jette-2005-cjc1295-albumin" /> In two randomised, placebo-controlled trials in healthy adults its estimated half-life was 5.8 to 8.1 days.<Cite id="teichman-2006-cjc1295" />

Every trick changes the molecule. A longer half-life also means that a side effect, or an error in the amount used, lasts longer.<Cite id="ema-epar-rybelsus" />

</InDetail>

## Under the skin: how an injection reaches the blood

<Lead question="What happens after an injection under the skin?">The medicine seeps into small blood vessels, or into lymph if the molecule is large.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Most of the injected dose got into the blood" value="89%" label="of the dose reached the bloodstream after an injection under the skin" who="Semaglutide; US label for Wegovy" source="fda-label-wegovy" level="established" />

{/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}
<KeyNumber title="With this one, only about half got in" value="about 55%" label="of the dose reached the bloodstream after an injection under the skin" who="Liraglutide; European product information for Victoza" source="ema-epar-victoza" level="established" />

Even an injection does not deliver the whole dose, and the share differs between molecules. Small molecules enter the tiny blood vessels near the injection site. <Claim level="animal">In a study in sheep (three per substance, four substances), the larger the molecule, the more of it travelled through the lymph instead; above a molecular weight of 16,000, most did</Claim>.<Cite id="supersaxo-1990-lymphatic" />

<InDetail title="What slows absorption, and what does not">

- **Size.** Large molecules are absorbed more slowly. The European product information for dulaglutide says its large size slows absorption.<Cite id="ema-epar-trulicity" /> {/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */} The sheep study above found more lymphatic absorption with every step up in molecular weight, from 246 to 19,000.<Cite id="supersaxo-1990-lymphatic" />
- **Clustering.** Liraglutide molecules cluster together under the skin, which releases them slowly.<Cite id="ema-epar-victoza" />
- **Site.** For semaglutide, the US label reports similar exposure after injection in the abdomen, thigh or upper arm.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" />

Bioavailability after an injection under the skin is a property of each product, measured in trials. It cannot be assumed for a molecule that has never been studied in people.

</InDetail>

## Many unapproved peptides have no human data

<Lead question="How do unapproved peptides behave in the human body?">Often nobody knows. For many of them, this was never measured in people.</Lead>

<Compare title="For an authorised medicine these basic facts are known. For three unapproved peptides, they are not." detail="What the US label says about semaglutide, and what the FDA found for BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV in 2026.">

| | Semaglutide (authorised) | BPC-157 | TB-500 | KPV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Half-life measured in people? | <Verdict is="yes" /> About one week<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No human measurement<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No human studies found<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No human studies found<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> |
| Known how much reaches the blood after an injection under the skin? | <Verdict is="yes" /> 89%<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Not known, even in animals<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Not known in people<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Not known in people<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> |
| Known how long it stays after stopping? | <Verdict is="yes" /> About five to seven weeks<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Not known in people<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Not known in people<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Not known in people<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> |

</Compare>

Without these measurements nobody can say how long an effect or a side effect will last, whether the substance builds up with repeated use, or how much of a dose reaches the blood at all. Missing data is not proof of harm. It means the risk cannot be estimated.

<InDetail title="What the FDA found, and what a missing half-life means in practice">

### What the regulators found

In 2026 the FDA evaluated several unapproved peptides that had been put forward for use in US pharmacy compounding, including these three. For BPC-157 it found no human pharmacokinetic data after use by mouth, under the skin, in the nose or through the skin. It noted that in two human studies of BPC-157 given as an enema, the authors reported that it was not detected in the blood (one abstract said "most" levels were too low to measure).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> For TB-500 and KPV it found no human studies of pharmacokinetics by any route.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> Because BPC-157's absolute bioavailability by the proposed routes was not known even in rats and dogs, the FDA could not use the animal safety data to estimate no-harm levels for those routes.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

### What follows from it

- **Animal half-lives do not transfer.** A peptide that lasts minutes in a rat may behave differently in a person; that is the subject of [the translation gap](/en/evidence/the-translation-gap/).
- **The product adds its own uncertainty.** A vial from outside the legal supply chain may hold a different amount, a different salt form or a different substance from its label, which changes everything above (see [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/)).
- **A doctor can still help.** Even without data on the substance, a doctor can check for the effects that matter, such as blood sugar or liver tests, and record what happened. See [monitoring](/en/safety/monitoring/).

This gap has a cost in both directions. People who use these products cannot know how long an effect lasts, and researchers cannot tell whether a promising animal result could ever work in people until someone measures it.

</InDetail>
