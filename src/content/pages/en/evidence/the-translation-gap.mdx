Many of the peptides on this site, such as BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV, have been studied mainly in cells and animals, and some only in a handful of studies.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> This page explains, cause by cause, why a result in an animal so often fails to appear in people, and why an animal dose cannot simply be scaled up to a human one. It gives no conversion factor or formula, on purpose.

## How often animal results reach people

<Lead question="How often does an animal result become a medicine?">Rarely. In one large review, 5 in 100 treatments tested in animals were approved.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Half reached a test in people. Only 5 in 100 were approved." value="5%" label="of treatments tested in animals were later approved for use in people" counted="281 treatments from 122 systematic reviews, more than 10 years after the first animal study (Ineichen 2024)" source="ineichen-2024-animal-to-human" />

<KeyNumber title="About a third of famous animal studies held up in trials" value="about 1 in 3" label="well-known animal studies held up in fair trials in people" counted="76 highly cited animal studies in leading journals (Hackam and Redelmeier 2006)" source="hackam-2006-animal-translation,hackam-2007-translating-animal-research" />

<KeyNumber title="Even after tests in people begin, most drugs fail" value="13.8%" label="of drug programmes went from the first human trials to approval" counted="41,040 industry programmes, each one drug for one use, 2000 to 2015 (Wong 2019)" source="wong-2019-clinical-trial-success" />

The three numbers count different things, but they point the same way. An animal result is a good reason to test in people. It is not a forecast of what will happen in people. The sections below explain why, one cause at a time.

<InDetail title="What each number counts, and how to read it">

**From animals to approval.** An umbrella review from Zurich and Edinburgh collected 122 systematic reviews on 367 treatments. Of the 281 treatments whose first animal study was more than 10 years old, 50% had entered any study in people, 40% a randomised trial, and 5% had been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration; for those that got there, approval came a median of 10 years after the first animal study.<Cite id="ineichen-2024-animal-to-human" /> The same review cuts the other way too: among 62 treatments with at least five animal studies, 79% of animal studies were positive, against 62% of clinical studies and 50% of randomised trials. The authors call this 86% concordance, argue that translation may be better than often claimed, and link the low approval rate to weaknesses in both animal studies and early trials.<Cite id="ineichen-2024-animal-to-human" />

**Highly cited animal studies.** Of 76 highly cited animal studies from leading journals, only just over a third were confirmed by randomised trials in people, and about one in ten treatments was approved.<Cite id="hackam-2006-animal-translation,hackam-2007-translating-animal-research,van-der-worp-2010-animal-models" /> These had a median of 889 citations; less visible animal research probably translates less often.<Cite id="van-der-worp-2010-animal-models" />

**Once trials in people start.** Of 41,040 industry programmes from 2000 to 2015, 66.4% moved from phase 1 to phase 2, 58.3% from phase 2 to phase 3 and 59.0% from phase 3 to approval: 13.8% in all, and 3.4% for cancer.<Cite id="wong-2019-clinical-trial-success" />

None of this makes animal research useless: it is how promising leads are found, and how many dangers are caught before anyone is exposed. The gap is the reason human trials exist.

</InDetail>

## A rat is not a small person

<Lead question="Why can a rat respond differently from a person?">Its receptors, immune system and drug handling differ, and its whole life lasts a few years.</Lead>

<Diagram id="species-differences" caption="Five ways a rat or mouse differs from a person. Each can change a result in either direction.">

| What differs | An example | What it means for a result |
|---|---|---|
| Where receptors sit | Rat and mouse thyroid C-cells carry receptors for GLP-1 medicines; human and monkey C-cells have few | Rodents grew thyroid tumours on these medicines; regulators judge the risk to people low, but not ruled out |
| The immune system | Many immune cell types, receptors and signals differ between mice and people | An anti-inflammatory effect in mice may not appear in people |
| How the body handles a drug | Protein binding, breakdown and transport differ between species | The same dose can give a different amount in the blood, for a different time |
| Pace of life | Laboratory rats live about 2 to 3.5 years | Time in a rat study does not match time in a person's life |
| The target itself | A human target can be missing, or work differently, in test species | Safety tests can miss what only happens in people (see TGN1412 below) |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="knudsen-2010-thyroid-c-cells,ema-epar-wegovy,mestas-2004-mice-not-men,seok-2013-mouse-inflammation,sharma-2009-dose-extrapolation,sengupta-2013-rat-age,eastwood-2010-tgn1412-cd28" />

The difference cuts both ways. An effect seen in rats may not appear in people, and a harm seen in rats may not apply to people either. So each animal finding is a hypothesis about people that has to be tested in people.

<InDetail title="Four differences in more detail">

**Receptors in different places.** <Claim level="animal">GLP-1 medicines raised calcitonin and caused C-cell overgrowth in rodents, whose thyroid C-cells carry GLP-1 receptors; human and monkey C-cells had low receptor levels, and 20 months of liraglutide caused no C-cell overgrowth in monkeys</Claim>.<Cite id="knudsen-2010-thyroid-c-cells" /> The [semaglutide page](/en/compounds/semaglutide/) explains what this means for people, which is not fully settled.

**Immune systems.** Mouse and human immune systems differ in the balance of white-cell types, in receptors that detect germs, in signalling molecules (cytokines) and in the signals that switch immune cells on.<Cite id="mestas-2004-mice-not-men" /> In one comparison of gene responses to trauma, burns and bacterial toxin, the mouse models matched the human conditions little better than chance.<Cite id="seok-2013-mouse-inflammation" /> A later re-analysis of the same data, looking only at genes that changed in both species, found much closer agreement, so how well these mouse models mirror human inflammation is still argued over.<Cite id="takao-2015-mouse-inflammation-reanalysis" />

**Drug handling.** How long a drug stays in the blood depends on protein binding, breakdown and transport, and all three differ between species. Drugs that bind strongly to blood proteins, are broken down extensively or carried actively, or whose targets differ between species, are unlikely to suit simple scaling by size.<Cite id="sharma-2009-dose-extrapolation" /> Some peptide medicines are built to bind blood proteins on purpose: semaglutide is more than 99% bound to albumin.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy" />

**A short life.** Laboratory rats live about 2 to 3.5 years; in adulthood, one rat month corresponds to roughly three human years.<Cite id="sengupta-2013-rat-age" /> So time does not carry over simply: how long a rat study lasted says little about what years of use would do in a person.

</InDetail>

## The animal and the injury are not the patient

<Lead question="Is a rat with a lab-made injury like a patient?">Rarely. The animals are young and healthy, and the injury is fresh and uniform.</Lead>

<Diagram id="lab-versus-clinic" caption="Stroke research shows how far a typical animal study can be from a patient.">

| | In a typical animal study of stroke | In patients with stroke |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Almost always young animals, female animals rarely; over 95% rats and mice | Mostly older people with other health problems |
| Genetic make-up | A uniform group of animals | A varied population |
| Other illnesses | About 1 in 10 studies used animals with high blood pressure; fewer than 1 in 100 used animals with diabetes | Up to 75% have high blood pressure and 68% high blood sugar |
| Time to treatment | A median of 10 minutes after the injury | Later: 10 minutes is not possible in a clinic |
| What is measured | Tissue damage after 1 to 3 days | Function after about 3 months |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="van-der-worp-2010-animal-models" />

About 500 treatments improved outcome in animal models of stroke. In patients, only aspirin and very early clot-dissolving treatment proved effective.<Cite id="van-der-worp-2010-animal-models" /> Peptide studies often follow the same pattern: two of the Zagreb group's BPC-157 studies, of tendons and of fistulas, gave the first dose 30 minutes after making the injury.<Cite id="staresinic-2003-achilles,klicek-2008-fistula" />

<InDetail title="Why a fresh, uniform injury flatters a treatment">

A model is built for a clear result: the same injury, in animals of the same age, strain and sex, treated at the same moment. That clarity makes a small study possible, and makes the result hard to carry over. The stroke review lists the causes of poor external validity: disease induced in young, otherwise healthy animals; a uniform group of animals instead of varied patients; timing and dosing impossible in a clinic; and outcome measures unlike those that matter in trials.<Cite id="van-der-worp-2010-animal-models" /> The Zurich umbrella review names the same problem.<Cite id="ineichen-2024-animal-to-human" />

A tendon cut in a young rat and treated within half an hour is a long way from a sports injury that has troubled someone for a year.

</InDetail>

## Small, unblinded and unpublished

<Lead question="Why are animal results often too good?">Many are small, not blind and not random. Studies that find nothing often go unpublished.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="When chance did not pick the groups, studies found an effect more often" value="3.4" label="times the odds of reporting a positive result when chance did not pick the groups" counted="290 animal studies with two or more groups, presented as abstracts at US emergency-medicine meetings 1997–2001 (Bebarta 2003)" source="bebarta-2003-randomisation-blinding" />

<KeyNumber title="Missing studies that found nothing make the benefit look bigger" value="about 1 in 3" label="of the benefit seen in stroke studies in animals may come from missing studies" counted="525 publications in 16 systematic reviews of animal stroke studies (Sena 2010)" source="sena-2010-publication-bias" />

<KeyNumber title="Few studies planned how many animals they needed" value="8 of 113" label="studies of a stroke drug in animals said how they chose how many animals to use" counted="113 animal studies of thrombolysis for stroke (cited by Hackam 2007)" source="hackam-2007-translating-animal-research" />

Each of these flaws pushes results the same way: towards an effect. Researchers who know which animals were treated may score them more kindly. Choosing which animal goes into which group can tilt the groups. Small studies swing widely, and the swings in the positive direction are the ones that get published.

<InDetail title="The evidence on bias in animal research">

- **Randomisation and blinding.** In 290 animal studies, those without randomisation were more likely to report a positive outcome (odds ratio 3.4), and so were those without blinding (3.2).<Cite id="bebarta-2003-randomisation-blinding" /> In most systematic reviews of animal studies, fewer than half of the studies reported random allocation, and 0 to 3% a sample size worked out in advance.<Cite id="van-der-worp-2010-animal-models" /> In 2,671 animal studies of drugs collected for systematic reviews, 25% reported randomisation and under 1% a sample size calculation.<Cite id="macleod-2015-risk-of-bias" />
- **What gets published.** Of 525 publications of animal stroke experiments, only 2% reported no significant effect on the size of the brain damage. Correcting for the studies that seem to be missing lowered the average benefit by about a third.<Cite id="sena-2010-publication-bias" />
- **What holds up.** Scientists at one company could confirm only 6 of 53 landmark preclinical cancer studies.<Cite id="begley-2012-preclinical-standards" /> When 50 experiments from high-profile cancer papers were repeated, the typical (median) positive effect was 85% smaller than first reported.<Cite id="errington-2021-replicability" />

For BPC-157, most of the animal literature comes from a single group; see [the Croatian lab](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/).

</InDetail>

## Why an animal dose cannot be scaled to a person

<Lead question="Can an animal dose be converted to a human dose?">No. Scaling gives regulators only a cautious first test dose. Then trials in people take over.</Lead>

<Diagram id="first-dose-in-people" caption="How regulators pick a first dose for people. The animal number is just a careful first step.">

1. **Animal toxicity studies** find the highest dose at which no harm was seen.
2. **That dose is scaled** to a human figure, usually by body surface area: a rough correction for size, nothing more.
3. **A safety margin is applied**, larger when there are more reasons for concern.
4. **Researchers also estimate the lowest amount expected to have any effect at all** (MABEL), using human cells where they can. In Europe, the first dose for healthy volunteers should stay below the amount expected to have the intended effect.
5. **A few volunteers** get the first dose, one person first, with time to watch for reactions before the others are dosed.
6. **Doses go up step by step**, with rules for stopping, while blood levels and effects are measured in people.

</Diagram>

<Cite id="fda-2005-starting-dose-guidance,ema-2017-first-in-human-guideline" />

The calculation exists to protect volunteers at a first exposure, under medical watch, with a known and pure substance. It starts from what harmed animals, and it aims to stay below the amount expected to work. So it cannot tell anyone what dose works in people, or how much can be used without harm outside a trial. Only the human trials that follow can.

<InDetail title="Why scaling cannot give a dose that works, or one safe to keep using">

The US guidance describes how to choose the first dose for healthy volunteers "regardless of the projected clinical use". It starts from the highest dose without observed harm in animal toxicity studies, not from a dose that worked, and it "does not address dose escalation". Its safety margin exists because of known gaps between species: people may be more sensitive; some toxic effects, "e.g., headache, myalgias, mental disturbances", are hard to detect in animals; receptor numbers and binding strength can differ; toxicity can be unexpected; and absorption, breakdown and excretion differ. It warns that "human bioavailability and metabolism may differ significantly from that of animals".<Cite id="fda-2005-starting-dose-guidance" />

The European guideline states that the expected exposure in people "is considered more relevant than the relative dose levels between animals and humans", and asks for estimates from human cells where possible, because sensitivity can differ. For healthy volunteers, it asks that the first dose give an exposure below the level expected to have a drug effect (the pharmacologically active dose), unless there is a robust reason to go higher.<Cite id="ema-2017-first-in-human-guideline" /> Differences in transport, breakdown and dose-response "can override the effect of size alone".<Cite id="sharma-2009-dose-extrapolation" />

So a scaled animal dose answers one narrow question: roughly how much of this pure substance a closely watched volunteer can probably tolerate once. It does not say how much works, how much can be taken week after week without harm, or what is actually in a vial from outside the legal supply chain; tests of such products have found amounts that differ from the label (see [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/)).<Cite id="ashraf-2024-online-semaglutide" />

</InDetail>

## TGN1412: when animal safety tests missed the danger

<Lead question="Can animal tests miss a serious danger?">Yes. In 2006, six healthy men became critically ill within hours of a first human dose.</Lead>

<TimeStrip title="Within hours of one infusion, all six volunteers were seriously ill. All six survived." detail="What happened to the six men in the first trial of TGN1412, in hours after the infusion." min={0} max={24} step={4} axis="Hours after the infusion" source="suntharalingam-2006-tgn1412" rows={[
  { label: "Body-wide inflammatory reaction", spans: [{ from: 0, to: 1.5, text: "began within 90 minutes" }] },
  { label: "Critically ill: lungs, kidneys and blood clotting affected", spans: [{ from: 12, to: 16, text: "12 to 16 hours after the infusion" }] },
  { label: "Immune cells in the blood fell sharply", spans: [{ from: 0, to: 24, text: "within 24 hours" }] },
]} />

TGN1412 was a lab-made antibody, not a peptide, designed to switch on immune cells. The animal safety tests had not predicted the reaction.<Cite id="suntharalingam-2006-tgn1412,eastwood-2010-tgn1412-cd28" /> The likely cause, found later: one type of human immune cell carries the switch the drug acts on, and the same cells in the test species do not. The next year, the EU medicines agency issued its first guideline on first trials in people.<Cite id="eastwood-2010-tgn1412-cd28,ema-2017-first-in-human-guideline" />

<InDetail title="What went wrong, and what changed">

Six healthy young men each received a single dose into a vein of TGN1412, a monoclonal antibody designed as a superagonist of CD28 (a switch on T cells). <Claim level="human-limited">Within 90 minutes all six had a body-wide inflammatory reaction; within 12 to 16 hours they were critically ill, with lung injury, kidney failure and widespread clotting problems</Claim>. Two needed intensive organ support for 8 and 16 days. All six survived.<Cite id="suntharalingam-2006-tgn1412" />

The <Term id="cytokine-storm">cytokine storm</Term> was "not predicted by pre-clinical safety testing". In 2010, scientists at the UK National Institute for Biological Standards and Control reported the likely reason: <Claim level="in-vitro">in human cells TGN1412 switched on CD4+ effector memory T cells, and in the species used for safety testing these cells do not carry CD28, so the antibody could not act on them</Claim>.<Cite id="eastwood-2010-tgn1412-cd28" /> The animals lacked the part of the human system the drug acted on.

The European guideline on first-in-human trials, first issued in 2007 and revised in 2017, asks sponsors to show that the species tested is relevant to people, to take "a cautious approach" with medicines that are highly specific to humans, to choose the starting point for the first dose (the lowest expected effect, the active dose or the highest dose without harm in animals) according to how much is uncertain, and to dose a first person and watch before dosing the rest of a group.<Cite id="ema-2017-first-in-human-guideline" />

Some peptides sold outside pharmacies have never had a published first trial in people. For KPV, the FDA found no study in people by any route.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> For BPC-157, the developer's early human studies survive only as conference abstracts, and the FDA found the human safety information too thin to describe its safety profile.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

</InDetail>

## What an animal result means for a peptide

<Lead question="What does an animal result mean for me?">It is a reason to test it in people. It does not tell you what you will feel.</Lead>

<Compare title="Animal tests alone leave the main questions open." detail="What is known for a peptide studied mainly in animals, and for a medicine tested in large trials in people.">

| | Studied mainly in animals (BPC-157) | Tested in large trials in people (semaglutide) |
|---|---|---|
| Shown to work in people? | <Verdict is="unknown" /> No controlled trial published in full<Cite id="poolton-2026-human-sr" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> For the uses studied, in large randomised trials<Cite id="lincoff-2023-select" /> |
| Side effects in people, and how often? | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Too little human data to say<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Counted in trials and listed on the label<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy" /> |
| Rare harms known? | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Not studied: no systematic safety data in people<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Partly: some were found only after years of use<Cite id="ema-prac-2025-06-naion" /> |
| Amount safe in people known? | <Verdict is="no" /> Only conference abstracts and small reports without a comparison group<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,poolton-2026-human-sr" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Authorised doses are on the label; a prescriber decides<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy" /> |

</Compare>

If you already use a product that was tested mainly in animals, its effects and risks in people are, honestly, not known. That makes checks more useful, not less. See [monitoring](/en/safety/monitoring/), the [warning signs](/en/safety/warning-signs/) and [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

<InDetail title="Reading a peptide claim with the translation gap in mind">

When a page or a post says a peptide "heals" or "repairs", ask:

- **Which species, and which model?** A fresh injury in a young rat, treated within minutes, is the easiest case there is.<Cite id="van-der-worp-2010-animal-models" />
- **Which target, and is it the same in people?** If no receptor has been identified, nobody can check.
- **Randomised and blinded?** Without these, positive results are more likely whether or not the effect is real.<Cite id="bebarta-2003-randomisation-blinding" />
- **Repeated by an independent laboratory, and tested in people?** If not, the honest answer to "does it work?" is "unknown" (see [evidence grading](/en/evidence/evidence-grading/)).

Promising animal results deserve proper trials in people. Where that is left undone for years, users carry the uncertainty; see [the case for reform](/en/the-case-for-reform/).

</InDetail>
