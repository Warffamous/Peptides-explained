A conflict of interest is a situation in which someone could gain, in money or reputation, from a particular result. This page explains how conflicts shape what gets studied and published, how to spot them, and how much independent checking they call for. It names no sellers and no websites.

## Does it matter who paid for a study?

<Lead question="Does it matter who paid for a study?">Yes. Studies paid for by the maker more often report results and conclusions that favour the product.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Studies paid for by the maker more often found good results" value="1.27" label="times as likely to report results in favour of the product (risk ratio)" counted="Studies of drugs and devices compared by who paid for them, pooled from 25 papers in a Cochrane review of 75 (Lundh 2017)" source="lundh-2017-industry-sponsorship" />

<KeyNumber title="And more often drew conclusions in favour" value="1.34" label="times as likely to draw conclusions in favour of the product (risk ratio)" counted="Pooled from 29 papers in the same Cochrane review (Lundh 2017)" source="lundh-2017-industry-sponsorship" />

The usual checks of how a study was done did not explain the difference: company-paid studies were, if anything, more often well blinded. One clue to where the bias lies: in company-paid studies, the conclusions agreed less often with the results.

<InDetail title="What the Cochrane review found, and what it did not">

The Cochrane review *Industry sponsorship and research outcome*, in its current version, updated in 2017, included 75 papers that compared drug and device studies paid for by the manufacturer with studies paid for by others.<Cite id="lundh-2017-industry-sponsorship" />

- Industry-sponsored studies more often had favourable efficacy results (risk ratio 1.27, 95% confidence interval 1.17 to 1.37; moderate-quality evidence) and favourable conclusions (risk ratio 1.34, 1.19 to 1.51; low-quality evidence).
- They did not differ in the standard risk-of-bias items for how groups were formed, followed and reported, and they more often had a low risk of bias from blinding.
- Their conclusions agreed less often with their own results (risk ratio 0.83, from six papers).
- The authors concluded that there is "an industry bias that cannot be explained by standard 'Risk of bias' assessments".

The same pattern shows up in opinion. Of 202 articles commenting on the heart risk of the diabetes drug rosiglitazone, only about half carried a conflict-of-interest statement, and authors with a favourable view were more likely to have financial ties with its makers (rate ratio 4.29). The authors note that this does not prove the ties caused the views.<Cite id="wang-2010-rosiglitazone-coi" />

None of this means company research is worthless. Clinical research is increasingly paid for by the companies that make the products.<Cite id="lundh-2017-industry-sponsorship" /> It means a company's result needs a second, independent look before it can be taken as settled; the semaglutide example at the end of this page shows what that looks like.

</InDetail>

## How to spot a sponsored study or a seller's review

<Lead question="How can I tell who is behind a study?">Read the funding, author and conflict statements. They are usually at the end of the paper.</Lead>

<Diagram id="who-is-behind-a-study" caption="Where to look to find out who is behind a study, and what each sign means.">

| What to look for | Where to find it | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Funding statement | Near the end of the paper, or the first page | Who paid for the work |
| Author affiliations | Under the author names | Whether authors work for the maker or a seller |
| Competing interests | Near the end of the paper | Payments, shares, patents or advisory roles |
| Trial registration | In the abstract or methods; in a trial registry | The sponsor, and the outcomes planned in advance |
| Who wrote it | Acknowledgements ("writing support") | Whether a paid writer drafted the text |
| What it cites | The reference list | Whether a review relies on one group's work, or its own authors' |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="drazen-2010-icmje-coi-form,de-angelis-2004-trial-registration,ross-2008-rofecoxib-ghostwriting" />

A review written or paid for by someone who sells the product works like a sales document, even when every study it cites is real. Read it for its references, then read those studies yourself.

<InDetail title="Sponsored studies, sellers' reviews and sellers' lab reports">

**Sponsored studies.** Medical journals use a shared form on which authors declare their financial and other relationships.<Cite id="drazen-2010-icmje-coi-form" /> The trial registry entry names the sponsor and the outcomes planned in advance, so a published paper can be compared with its plan (see [how to read a study](/en/evidence/how-to-read-a-study/)).<Cite id="de-angelis-2004-trial-registration" /> A missing statement is itself information: in the rosiglitazone debate, almost half of the articles had none.<Cite id="wang-2010-rosiglitazone-coi" />

**Reviews and "research" pages.** A narrative review chooses its own studies. When the author has something to sell, three patterns are worth watching for: animal results described as if they were shown in people, positive studies cited and negative ones left out, and reviews cited as if they were new evidence (see "When reviews echo reviews" below).<Cite id="sumner-2014-press-releases,greenberg-2009-citation-distortion" /> The same applies to the "research" or "science" pages on websites that sell peptides. They may cite real studies, accurately, and still mislead by what they leave out.

**Sellers' lab reports.** A certificate of analysis supplied by a seller is also a claim by an interested party. In one study of products sold online without a prescription, the sellers' documents claimed a purity of at least 99%. Independent testing of the three vials that arrived found that semaglutide made up only about 8% to 14% of the powder, and that the amount of semaglutide was 29% to 39% above the amount on the label.<Cite id="ashraf-2024-online-semaglutide" /> What a certificate can and cannot show is explained on [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

</InDetail>

## Ghost-writers and guest authors

<Lead question="Is the named author always the one who wrote it?">Not always. Some papers are written by paid writers and signed by well-known names.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="One in five papers in top journals had a hidden or honorary author" value="21%" label="had an author who did too little to qualify, or a writer who was not named" who="Survey of 630 corresponding authors of 2008 articles in six high-impact medical journals (Wislar 2011)" source="wislar-2011-ghost-authorship" />

<Term id="ghost-writing">Ghost-writing</Term> means the real writer, often paid by a company, is not named. <Term id="guest-authorship">Guest authorship</Term> means a respected name is added who did little of the work. Both make a company's text look independent. In top medical journals, ghost authorship became less common between 1996 and 2008, but it did not go away.<Cite id="wislar-2011-ghost-authorship" />

<InDetail title="What the evidence shows">

In 2011 a survey of corresponding authors of 2008 articles in six high-impact medical journals found honorary or ghost authors, or both, in 21.0% of articles: 17.6% had an honorary author and 7.9% a ghost author, a fall from 1996 for ghost authorship.<Cite id="wislar-2011-ghost-authorship" />

Court documents from litigation over the painkiller rofecoxib showed how this can work. Company employees, alone or with medical publishing companies, prepared trial manuscripts and then recruited academic investigators as authors, often in first and second place. Marketing staff planned review articles, contracted publishing companies to write them and recruited academic authors, who were often offered payment. Of 72 related review articles, only half disclosed the company's sponsorship or the author's payment.<Cite id="ross-2008-rofecoxib-ghostwriting" />

</InDetail>

## Predatory journals

<Lead question="Is every journal a real journal?">No. Some take a fee to publish almost anything, with no real peer review.</Lead>

<Diagram id="predatory-journal-signs" caption="Signs seen far more often on predatory journal websites than on real ones.">

| Sign on the journal's website | Potential predatory journals | Legitimate open-access journals |
|---|---|---|
| Spelling errors on the homepage | 66% | 6% |
| Distorted or possibly unauthorised images | 63% | 5% |
| A made-up impact metric | 33% | 3% |
| Editors whose link to the journal could not be confirmed | 73% | 2% |
| Median publication fee | about US$100 | about US$1,865 |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="shamseer-2017-predatory-journals" />

A <Term id="predatory-journal">predatory journal</Term> asks authors for papers and charges a fee, without real peer review or editing. A paper in such a journal is not automatically wrong. But it may not have been properly checked, so it should count for less.

<InDetail title="More about predatory journals">

The comparison in the table comes from a study of 93 potential predatory journals, 99 legitimate open-access journals and 100 subscription journals, which identified 13 characteristics that may help tell them apart. Subscription journals showed even fewer of these signs, for example spelling errors on 3% of homepages, and charged a median fee of US$3,000.<Cite id="shamseer-2017-predatory-journals" /> Scholars and publishers from ten countries later agreed a shared definition of predatory publishing.<Cite id="grudniewicz-2019-predatory-definition" />

Practical checks: is the journal indexed in MEDLINE (search it on PubMed), does it name an editor you can find elsewhere, and does it describe its peer review? A paper in a legitimate journal can still be wrong; [how to read a study](/en/evidence/how-to-read-a-study/) applies to every paper.

</InDetail>

## When reviews echo reviews

<Lead question="Can a claim look strong only because it is often repeated?">Yes. A claim cited from paper to paper can read like a fact, with no new data.</Lead>

<Diagram id="citation-laundering" caption="How a claim gains weight just by being repeated, with no new evidence.">

1. **One study** reports a result, perhaps in animals, perhaps with doubts.
2. **A review** cites it, and drops the doubts.
3. **More reviews** cite the first review, not the study.
4. **Studies that disagree** are cited less, or not at all.
5. **The claim now has dozens of citations** and reads like an established fact. The evidence is still the one study.

</Diagram>

<Cite id="greenberg-2009-citation-distortion" />

We call this <Term id="citation-laundering">citation laundering</Term>. A study of one medical claim traced 242 papers and 675 citations. It found that authority was built by citing supporting papers more than critical ones, by papers that repeated the claim without data, and by turning a hypothesis into a "fact" through citation alone.<Cite id="greenberg-2009-citation-distortion" />

<InDetail title="How to check whether a claim has a real source">

- **Follow the citation back.** If a review says "BPC-157 heals tendons", find the study it cites. Is it in rats or people? From which group?
- **Count primary studies, not citations.** A claim with fifty citations may rest on one experiment.
- **Watch for reviews of the same group.** When most reviews on a compound are written by, or depend on, the group that did most of the original work, they echo it; they do not confirm it.
- **Look for the disagreements.** A fair review cites the studies that found nothing, and says why it weighs them as it does.

BPC-157 is a live example. Between 2024 and 2026, most new BPC-157 records in PubMed (34 of 52) did not list the leader of the Zagreb group that did most of the original work as an author. But more than half of those were tagged as reviews, comments, letters or editorials, and because about 85 in 100 primary studies come from Zagreb, any review of this literature rests mostly on that one group's data.<Cite id="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" /> A review that cites a study is an echo of it, not a second test (see [the Croatian lab](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/)).

</InDetail>

## Paid experts and influencers

<Lead question="Can I trust an expert or influencer who recommends a product?">Ask who pays them. Small gifts go with changes in what doctors do. Payment is often not shown.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="One sponsored meal went with more prescribing of the promoted drug" value="1.18 to 2.18" label="times the odds of prescribing the promoted brand after one sponsored meal, for four drugs" who="279,669 US doctors prescribing for Medicare in 2013; 95% of payments were meals, worth under US$20 on average (DeJong 2016)" source="dejong-2016-industry-meals" />

Companies pay experts, sometimes called key opinion leaders, to speak and advise, and pay influencers to post. In a US study, doctors who received a single company-paid meal, worth under US$20 on average, prescribed the promoted brand more often.<Cite id="dejong-2016-industry-meals" /> That is an association, not proof of cause. Influencer promotion of medicines often blurs personal stories with paid content.<Cite id="gell-2026-influencer-drug-promotion" />

<InDetail title="What studies of paid experts and influencers show">

**Payments to doctors.** Among 279,669 US doctors who prescribed in four common classes of medicine, there were 63,524 company payments linked to the four promoted brand-name drugs between August and December 2013; 95% were meals, with a mean value under US$20. Doctors who received one sponsored meal had higher odds of prescribing the promoted drug over alternatives (odds ratios from 1.18 to 2.18 for the four drugs), and more or costlier meals went with higher rates. The authors stress that this shows an association, not cause and effect.<Cite id="dejong-2016-industry-meals" /> In the United States such payments are published: the Open Payments database lists payments that drug and device companies make to doctors.<Cite id="cms-open-payments" />

**Influencers.** A preregistered review of research on prescription-drug promotion by social-media influencers, published in 2026, found three recurring problems: misinformation, because influencers often lack expertise and their audiences often have low health literacy; weak oversight and inconsistent disclosure of payment; and personal, emotional stories that blur the line between testimony and paid promotion. The evidence base was small, 12 articles.<Cite id="gell-2026-influencer-drug-promotion" />

For peptides sold outside pharmacies the same questions apply, with one more: does the person recommending a product earn from its sale, through a discount code, an affiliate link or their own shop? If so, treat what they say as advertising, however sincere it sounds.

</InDetail>

## How to check funding and conflicts yourself

<Lead question="How do I check who paid and who gains?">Use these questions. Most answers are in the paper, the trial registry or the author list.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- **Who paid?** Find the funding statement. "None" or "not stated" is information too.
- **Who are the authors?** Check their affiliations: a company, a university, a clinic that sells the treatment?
- **What do they declare?** Read the competing-interests statement: payments, shares, patents, advisory roles.
- **Was it registered?** Find the trial in a registry and compare the planned outcomes with the reported ones.
- **Who wrote it?** Look for medical-writing support in the acknowledgements.
- **Where was it published?** Is the journal indexed in MEDLINE, with named editors and real peer review?
- **What does it cite?** Primary studies, or other reviews? One group's work, or many groups'?
- **Who is telling me?** Does the person sharing it earn from the product?

</Checklist>

<Cite id="drazen-2010-icmje-coi-form,de-angelis-2004-trial-registration,shamseer-2017-predatory-journals,greenberg-2009-citation-distortion" />

You will rarely find a study with no possible conflict at all. The goal is not a clean record but a clear one: knowing who gained helps you decide how much independent confirmation to look for.

## A conflict changes how much checking you need

<Lead question="Does a conflict of interest make a result false?">No. It changes how much independent confirmation you should look for before you rely on it.</Lead>

<Diagram id="conflict-and-confirmation" caption="The more someone gains from a result, the more independent checking it needs.">

| Situation | How much independent confirmation to look for |
|---|---|
| Large, registered trials by the maker, repeated by independent groups | Little more: the core result has been checked by others |
| Large, registered trials by the maker only | Independent studies and regulators' assessments |
| Small studies by the group that holds patents on the compound | Repeats by other laboratories, blinded and randomised |
| A seller's review, "research" page or lab report | Treat as advertising; read the primary studies and independent tests |
| An influencer or expert paid by a seller | Treat as advertising, whatever the tone |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="lundh-2017-industry-sponsorship" />

Semaglutide shows how company-funded evidence gets checked: the manufacturer paid for every pivotal trial, and independent academic groups and regulators have since examined the main safety questions. They found one new, very rare harm and did not confirm two other concerns.<Cite id="hathaway-2024-naion,ema-prac-2025-06-naion,pasternak-2024-thyroid-cancer,ema-prac-2024-04-suicidal-thoughts" /> BPC-157 shows the opposite case: most of the evidence comes from one group, and other laboratories have rarely checked it.<Cite id="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" />

<InDetail title="Two examples from this site">

**Semaglutide.** Every pivotal trial was funded by the manufacturer, with many company authors. The main recent safety questions, NAION, thyroid cancer and suicidal thoughts, were examined by independent academic groups and by regulators. The European Medicines Agency's safety committee concluded that NAION is a very rare side effect, and the UK regulator has since warned about it; a large Scandinavian study of GLP-1 medicines as a group found no substantial rise in thyroid cancer over about four years; and the European committee found that the evidence does not support a causal link with suicidal thoughts.<Cite id="hathaway-2024-naion,ema-prac-2025-06-naion,mhra-2026-02-semaglutide-naion,pasternak-2024-thyroid-cancer,ema-prac-2024-04-suicidal-thoughts" /> {/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */} Company funding did not make the results false; independent checking is what lets them be relied on, and it can find what the company trials missed. Its evidence grade is "broadly replicated" for independence (see the [semaglutide page](/en/compounds/semaglutide/)).

**BPC-157.** Most of the published research comes from one group in Zagreb, and its leader is named as an inventor on a 1998 international patent application for salts of the peptide.<Cite id="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20,jozwiak-2025-review" /> That does not show wrongdoing, and we found no sign of any. It means the findings need what they mostly lack: repeats by other laboratories. Its grade for independence is "single group dominant" (see [the Croatian lab](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/)).

The same rule applies to this site. We sell nothing and take no money from anyone who makes or sells these products; how we are funded is set out on [funding](/en/about/funding/).

</InDetail>
