The Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 is the main national law on medicines in Australia. The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) runs it.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences,au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> The states and territories decide who may have a prescription medicine.<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10,au-tga-poisons-standard-page" /> This page explains what the law says about products such as BPC-157, TB-500 or "research" retatrutide. It does not tell you how to get round the law.

## What counts as a therapeutic good

<Lead question="Is a peptide a therapeutic good under Australian law?">Yes, if it is offered for a health purpose, such as changing how the body works.</Lead>

<Diagram id="au-therapeutic-good-tests" caption="Australian law has two main tests. A product that meets either one is a therapeutic good.">

| Test | The question the law asks |
|---|---|
| Disease | Is it meant for "preventing, diagnosing, curing or alleviating a disease, ailment, defect or injury"? |
| Body | Is it meant for "influencing, inhibiting or modifying a physiological process", that is, changing how the body works? |
| How it is judged | By how it is presented, or how it is likely to be taken, not only by the words on its label |

</Diagram>

These tests come from the Therapeutic Goods Act.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s3-definitions" /> The TGA says a peptide product promoted for things like weight loss, muscle growth, recovery or performance may be a therapeutic good. Some peptide products with only cosmetic uses may be cosmetics instead.<Cite id="au-tga-peptide-products-hub" /> A label saying "research use only" or "not for human use" does not change this.<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" />

<InDetail title="The ARTG: the national list of approved goods">

The Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG) is "the public database of therapeutic goods that can be legally supplied in Australia".<Cite id="au-tga-about-artg" />

- **Registered medicines** (AUST R on the label) include all prescription medicines. The TGA checks evidence of their quality, safety and effectiveness first.<Cite id="au-tg-act-artg-registration-listing,au-tga-prescription-medicines-overview" />
- **Listed medicines** (AUST L) are low-risk medicines such as vitamins. The company itself certifies that they meet the rules, and they may contain only permitted ingredients.<Cite id="au-tg-act-artg-registration-listing,au-tga-listed-medicines" />

Approved peptide medicines include insulin, semaglutide, oxytocin, desmopressin and octreotide.<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> None of the experimental peptides on this site is in the ARTG.<Cite id="au-artg-search-status-b-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-d-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-australia-legal-2026-10-24" />

</InDetail>

## The core rule: no ARTG entry, no supply

<Lead question="What exactly does the law forbid?">Bringing in, making or giving out a health product that is not on the list, without approval.</Lead>

<Diagram id="au-artg-chain" caption="Why selling a peptide that is not on the national list breaks the law in Australia.">

1. **It is a therapeutic good.** A product for a health purpose is a therapeutic good.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s3-definitions" />
2. **It is not in the ARTG.** Products that are not in the ARTG are "unapproved".<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" />
3. **No import, making or supply.** Without an exemption, approval or authority, this is an offence. "Supply" includes giving it away.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences,au-tg-act-s3-definitions" />
4. **The penalty.** Up to 5 years in prison if use of the goods is likely to cause harm. Civil penalties of up to $1.82 million for a person, and $18.2 million for a company, for each breach.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences,au-crimes-penalty-unit-2026" />

</Diagram>

The TGA says that peptide products must be in the ARTG "before they can be imported, manufactured, supplied, exported or advertised", with limited exceptions.<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> If your product did not come from a pharmacy, no regulator has checked what is in it. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

<InDetail title="Fines without a court case, and fake products">

For smaller breaches, the TGA can give an **infringement notice**: a fine that avoids a court case if it is paid. For a person it is at most 12 penalty units for each breach, which is $4,368 from 1 July 2026.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s42yk-infringement-notices,au-crimes-penalty-unit-2026" /> Dealing in counterfeit (fake) therapeutic goods carries up to 7 years in prison.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s42e-counterfeit" />

The prison terms and fines above are maximums. They say what the law allows, not what usually happens.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences" />

</InDetail>

## Schedule 4 and the Poisons Standard

<Lead question="Are these peptides prescription-only in Australia?">Yes, many of them. By law, BPC-157, TB-500 and others need a prescription.</Lead>

<Diagram id="au-poisons-standard-peptides" caption="How the Poisons Standard of October 2026 treats the peptides on this site.">

| What the Poisons Standard says | Peptides on this site |
|---|---|
| Named in Schedule 4, and on the list of medicines that may not be had without authority | BPC-157, TB-500 (and thymosin beta-4), AOD-9604, ipamorelin, hexarelin, and CJC-1295 (named, but see the note below) |
| Named in Schedule 4 only | Melanotan II, and the approved medicines semaglutide and tirzepatide |
| Not named | Tesamorelin (it may be covered by a group entry for growth hormone releasing hormones, which does not name it), retatrutide, PT-141, SS-31, thymosin alpha-1, KPV, GHK-Cu, MOTS-c, epitalon, Semax, Selank, and the NAD+ precursors NR and NMN |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10" />

The Poisons Standard sorts medicines into schedules. **Schedule 4** is the schedule of "prescription only medicines".<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10" /> The schedules take legal effect through state and territory law.<Cite id="au-tga-poisons-standard-page" /> Being in a schedule does not mean that a medicine is approved or available.<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10" />

<InDetail title="Not named does not mean legal">

A peptide that the Poisons Standard does not name is still an unapproved therapeutic good if it is not in the ARTG. The rules on import, supply and advertising apply either way.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences,au-tg-regs-reg-7-advertising-prescribed-goods" />

Retatrutide is not named. But the TGA says that GLP-1 medicines "are regulated as prescription-only medicines", and that this "includes derivatives of semaglutide or dulaglutide such as retatrutide".<Cite id="au-tga-advertising-glp1-prohibited" /> Whether a group entry covers tesamorelin is a question for our legal reviewer.<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10" />

CJC-1295 is named with a chemical registry number (a CAS number) that belongs to the form without the "DAC" part. Whether the longer-acting form with DAC is covered by that entry, or by the group entry for growth hormone releasing hormones, is also a question for our legal reviewer. Both entries are in Schedule 4.<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10" />

Many of these peptides were put into Schedule 4 because people use them to enhance performance or looks. For BPC-157, the TGA pointed to 48 referrals for importation it had received since 1 July 2022.<Cite id="au-tga-2014-09-scheduling-consultation,au-tga-2024-05-final-decisions-notice" />

</InDetail>

## Having one without a prescription

<Lead question="Is it an offence to have one without a prescription?">In some states, yes. Victoria and Queensland make it an offence for any Schedule 4 medicine.</Lead>

<Compare title="Each state makes its own rules on having a prescription medicine. Three states compared." detail="Victorian and Queensland Acts as in force on 24 October 2026; New South Wales from NSW Health's published list. Other states and territories were not checked.">

| | Victoria | Queensland | New South Wales |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is having any Schedule 4 medicine without authority an offence? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Unless it was supplied to you by a prescriber or pharmacist.<Cite id="au-vic-dpcs-act-1981,au-vic-dpcs-regs-2017" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Unless it was lawfully supplied for your own treatment.<Cite id="au-qld-medicines-poisons-act-2019" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Only for medicines on its "S4D" list, unless they were prescribed for you.<Cite id="au-nsw-ptg-act-1966,au-nsw-health-s4d" /> |
| Are TB-500, ipamorelin, hexarelin and AOD-9604 covered? | <Verdict is="yes" /> They are in Schedule 4.<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10,au-vic-dpcs-act-1981" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> They are in Schedule 4.<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10,au-qld-medicines-poisons-act-2019" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> They are on the S4D list.<Cite id="au-nsw-health-s4d,au-nsw-health-s4d-2026" /> |
| Is BPC-157 covered? | <Verdict is="yes" /> It is in Schedule 4.<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10,au-vic-dpcs-act-1981" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> It is in Schedule 4.<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10,au-qld-medicines-poisons-act-2019" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> It is not on the list NSW Health publishes, but the national list names it.<Cite id="au-nsw-health-s4d,au-poisons-standard-2026-10" /> |

</Compare>

In New South Wales, new laws apply from 5 November 2026. The S4D list that NSW Health publishes for the new laws names the same peptides.<Cite id="au-nsw-health-s4d-2026" /> We did not check the other states and territories, so check your own state or territory's law. The TGA says that, depending on the drug and your state, having a performance and image enhancing drug without a prescription "may also be illegal".<Cite id="au-tga-pieds-article" />

<InDetail title="The penalties, and what still applies everywhere">

The maximum for having a Schedule 4 medicine without authority is 10 penalty units in Victoria and 200 penalty units in Queensland. In New South Wales, it is 20 penalty units or 6 months in prison under the law in force until 4 November 2026.<Cite id="au-vic-dpcs-act-1981,au-qld-medicines-poisons-act-2019,au-nsw-ptg-act-1966" /> The value of a penalty unit differs from state to state; we did not check it.

Even where having a product is not an offence, importing or supplying an unapproved therapeutic good without approval still is, under national law.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences" />

</InDetail>

## Importing for yourself

<Lead question="Can I import a peptide for my own use?">Only under strict rules. If it needs a prescription, you need one from an Australian doctor.</Lead>

<Diagram id="au-personal-import" caption="How you may bring in a medicine for yourself: the rules from 8 September 2026.">

| Rule | What the law and the TGA say |
|---|---|
| Who it is for | You or your immediate family. You may not sell it or give it away.<Cite id="au-tg-regulations-1990-sch-5-item-1,au-tga-personal-importation-scheme" /> |
| How much | At most 3 months' supply at a time, and 15 months' supply in 12 months.<Cite id="au-tg-regulations-1990-sch-5-item-1" /> |
| The pack | It must let the medicine be "accurately identified". Unlabelled vials are not released.<Cite id="au-tg-regulations-1990-sch-5-item-1,au-tga-2026-05-peptide-import-warning" /> |
| Prescription-only medicines | You need a written authority from an Australian doctor, such as a prescription with set details. You must have it when the goods arrive. Electronic prescriptions (eScripts) are not accepted.<Cite id="au-tga-personal-importation-scheme" /> |
| If the rules are not met | The goods may be seized and destroyed. You may lose them and the money you paid.<Cite id="au-tga-personal-importation-scheme,au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> |

</Diagram>

The TGA asks the Australian Border Force (ABF) to seize and destroy unlabelled peptide vials found at the border.<Cite id="au-tga-2026-05-peptide-import-warning" /> In 2024, it fined a person in Victoria for importing a peptide, a prescription-only medicine, without approval or authority.<Cite id="au-tga-2024-04-peptide-import-infringement" />

<InDetail title="Growth hormones, and what changed in September 2026">

Growth hormones and growth hormone releasing hormones need an import permit from the Office of Drug Control.<Cite id="au-customs-prohibited-imports-regs,au-odc-growth-hormones-import-permit" /> A traveller may bring prescribed growth hormone in their luggage with a doctor's letter, up to 3 months' supply. Athletes and the people who work with them need a permit.<Cite id="au-abf-medicines-and-substances,au-customs-prohibited-imports-regs" />

Since 8 September 2026, the packaging rule is in the law, and travellers who carry a Schedule 4 medicine also need a written authority.<Cite id="au-tg-amendment-2026-measures-1,au-tga-2026-09-personal-import-packaging" />

</InDetail>

## Advertising

<Lead question="Can anyone advertise these peptides in Australia?">No. It is against the law to advertise them to the public.</Lead>

<Diagram id="au-what-is-advertising" caption="How the TGA decides whether something is an advert.">

| The TGA looks at | What it says |
|---|---|
| The content itself | Whether it "on its face appears to be designed or calculated to promote" the use or supply of a product, not what the person meant |
| Signs of promotion | Calls to act, showing only benefits, prices and links to sellers |
| A sign of information | A balanced account of good and bad findings, with no mention of where to get the product |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="au-tga-advertising-determining-content" />

An advert may not refer to a Schedule 4 substance, or to unapproved goods, when it is aimed at the public.<Cite id="au-tg-act-advertising,au-tg-regs-reg-7-advertising-prescribed-goods,au-tga-advertising-legal-framework" /> The TGA says influencers, affiliates, online sellers and health services may be held to account.<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> This site sells nothing and names no seller.

<InDetail title="The advertising code">

Adverts for therapeutic goods may not say that goods are "safe, or without harm or side-effects", or "a guaranteed cure". People who make, market or supply the goods may not give testimonials, and that includes influencers who are paid for them.<Cite id="au-advertising-code-2021" /> Compounded medicines may not be advertised to the public; since 8 September 2026 the rules say so directly.<Cite id="au-tga-compounded-medicines-guidance,au-tg-amendment-2026-measures-1" />

</InDetail>

## Pharmacy compounding

<Lead question="Can an Australian pharmacy make a peptide for me?">Only for one person, after a prescription. The TGA does not check these medicines first.</Lead>

<Diagram id="au-compounding-rules" caption="When may a pharmacy make a drug itself? The rules in Australia.">

1. **For one person.** The medicine is made for a particular person.<Cite id="au-tg-regs-sch-5-item-6-compounding" />
2. **Prescription first.** For a prescription-only medicine, the pharmacist needs the prescription and the patient's request before making it.<Cite id="au-tga-compounded-medicines-guidance" />
3. **Not in advance.** Making medicines before anyone asks is allowed only for hospital pharmacists.<Cite id="au-tg-regs-sch-5-item-6-compounding,au-tga-compounded-medicines-guidance" />
4. **No GLP-1 medicines.** Since 1 October 2024, pharmacists may not compound medicines with GLP-1 receptor agonist analogues, such as semaglutide.<Cite id="au-tg-regs-sch-5-item-6-compounding,au-tga-2024-06-glp1-compounding-update" />

</Diagram>

Compounding means a pharmacy makes a medicine itself for one patient. The TGA says: "We do not evaluate the safety, quality or efficacy of compounded medicines before they are supplied".<Cite id="au-tga-compounded-medicines-guidance" /> Compounded peptides may still be therapeutic goods, with rules on making, supply, prescribing and advertising.<Cite id="au-tga-peptides-health-practitioners" />

## Treatment routes

<Lead question="Is there a legal way to get an unapproved peptide in Australia?">Only in a few cases. A doctor must apply to the TGA and take responsibility.</Lead>

<Diagram id="au-access-routes" caption="The TGA's routes for unapproved medicines, and how they apply to the peptides on this site.">

| Route | Who uses it | For the peptides on this site |
|---|---|---|
| Special Access Scheme, Category A | A doctor, for a patient so ill that death is likely within months, or early without treatment | Possible in principle |
| Special Access Scheme, Category B | A health practitioner, who must apply to the TGA first | The TGA wants human studies for that peptide, form, route and use |
| Special Access Scheme, Category C | Listed types of health practitioner, only for goods on a TGA list | Not possible: none of these peptides is on the list |
| Authorised Prescriber | A doctor, for a group of patients, with an ethics committee's approval or a specialist college's support | Possible in principle |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="au-tga-sas-individual-patients,au-tg-regs-reg-12a-sas-category-a,au-tga-authorised-prescriber,au-tga-established-history-lists-2026-10-24,au-tga-peptides-health-practitioners" />

Only registered health practitioners can apply; patients cannot.<Cite id="au-tga-access-unapproved-goods-practitioners" /> The doctor is responsible for the product and its outcomes, and may say no.<Cite id="au-tga-access-unapproved-goods-practitioners" /> More detail: [treatment routes in Australia](/en/legal-routes/#australia-sas). See also [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

## How the law is enforced

<Lead question="Does the TGA act against peptides?">Yes. Since June 2026, unapproved peptides have been a priority for the TGA.</Lead>

<Diagram id="au-enforcement-2024-2026" caption="Some steps the TGA and others took against peptides, 2024 to 2026.">

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| April 2024 | A person in Victoria was fined for importing a peptide without approval or authority |
| March to April 2026 | Border Force and TGA officers seized over 10,000 vials of steroids, peptides and similar drugs in Melbourne. Three people were charged with importing performance and image enhancing drugs |
| May 2026 | A person in New South Wales received 27 infringement notices, $101,412 in total, for supplying melanotan II |
| June 2026 | The TGA made unapproved peptides a priority, and warned with the Chief Medical Officer of liver damage, severe allergic reactions and inflammation |
| August 2026 | With NSW Police, the TGA seized over $120,000 of peptides and steroids linked to a social media influencer |
| September 2026 | A product sold as retatrutide was found to contain semaglutide and no retatrutide. The TGA started a Federal Court case over peptide adverts |
| October 2026 | Infringement notices were given to people who imported prescription-only medicines, such as Mounjaro pens |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="au-tga-2024-04-peptide-import-infringement,au-tga-2026-04-vic-pied-arrests,au-tga-2026-05-melanotan-ii-infringement-notices,au-tga-2026-06-peptide-compliance-priority,au-tga-2026-06-cmo-peptide-statement,au-tga-2026-08-peptide-seizure,au-tga-2026-09-retatrutide-counterfeit,au-tga-court-action-list,au-tga-media-releases-peptide-log-2026-10-24,au-tga-2026-10-prescription-import-infringements" />

The TGA says its actions "may include infringement notices, product seizures, import interventions and, where appropriate, civil or criminal penalties".<Cite id="au-tga-2026-06-peptide-compliance-priority" /> In September 2026, Ahpra, the regulator of health practitioners, said it had acted against prescribers over unsafe prescribing, including of unapproved peptides.<Cite id="au-ahpra-2026-09-peptide-prescribing" />

<InDetail title="What the TGA tells people who already have a product">

- **Melanotan II tanning products:** the TGA tells people to stop using them and to take what is left to a pharmacy for disposal.<Cite id="au-tga-2026-08-melanotan-ii-safety-alert" />
- **Disposal:** performance and image enhancing drugs can be taken to a pharmacy for disposal.<Cite id="au-tga-pieds-article" />
- **Clean equipment:** needle and syringe programs give out sterile injecting equipment.<Cite id="au-tga-pieds-article" />
- **Report** side effects to the TGA: see below.<Cite id="au-tga-report-adverse-event" />

</InDetail>

## Where to report

<Lead question="Where can I report a side effect in Australia?">To the TGA. Anyone can report online.</Lead>

<JurisdictionCard code="AU" />

**Side effects.** You can report a side effect to the TGA online, or ask your doctor, nurse practitioner or pharmacist to help you report it.<Cite id="au-tga-report-adverse-event,au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers" />

**Selling and adverts.** You can also report illegal selling, fake products or adverts that break the rules to the TGA.<Cite id="au-tga-report-breach" /> Concerns about a prescriber can go to Ahpra.<Cite id="au-ahpra-2026-09-peptide-prescribing" />

**Before you report.** Keep the product, its label and the batch number. Tell your doctor what you use: see [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

## Sport

<Lead question="What if I compete in Australia?">Many unapproved peptides are banned in sport. You answer for anything found in your body.</Lead>

Sport Integrity Australia runs anti-doping in Australia.<Cite id="au-sia-act-2020" /> It says that "many unapproved peptides are prohibited in sport", and that having or using them can be an anti-doping rule violation.<Cite id="au-sia-peptides" /> Athletes "are accountable for any prohibited substance in their system, no matter the source".<Cite id="au-sia-2026-05-peptides-statement" /> A compounded medicine may not be approved by the TGA, and an exemption for medical use "is unlikely to be granted for an unregistered medicine".<Cite id="au-sia-peptides" /> See [sport and doping](/en/legal/sport-and-doping/).
