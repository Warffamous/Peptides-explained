<Lead question="What does US law say about peptides?">Most peptides sold online count as unapproved drugs. Selling them breaks federal law.</Lead>

<Compare title="US law forbids selling unapproved drugs. The July 2026 vote did not change that." detail="Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and FDA policy, checked on 22 October 2026. This page covers federal law only.">

| | Under US federal law |
|---|---|
| Is selling an unapproved new drug across state lines forbidden? | <Verdict is="yes" /> No new drug may enter trade between states until the FDA approves it.<Cite id="usc-21-355" /> |
| Can a US pharmacy legally make BPC-157 or the other peptides voted on in 2026? | <Verdict is="no" /> Pharmacies may only use allowed ingredients. The FDA's list of extra allowed ingredients has six substances, and none is a peptide.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,ecfr-21-216-23" /> |
| Did the July 2026 advisory vote change the law? | <Verdict is="no" /> It was advice. Only an FDA rule can add a substance to the list.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-503a-bulks-page" /> |
| Does a "research use only" label make selling legal? | <Verdict is="no" /> The FDA judges what the seller really intends, from the whole website.<Cite id="ecfr-21-201-128,fda-warning-letters-index" /> |
| Is importing an unapproved drug for yourself allowed? | <Verdict is="no" /> "In most circumstances" it is illegal, the FDA says. Its staff may allow some cases.<Cite id="fda-personal-importation" /> |

</Compare>

The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) is the main US law on medicines. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) enforces it. This page explains what the law says about products such as BPC-157, TB-500 or "research" retatrutide. It does not tell you how to get round the law. Nothing the FDA decides changes the law in the Netherlands or the rest of the EU.<Cite id="nl-geneesmiddelenwet" />

## What counts as a drug

<Lead question="Is a peptide a drug under US law?">Yes, if it is meant to treat a disease or to change how the body works.</Lead>

<Diagram id="us-drug-two-tests" caption="US law has two main tests. A product that meets either one is a drug.">

| Test | The question the law asks |
|---|---|
| Disease | Is it meant to diagnose, cure, ease, treat or prevent a disease? |
| Body | Is it meant to change the structure or a function of the body? |
| How intent is judged | By the seller's real intent: their claims, their adverts, the product itself and how it is sold |

</Diagram>

These tests come from the definition of "drug" in the law.<Cite id="usc-21-321" /> The FDA judges intent from everything the seller says and does, not only from the label.<Cite id="ecfr-21-201-128" /> See [research use only](/en/legal/research-use-only/) for how this works.

<InDetail title="The words of the law, and what makes a drug 'new'">

The law defines a drug as, among other things, an article "intended for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease". It also covers articles, other than food, meant "to affect the structure or any function of the body".<Cite id="usc-21-321" />

A **new drug** is a drug that experts do not generally recognise as safe and effective for the use its labelling suggests.<Cite id="usc-21-321" /> In its warning letters to peptide sellers, the FDA has said that their products are new drugs for this reason.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index" />

**Intended use** means the "objective intent" of the people responsible for the product. The FDA can show it from labelling claims, adverts, spoken or written statements, and the way the product is sold.<Cite id="ecfr-21-201-128" />

</InDetail>

## The core rule: approval before sale

<Lead question="What exactly does the law forbid?">Sending or selling a new drug across state lines before the FDA has approved it.</Lead>

<Diagram id="us-approval-chain" caption="Why a peptide with no approval, sold online, breaks US law.">

1. **It is a drug.** A product meant to treat disease or change the body is a drug.<Cite id="usc-21-321" />
2. **It is a new drug.** Experts do not generally recognise it as safe and effective.<Cite id="usc-21-321" />
3. **No approval, no trade.** Nobody may put a new drug into trade between states without an approved application.<Cite id="usc-21-355" />
4. **The penalty.** Up to one year in prison for a breach. Up to three years after an earlier conviction, or with intent to cheat or mislead.<Cite id="usc-21-333" />

</Diagram>

None of the seven peptides discussed by the FDA's advisers in July 2026 is an FDA-approved medicine. That is why they needed a place on the 503A list.<Cite id="fr-2026-07361-pcac-notice,usc-21-353a" /> For BPC-157, the FDA found no approved product in any country.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> Semax is registered as a medicine in Russia.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-semax-briefing" />

<InDetail title="What else the FDA can do">

The FDA's warning letters tell sellers what happens if they do not fix the problems. It "may lead to regulatory or legal action", for example seizure of the products and a court order (an injunction).<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index" /> The prison terms above are maximums for criminal cases. They say what the law allows, not what usually happens.<Cite id="usc-21-333" />

</InDetail>

## Pharmacy compounding: the legal route, and its limits

<Lead question="Can a US pharmacy make these peptides legally?">Only from allowed ingredients. The peptides voted on in 2026 are not on the FDA's list.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="The FDA's list of extra allowed ingredients has six substances. None is a peptide." value="6" label="substances on the 503A bulks list, the FDA's list of extra ingredients pharmacies may use" counted="21 CFR 216.23(a), unchanged since March 2019 (eCFR text as of 15 October 2026)" source="ecfr-21-216-23" />

Compounding means a pharmacy makes a medicine itself for one patient. US law allows this without FDA approval, but only under strict conditions.<Cite id="usc-21-353a" /> One condition is about the active ingredient. If it has no official quality standard and is not part of an approved drug, it must be on the FDA's 503A bulks list.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,fda-503a-bulks-page" />

<Diagram id="us-compounding-routes" caption="The two kinds of compounding in US law, and the ingredients each may use.">

| | Pharmacy (section 503A) | Outsourcing facility (section 503B) |
|---|---|---|
| Who | A licensed pharmacist or doctor | A facility that makes sterile drugs and has registered with the FDA |
| For whom | One identified patient, on a valid prescription | With or without a prescription for a named patient |
| Which raw ingredients | One with an official quality standard, one that is part of an approved drug, or one on the 503A list | One on the 503B list of clinical need, or one for a drug on the shortage list |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="usc-21-353a,usc-21-353b" />

<InDetail title="The FDA's interim categories, and where the peptides stand now">

While it builds the 503A list, the FDA sorts nominated ingredients into three interim categories:<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" />

- **Category 1:** nominated with enough information to evaluate. The FDA does not intend to take action against pharmacies that compound with these, if conditions are met.
- **Category 2:** the FDA has found possible significant safety risks.
- **Category 3:** nominated without enough supporting information.

The FDA's page on safety risks lists BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, CJC-1295, AOD-9604, Semax, Selank, epitalon, emideltide, melanotan II, thymosin alpha-1 and injectable GHK-Cu as "previously in category 2". Their nominations were withdrawn. For most, the FDA names a risk of unwanted immune reactions, impurities, and little or no safety information.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" /> Ipamorelin acetate is still in category 2 for outsourcing facilities, since 29 September 2023.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks" />

On the FDA's list of 14 May 2026, none of these peptides is in category 1. The only exception is GHK-Cu for routes other than injection.<Cite id="fda-503a-categories-2026-05" /> The FDA also says it does not intend to sort ingredients nominated on or after 7 January 2025 into these categories at all.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="A second advisory meeting is planned">

The FDA says it will hold another advisory meeting "before the end of February 2027". It will discuss LL-37, GHK-Cu, dihexa acetate, melanotan II and pegylated mechano growth factor (PEG-MGF). The date, place and public docket are still to come.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2027-early-announcement" />

</InDetail>

## The July 2026 advisory vote

<Lead question="What did the FDA's advisers decide in July 2026?">They backed six of seven peptides for pharmacy compounding. It was only advice.</Lead>

<Diagram id="us-pcac-2026-outcomes" caption="The seven peptides the FDA's advisers looked at in July 2026, and what they said.">

| Peptide | Use the FDA reviewed | FDA staff proposal | Committee outcome, as reported |
|---|---|---|---|
| BPC-157 | Ulcerative colitis | Do not add | Recommended (8 to 6, 1 abstention) |
| KPV | Wound healing, inflammation | Do not add | Recommended |
| TB-500 | Wound healing | Do not add | Recommended |
| MOTS-c | Obesity, osteoporosis | Do not add | Recommended |
| Emideltide (DSIP) | Opioid withdrawal, insomnia, narcolepsy | Do not add | Not recommended |
| Semax | Poor blood flow to the brain, migraine, face nerve pain | Do not add | Recommended |
| Epitalon | Insomnia | Do not add | Recommended |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="fr-2026-07361-pcac-notice,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro,fda-pcac-2026-questions" />

On 23 July 2026, the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) voted 8–6, with one abstention, to recommend BPC-157 for the 503A list. It considered seven peptides and recommended six. These results are as reported from the public meeting: the FDA has posted no minutes.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-questions" /> The vote is advisory and non-binding. It went against FDA staff's own proposal.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" />

<InDetail title="The five points to keep in mind about this vote">

1. **The vote.** On 23 July 2026 the committee voted 8–6, with one abstention, to recommend BPC-157 for the 503A bulks list (count as reported from the public meeting).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-questions" />
2. **Seven considered, six recommended.** FDA documents show seven peptides on the agenda, each voted on separately as a free base and as an acetate salt: 14 questions in all.<Cite id="fr-2026-07361-pcac-notice,fda-pcac-2026-questions" /> The six recommendations, and emideltide's rejection, are as reported from the meeting. We do not print the other vote counts: the FDA has not published them, and reports differ.
3. **Only advice, against FDA staff.** Advisory committees give the FDA "non-binding recommendations".<Cite id="fda-pcac-2027-early-announcement" /> For all 14 forms, FDA staff had proposed that they should not be added.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" />
4. **Still prohibited for now.** Compounding with these peptides stays unlawful until the FDA adopts the advice and completes rulemaking. The FDA adds substances to the list through notice-and-comment rulemaking.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page,usc-21-353a" />
5. **Not an approval.** None of these is an approved medicine in the EU, the UK or the US (Semax is registered as a medicine in Russia).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-semax-briefing,ema-medicines-search" /> A listing would let US pharmacies compound them on prescription. It would not be an approval. It would say nothing about whether they work, and it would change nothing in the EU or the Netherlands.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1,ecfr-21-216-23,nl-geneesmiddelenwet" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="What a listing would mean, and what happens next">

The FDA told the committee what changes when an ingredient goes on the 503A list. The compounded product is still not FDA-approved. It needs no label telling patients how to use it. It does not have to meet the manufacturing quality rules (CGMP), although dirty conditions are still banned. The pharmacy does not have to report side effects to the FDA. Unless the listing sets limits, any use and any route is allowed.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1" /> Each salt form counts as a separate ingredient.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1" />

The FDA says it will not decide until it has considered the committee's input and finished its reviews.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" /> To add a substance, it must propose a rule, take public comments and publish a final rule.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" /> The last proposed rule on the 503A list dates from September 2019.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" /> On 22 October 2026 we found no new rule, and no notice of the next committee meeting, in the Federal Register.<Cite id="federalregister-fda-search-2026-10-22" /> We follow this on [what's changing](/en/legal/whats-changing/).

</InDetail>

## "Research use only" sellers and FDA warning letters

<Lead question="Does a 'research use only' label make selling legal?">No. The FDA looks at the whole website and calls such products unapproved new drugs.</Lead>

<Diagram id="us-warning-letter-logic" caption="What the FDA's warning letters to online peptide sellers say.">

1. **The label is not enough.** The sellers wrote "Research Use Only" and "not intended for human consumption".<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />
2. **The website decides.** Claims on the sellers' own websites showed the products were meant as drugs for people.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index,ecfr-21-201-128" />
3. **So they are unapproved new drugs.** Selling them across state lines without approval breaks the law.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index,usc-21-355" />
4. **The water counts too.** Bacteriostatic water sold for mixing with the peptides is itself a drug, the FDA said.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index" />

</Diagram>

Letters from March and August 2026 named semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide, SS-31, tesamorelin and PT-141.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index" /> The FDA also warns that products falsely labelled "for research purposes" are sold to people with dosing instructions, and are of unknown quality.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" /> If your product did not come from a pharmacy, no regulator has checked what is in it. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

During a shortage, US law allows more compounding. Semaglutide and tirzepatide are no longer on the FDA's shortage list or the 503B list.<Cite id="fda-glp1-compounding-policy" /> On 30 April 2026, the FDA proposed to keep them off the 503B list, because it found no clinical need.<Cite id="fda-2026-503b-glp1-exclusion-proposal,fr-2026-08552-503b-glp1" />

<KeyNumber title="The FDA has had close to a thousand reports of side effects from semaglutide made in pharmacies." value="990" label="reports of side effects from versions made by pharmacies, up to the end of May 2026" counted="Reports to the FDA about compounded semaglutide up to 31 May 2026; for compounded tirzepatide, more than 730. Pharmacies that are not outsourcing facilities do not have to report, so the real numbers are likely higher." source="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />

<InDetail title="Salt forms, copies and fakes">

- **Copies.** A pharmacy may not compound, regularly or in large amounts, drugs that are "essentially copies" of a medicine on the market.<Cite id="usc-21-353a" />
- **Salt forms.** Some compounders used semaglutide sodium or semaglutide acetate. These are different active ingredients from the one in the approved medicines. The FDA says it knows of no lawful basis for using them in compounding.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />
- **Fakes.** The FDA knows of counterfeit Ozempic sold in the US. Fakes can contain the wrong ingredient, too much, too little or none.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />
- **Reports.** Many reported side effects match those of the approved medicines. Some were linked to dosing mistakes with compounded products.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />

</InDetail>

## Bringing drugs into the US for yourself

<Lead question="Can I import a peptide into the US for my own use?">In most cases it is illegal. FDA staff may allow some cases, but it is not a right.</Lead>

<Diagram id="us-personal-import" caption="What the FDA says about importing a drug for your own use.">

| | What the FDA and the law say |
|---|---|
| The general rule | Importing drugs for personal use is illegal in most circumstances. A drug approved abroad but not in the US is illegal to import.<Cite id="fda-personal-importation" /> |
| When staff may be more lenient | In narrow cases, such as a serious condition with no treatment available in the US, under further conditions.<Cite id="fda-personal-importation" /> |
| When the FDA refuses | The product is on an import alert, looks meant for sale, seems a serious health risk, or seems to be health fraud.<Cite id="fda-personal-importation" /> |
| At the border | A drug that appears to break the approval rule "shall be refused admission". If it is worth $2,500 or less (or a higher limit set by regulation), it may be destroyed, after notice.<Cite id="usc-21-381" /> |

</Diagram>

The FDA says it "cannot ensure the safety and effectiveness" of medicines bought from foreign sources over the internet.<Cite id="fda-personal-importation" />

We found no FDA figures on how often small parcels of peptides are refused or destroyed. Our legal reviewer will check how the policy works in practice.
</Callout>

## Where to report

<Lead question="Where can I report a side effect in the US?">To MedWatch, the FDA's reporting system. Patients can report too, not only doctors.</Lead>

<JurisdictionCard code="US" />

**Side effects.** MedWatch is the FDA's safety reporting programme "for health professionals, patients and consumers".<Cite id="fda-medwatch" /> The FDA asks patients and health workers to report side effects and quality problems with compounded GLP-1 products, and with any other medicine.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />

**Before you report.** Keep the vial, its label and any papers. Write down the batch number. Tell your doctor what you use: see [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).
