The Food and Drugs Act is the main federal law on medicines in Canada, and Health Canada applies it.<Cite id="ca-food-and-drugs-act,ca-hc-complaints" /> This page explains what the law says about products such as BPC-157, TB-500 or "research" retatrutide. Health Canada has not authorised (approved) them, and it has not assessed their safety, effectiveness or quality.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> This page does not tell you how to get round the law.

## What counts as a drug

<Lead question="Is a peptide a drug under Canadian law?">Yes, if it is sold or presented to treat a disease or to change how the body works.</Lead>

<Diagram id="ca-drug-two-tests" caption="Canadian law has two main tests. A product that meets either one is a drug.">

| Test | The question the law asks |
|---|---|
| Disease | Is it made, sold or presented for treating, easing or preventing a disease or its symptoms? |
| Body | Is it made, sold or presented for "restoring, correcting or modifying organic functions", that is, for changing how the body works? |
| How it is judged | By what the product is made, sold or presented for, not only by the words on its label |

</Diagram>

These tests come from the definition of "drug" in the Food and Drugs Act. In this law, "sell" also covers offering, keeping for sale and giving away for free.<Cite id="ca-fda-s2-definitions" /> See [research use only](/en/legal/research-use-only/) for why a "research" label does not change what a product is.

<InDetail title="New drugs, the notice of compliance and the DIN">

A **new drug** is a drug that has not been sold in Canada long enough, and in large enough amounts, to show how safe it is and that it works.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c08-001-new-drug" /> No one may sell or advertise a new drug until Health Canada has issued a **notice of compliance** (NOC).<Cite id="ca-food-and-drug-regulations" /> To get one, a company must send safety reports and "substantial evidence of the clinical effectiveness" of the drug.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c08-004-notice-of-compliance" />

A maker may not sell a drug in dosage form unless it has a **Drug Identification Number** (DIN).<Cite id="ca-food-and-drug-regulations" /> Health Canada says that authorised prescription drugs have an eight-digit DIN on the label, and that you can look the number up in its Drug Product Database.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Prescription drugs, and what Health Canada says about peptides">

Health Canada keeps a **Prescription Drug List**. A medicine that contains an ingredient on the list is a prescription drug, unless the list says otherwise. Prescription drugs can only be sold to the public by pharmacists, on a prescription.<Cite id="ca-fda-s29-1-prescription-drug-list,ca-hc-pdl-overview" />

The list names peptide medicines one by one, such as semaglutide, tirzepatide and tesamorelin. It names none of the experimental peptides on this site, and it has no general entry for peptides or for injections.<Cite id="ca-pdl-human" />

Health Canada says that "peptides are generally regulated as prescription drugs" in Canada.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> We could not find the rule in the law that makes a peptide that is not on the list a prescription drug. So we report this as Health Canada's position, and our legal reviewer will check it. Either way, selling such a peptide is against the new-drug rules above.<Cite id="ca-food-and-drug-regulations,ca-fdr-c08-001-new-drug" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Natural health products and skin creams">

Some health products are sold as **natural health products**. Each one needs its own product licence.<Cite id="ca-nhpr-definition" /> Nothing that is given by puncturing the skin can be a natural health product.<Cite id="ca-nhpr-schedule-2" /> So injected NAD+ cannot be one, although Health Canada has a natural health product monograph for oral NMN, an NAD+ precursor.<Cite id="ca-nhpr-schedule-2,ca-nhpid-nmn-monograph" />

A skin cream sold only for how the skin looks can be a cosmetic. Health Canada says that products given by injection are not cosmetics, and that products with health claims are assessed as drugs.<Cite id="ca-hc-cosmetic-drug-interface-guidance" />

</InDetail>

## The core rule: authorisation before sale

<Lead question="What exactly does the law forbid?">Selling or advertising a new drug before it has been approved.</Lead>

<Diagram id="ca-authorisation-chain" caption="Why selling a peptide with no approval breaks the law in Canada.">

1. **It is a drug.** A product sold to treat disease or to change how the body works is a drug.<Cite id="ca-fda-s2-definitions" />
2. **It is a new drug.** It has not been sold in Canada long enough to show how safe it is and that it works.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c08-001-new-drug" />
3. **No notice of compliance, no sale.** No one may sell or advertise a new drug without one.<Cite id="ca-food-and-drug-regulations" />
4. **The penalty.** For drugs, on indictment (the more serious kind of trial): a fine of up to $5,000,000, up to two years in prison, or both. Up to five years for knowingly or recklessly causing a serious risk to health.<Cite id="ca-fda-s31-2-therapeutic-products-penalty,ca-fda-s31-4-serious-risk-penalty" />

</Diagram>

Health Canada says that unauthorised drugs "are illegal in Canada" and have not been assessed for safety, efficacy and quality.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> When we checked in October 2026, no product with BPC-157 or any other experimental peptide on this site was in its Drug Product Database.<Cite id="ca-dpd-search-canada-legal-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-b-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-extract-search-merge-2026-10-24" /> Some vials it seized carried false DINs.<Cite id="ca-hc-recalls-database" />

<InDetail title="What else Health Canada can do">

Health Canada can order a recall of a drug that presents "a serious or imminent risk of injury to health".<Cite id="ca-fda-s21-3-recalls" /> It can order a person to take the steps it thinks are needed to stop a breach of the law.<Cite id="ca-fda-s27-3-measures" /> It can ask a court for an injunction, a court ruling that tells a person to stop.<Cite id="ca-fda-s21-5-injunction" /> Its inspectors can take samples, and seize and hold products.<Cite id="ca-fda-s23-inspection-seizure" /> The government can recover the costs of seizure, storage and disposal.<Cite id="ca-fda-s30-7-cost-recovery" />

The fines and prison terms above are maximums. They say what the law allows, not what usually happens.<Cite id="ca-fda-s31-2-therapeutic-products-penalty" /> We found no criminal case against a peptide seller in Canada, but we did not search court records.

</InDetail>

## "Research use only" sellers and Health Canada's actions

<Lead question="Does a 'research use only' label make selling legal?">No. Health Canada says the label does not make these products legal.</Lead>

<Diagram id="ca-enforcement-2023-2026" caption="What Health Canada did about peptide shots from 2023 to 2026.">

| When | What Health Canada did |
|---|---|
| December 2023 | Warned the public about injectable products "promoted as peptides" sold online, and told the seller to stop |
| June 2024 | Seized injectable peptides from a company in Québec. Its websites were taken down |
| April 2025 | Seized 26 injectable peptide products from a wellness clinic in Calgary, and peptides from an online seller in Québec |
| August 2025 | Seized 42 injectable peptide products from an online seller |
| December 2025 | Seized peptides from a fitness business in Vancouver and from two shops in British Columbia. Some vials had false DINs |
| April 2026 | Published a public advisory: "Think twice before injecting peptides bought online" |
| May 2026 | A Type I recall, the most serious kind, of BPC-157 and GHK-Cu powder sold to pharmacies |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="ca-hc-recalls-database,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,ca-hc-gui-0039-recall-types" />

Many injectable peptides carry the words "For Research Use Only – Not for Human Consumption". Health Canada says this "does not make these products legal".<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> It works with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) to stop such shipments.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> If your product did not come from a pharmacy, no regulator has checked what is in it. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

<InDetail title="What Health Canada tells people who already have a product">

- **If you feel unwell** after using one, talk to a health professional straight away.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" />
- **Unused products** can go back to a pharmacy for disposal, or follow your town's rules for hazardous waste.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,ca-hc-recalls-database" />
- **Check the number.** An authorised drug has a DIN, which you can look up in the Drug Product Database.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" />
- **The risks Health Canada names:** too much, too little or none of the active ingredient; ingredients not on the label; contamination, for example with bacteria, fungi or endotoxins; poor labelling; and, for injections, infection and allergic reactions.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,ca-hc-2026-01-glp1-advisory" />
- **Report** side effects to Health Canada: see below.

</InDetail>

## Pharmacy compounding

<Lead question="Can a Canadian pharmacy make these peptides?">Not as a way round the law. In 2026, Health Canada took BPC-157 powder back from pharmacies.</Lead>

<Diagram id="ca-compounding-policy" caption="When may a pharmacy make a drug itself? Health Canada's rules.">

1. **The provinces regulate it.** Each province or territory oversees compounding by its own pharmacists.<Cite id="ca-hc-pol-0051-compounding" />
2. **Not a way round approval.** Compounding "must not be used as a means to bypass the federal drug review and approval system".<Cite id="ca-hc-pol-0051-compounding" />
3. **For a real need.** There must be a patient and a health professional, and a medical need or no product available.<Cite id="ca-hc-pol-0051-compounding" />
4. **From accepted ingredients.** The product should be made from an ingredient of an authorised drug, or one listed in a recognised pharmacopoeia.<Cite id="ca-hc-pol-0051-compounding" />

</Diagram>

Compounding means that a pharmacy makes a medicine itself for one patient. In May 2026, Health Canada recalled GHK-Cu and BPC-157 powder sold to compounding and community pharmacies, as "unauthorized active pharmaceutical ingredients". It was a Type I recall: Health Canada uses that class when there is a "reasonable probability" of serious harm or death.<Cite id="ca-hc-recalls-database,ca-hc-gui-0039-recall-types" />

<InDetail title="Compounded GLP-1 medicines, and a warning to doctors">

In June 2025, Health Canada told the provinces that compounding injectable GLP-1 medicines when there is no shortage "is not permitted". It called sterile compounding "a high-risk activity".<Cite id="ca-hc-2025-glp1-compounding-position" /> In January 2025, it recalled a compounded semaglutide product made with an unauthorised ingredient (Type I).<Cite id="ca-hc-recalls-database" /> By 2026, Health Canada had also authorised generic semaglutide medicines, each with its own DIN.<Cite id="ca-dpd-search-canada-legal-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-extract-search-merge-2026-10-24" />

In April 2026, Alberta's College of Physicians and Surgeons told doctors that injectable peptides "are regulated as prescription medications in Canada". It asked them to ask patients about injections that were not prescribed, and to report side effects to Health Canada.<Cite id="ca-cpsa-2026-04-injectable-peptides" />

</InDetail>

## Bringing products into Canada

<Lead question="Can I import a peptide into Canada for my own use?">Not if it is a prescription drug. Border officers can hold a parcel and pass it on.</Lead>

<Diagram id="ca-personal-import" caption="What the rules say about bringing a drug into Canada for yourself.">

| | What the rules say |
|---|---|
| The general rule | "Generally, residents of Canada are not allowed to bring prescription drugs into Canada", whether they carry them in or have them sent by mail or courier.<Cite id="ca-hc-gui-0116-personal-import" /> |
| By mail or courier | "Canadian residents may not import prescription drugs by mail or courier."<Cite id="ca-cbsa-d19-9-1" /> |
| How much counts as personal use | At most a 90-day supply or one course of treatment, whichever is less. Giving or selling it to others makes you a commercial importer, even without money.<Cite id="ca-hc-gui-0116-personal-import" /> |
| At the border | The CBSA can hold goods and refer them to Health Canada. Health Canada can refuse, seize or test them, or order them sent back or destroyed, at your cost.<Cite id="ca-cbsa-d19-9-1,ca-customs-act-s101,ca-fda-s27-2-removal-destruction,ca-fda-s30-7-cost-recovery" /> |

</Diagram>

Health Canada says it works with the CBSA to stop unauthorised peptide shipments.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> When it refuses or seizes a product, it "is not responsible for any consequences including financial loss".<Cite id="ca-hc-gui-0116-personal-import" />

<Callout type="uncertain" title="What we could not find">
The guidance we read does not say how these rules apply when a person imports an unauthorised peptide that is not on the Prescription Drug List, such as BPC-157, for their own use. None of these peptides is a controlled substance.<Cite id="ca-cdsa" /> We also found no figures on how many peptide parcels are stopped. Our legal reviewer will check these points.
</Callout>

## Advertising

<Lead question="Can anyone advertise these products in Canada?">No. A new drug may not be advertised before it is authorised, and many health claims are banned.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="The law lists 29 conditions. No product may be advertised to the public as a cure for them." value="29" label="conditions on a list in the law. Obesity, cancer and diabetes are on it." counted="Schedule A.1 of the Food and Drugs Act, counted in the full text on 23 October 2026" source="ca-fda-schedule-a1" />

No one may sell or advertise a new drug without a notice of compliance.<Cite id="ca-food-and-drug-regulations" /> No one may advertise a product to the public as a treatment or cure for a condition on the Schedule A.1 list.<Cite id="ca-fda-s3-advertising,ca-fda-schedule-a1" /> An advert for a prescription drug to the public may only give its name, price and quantity.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c01-044-rx-advertising" /> False or misleading claims about a drug's safety or value are banned too.<Cite id="ca-fda-s9-deception" />

<InDetail title="Why this page says 'not authorised' so often">

Health Canada's guidance says that even an educational message about an unauthorised product can count as promotion. One example is a message that does not say that Health Canada has not authorised the product and that its safety and effectiveness are still being studied.<Cite id="ca-hc-advertising-distinction-2023" /> So we say it plainly: Health Canada has not authorised the experimental peptides on this site, and it has not assessed their safety or effectiveness.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> This site sells nothing and names no seller.

</InDetail>

## Where to report

<Lead question="Where can I report a side effect in Canada?">To Health Canada. Anyone can report, online or by mail.</Lead>

<JurisdictionCard code="CA" />

**Side effects.** Health Canada says that "anyone can report a side effect", even if you are not sure that the product caused it. You can report online, by mail, or with your doctor, nurse or pharmacist.<Cite id="ca-hc-medeffect-reporting" /> The reports go to the Canada Vigilance Program.<Cite id="ca-hc-canada-vigilance" />

**Selling and adverts.** You can also report unauthorised sales or misleading adverts to Health Canada.<Cite id="ca-hc-complaints" />

**Before you report.** Keep the vial, its label and the batch number. Tell your doctor what you use: see [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

## Sport

<Lead question="What if I compete in Canada?">BPC-157 and many other peptides are banned in sport at all times.</Lead>

Sport Integrity Canada, formerly the Canadian Centre for Ethics in Sport (CCES), runs the Canadian Anti-Doping Program.<Cite id="ca-sic-our-brand,ca-sic-cadp" /> It says that the use, trafficking or possession of BPC-157 and other non-approved peptides "could result in an anti-doping rule violation".<Cite id="ca-sic-2026-04-peptides-reminder,wada-2026-list-en" /> In 2025, a university volleyball player received a four-year ban for using BPC-157 and TB-500.<Cite id="ca-sic-2025-04-bpc157-tb500-sanction" /> See [sport and doping](/en/legal/sport-and-doping/).
