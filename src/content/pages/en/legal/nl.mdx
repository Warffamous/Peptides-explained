<Lead question="What does Dutch law say about peptides?">Many peptides sold online likely count as medicines with no licence. Trading in them is against the law.</Lead>

<Compare title="The law forbids trading in medicines without a licence. It does not name using one." detail="Geneesmiddelenwet, articles 40 and 84, in the version in force on 22 October 2026.">

| | Under the Geneesmiddelenwet |
|---|---|
| Is selling it, or offering it for sale, forbidden? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Forbidden for a medicine without a licence.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> |
| Is keeping a stock of it forbidden? | <Verdict is="yes" /> "In voorraad hebben" is on the list of forbidden acts.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> |

The Geneesmiddelenwet is the Dutch Medicines Act. This page explains what it says about products such as BPC-157, TB-500 or "research" semaglutide. It does not tell you how to get round the law. For the legal ways to get a treatment, see [legal routes](/en/legal-routes/).

## What counts as a medicine

<Lead question="Is a peptide a medicine under Dutch law?">Often, yes. The law looks at how it is sold and what it does, not just the label.</Lead>

<Diagram id="nl-medicine-two-tests" caption="Dutch law has two tests. A product that meets either one is a medicine.">

| Test | The question the law asks | What counts as evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Presentation | Is the product offered as something that cures or prevents disease, a wound or pain in people? | Claims, pictures, the website and the form of the product, not only the label |
| Function | Is it meant to restore, improve or change how the body works through a drug-like effect? | What the substance does in the body, as far as science can show |

</Diagram>

The Dutch definition comes from EU law, so the same two tests apply across the EU.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-1-geneesmiddel,eu-directive-2001-83" /> A label saying "research use only" does not decide the question. Who decides in a real case? First the inspectorate, and in the end a court.<Cite id="cjeu-2014-d-and-g" />

<InDetail title="The words of article 1, and where the tests come from">

Article 1 of the Geneesmiddelenwet says a medicine is a substance meant to be given, or presented as suitable, for one of three things:<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-1-geneesmiddel" />

1. curing or preventing a disease, defect, wound or pain in people;
2. making a medical diagnosis in people;
3. restoring, improving or otherwise changing body functions in people, through a pharmacological, immunological or metabolic effect.

The EU Court of Justice has explained both tests. "Presentation" is read broadly. It covers what a label says, but also the impression an ordinary, well-informed person gets, even from what is only implied.<Cite id="cjeu-1983-van-bennekom" /> For "function", the substance must be able to change body functions in a way that really matters. Authorities decide case by case. They look at what the product contains and what it does, as far as science can show. They also look at how it is used and at its risks.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-hecht-pharma,cjeu-2014-d-and-g" />

For a peptide tested only in animals, the "function" test can be an open legal question. A product sold with healing or weight-loss claims usually meets the "presentation" test anyway.<Cite id="cjeu-1983-van-bennekom" /> See [research use only](/en/legal/research-use-only/) for how this works.

</InDetail>

## The core rule: no licence, no trade

<Lead question="What exactly does the law forbid?">Trade in a medicine with no licence: selling it, passing it on, keeping stock, or bringing it in.</Lead>

<Diagram id="nl-article-40-chain" caption="The ban covers each step from maker to buyer for a medicine with no licence.">

1. **No licence.** A medicine may only be put on the market with a licence from the EU or from the CBG, the Dutch medicines board.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" />
2. **No trade.** Without that licence, nobody may keep it in stock, offer it for sale, sell it, supply it or hand it over. Nobody may import it, export it, or bring it into or out of the Netherlands in another way.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" />
3. **Narrow exceptions.** A pharmacy preparation, a medicine in a clinical trial, a named-patient exemption from the IGJ and a compassionate use programme are the main ones.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> See [legal routes](/en/legal-routes/).

</Diagram>

Article 40 has no exception for "own use". A separate article lets travellers carry medicines clearly meant for their own use without an import licence.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning,nl-gnw-art-18-licence" />

<InDetail title="Licences to make, import and sell in bulk (article 18)">

Making, importing or running a wholesale business in medicines needs a licence from the Minister of Health. A wholesaler may not trade at all in medicines that have no marketing authorisation.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-18-licence" />

Two exceptions matter here. A pharmacy may make medicines on a small scale, to hand over from its own pharmacy, without this licence. And the licence rule does not apply to medicines "clearly meant for the own use of the person who carries them". That exception is about the licence for importers. It does not switch off the ban in article 40.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-18-licence,nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Prescriptions and online prescribing">

Prescription-only medicines may only be handed over by pharmacists, dispensing GPs and a few other named people, and only against a prescription.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-61-prescription" />

No one may prescribe medicines over the internet to someone they have never met in person. The same goes for someone they do not know, or whose medicine history they cannot see.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-67-internet-prescribing" /> Selling medicines at a distance is only allowed under the rules that put EU law into effect.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-67a-distance-selling" />

</InDetail>

## Advertising, and why this site sells nothing

<Lead question="Can anyone advertise these products?">No. Ads for a medicine with no licence are banned, and so are public ads for prescription medicines.</Lead>

<Diagram id="advertising-purpose-test" caption="Information becomes an advert when its aim is to make people buy or use a medicine.">

1. **The law defines advertising by its purpose.** Any influence with the clear aim of making people prescribe, supply or use a medicine counts.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-1-reclame" />
2. **The writer does not need to earn money from it.** The EU Court of Justice ruled that an independent writer can be advertising too.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-damgaard" />
3. **In that case, the writer had said where the product was on sale.** The court noted that such information can lead people to get the product.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-damgaard" />
4. **So this site never says where to get a product.** No sellers, no links to shops, no prices, no doses. It explains effects, risks and the law.

</Diagram>

Advertising for a medicine without a licence is forbidden. Public advertising for prescription-only medicines is forbidden too.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-84-85-advertising" />

<InDetail title="The Damgaard case: an independent writer and an unlicensed product">

A Danish journalist wrote on his website about a product that had no licence in Denmark. He wrote that it was meant to ease pain from gout, and that it was on sale in Sweden and Norway. He said he did not work for the maker and had no stake in its sales. He was not paid by the maker for what he wrote. He was prosecuted for advertising.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-damgaard" />

In 2009 the EU Court of Justice ruled that information from an independent third party can still be advertising. What matters is the purpose of the message. The writer's link with the company is one factor, weighed together with the kind of activity and the content of the message. The national court then decides.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-damgaard" />

This is why this site never says where a product can be found, and never judges or recommends a seller.

</InDetail>

## Medicines from the internet

<Lead question="Can I legally get these peptides online in the Netherlands?">No legal seller may sell you a medicine with no licence. Legal online pharmacies show an EU logo.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Reports about illegal web shops are going up" value="114" label="reports to the IGJ in 2024 about web shops selling drugs that need a prescription, without one" counted="Reports received by the Dutch Health and Youth Care Inspectorate (IGJ) in 2024; not about peptides in particular" source="igj-2025-online-nepapotheken" />

<KeyNumber title="Already more in 2025, before the year was over" value="187" label="reports in 2025, up to the start of October" counted="Reports received by the IGJ from January to early October 2025 about the same kind of webshop" source="igj-2025-online-nepapotheken" />

Legal online sellers of medicines must show the EU logo. Clicking it should lead to a government list that names the seller and what it may sell.<Cite id="rijksoverheid-online-aanbieder-controleren" /> That list covers medicines that may legally be sold in the Netherlands. A peptide without a licence is not one of them.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" />

<InDetail title="What the IGJ says about medicines bought online">

The IGJ advises against ordering medicines online. Nobody checks them as a doctor or pharmacist would. A product can contain too much active substance, too little, or none. A contaminated product can damage organs. You can also be cheated: you pay and get nothing.<Cite id="igj-online-medicijnen" />

If your product did not come from a pharmacy, no regulator has checked what is in it. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/) for what you can and cannot check yourself.

</InDetail>

## Who enforces the law

<Lead question="Who enforces these rules, and how?">Mainly the health inspectorate (IGJ). It can warn, fine, order a stop and seize products.</Lead>

<Diagram id="nl-enforcement-map" caption="Who does what when the law on medicines is broken in the Netherlands.">

| Who | What they do | What they can do |
|---|---|---|
| IGJ, the health inspectorate | Checks that the Medicines Act is followed, including by websites aimed at Dutch buyers<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-100-supervision,igj-toezicht-online-medicijnen" /> | Warnings, administrative fines, orders to stop, seizure of products<Cite id="igj-toezicht-online-medicijnen,nl-gnw-art-101-fines,nl-gnw-art-115-seizure" /> |
| NVWA, the food and product safety authority | Shares supervision of the Medicines Act; looks mainly at veterinary medicines<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-100-supervision,igj-illegale-handel" /> | The same supervisory powers |
| Customs (Douane) | Checks imports and exports, with the IGJ<Cite id="igj-illegale-handel" /> | Stops parcels; contents can be seized and destroyed<Cite id="igj-2025-shield-seizures" /> |
| Public Prosecution Service (OM) | Tackles illegal trade under criminal law<Cite id="igj-illegale-handel" /> | Prosecution under the Economic Offences Act<Cite id="nl-wed-art-1-2-6" /> |

</Diagram>

<InDetail title="How high the penalties can be">

**Administrative fines.** The Minister, in practice the IGJ, can fine breaches of the Medicines Act without going to court. This covers trading without a licence (article 40) and advertising (articles 84 and 85).<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-101-fines,igj-toezicht-online-medicijnen" /> The maximum is the highest fine category in Dutch law, €1,100,000 since 1 January 2026.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-101-fines,rijksoverheid-fine-categories-2026" />

**Criminal law.** A breach of article 40 is also an economic offence. Done on purpose, it is a crime. The maximum is six years in prison, community service, or a fine of up to €110,000. If the goods are worth a lot, the fine can be one category higher.<Cite id="nl-wed-art-1-2-6,rijksoverheid-fine-categories-2026" />

**Seizure.** IGJ and NVWA officials may seize medicines at any time to prevent harm to public health. They can also order a seller to stop, or to take a product off the market.<Cite id="nl-gnw-art-115-seizure" />

These are maximums. They say what the law allows, not what usually happens.

</InDetail>

## What this means for you in practice

<Lead question="What happens if I order a peptide for myself?">The parcel can be stopped and destroyed. We found no fines reported for people buying for themselves.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Customs and the IGJ stopped hundreds of parcels in one month" value="329" label="parcels with medicines from abroad stopped in October 2024; all contents were seized and destroyed" counted="Postal parcels intercepted by Dutch Customs and the IGJ during operation SHIELD V, October 2024; most held small amounts" source="igj-2025-shield-seizures" />

The ban in article 40 is written for everyone. It has no exception for your own use.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> The text does not say whether a few vials at home count as "keeping a stock". The enforcement we found is aimed at sellers and websites.<Cite id="igj-toezicht-online-medicijnen,igj-2025-online-nepapotheken" /> For doping substances, the Dutch Doping Authority says the government tolerates possession for own use, but forbids trade.<Cite id="dopingautoriteit-doping-en-de-wet" />

<Callout type="uncertain" title="What we could not find">
We found no official statement on whether the IGJ fines people who import a small amount for themselves. We also found no published case of such a fine. The law allows it on paper. Our legal reviewer will check this point.
</Callout>

<InDetail title="When you stop being only a user">

The law treats supply very differently from use. Passing a product on to a friend, selling some on, or ordering for a group are all acts of supply. Supplying and handing over are on the list of forbidden acts in article 40.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> Done on purpose, that is a crime under the Economic Offences Act.<Cite id="nl-wed-art-1-2-6" />

</InDetail>

## Sport

<Lead question="Is doping a crime in the Netherlands?">Using doping is not a crime. Sport rules can ban you. Trading in doping is a crime.</Lead>

<Diagram id="nl-doping-law-and-sport" caption="In the Netherlands, criminal law targets trade in doping. Sport rules target the athlete.">

| Situation | Criminal law | Sport rules |
|---|---|---|
| You use doping, or test positive | You cannot be prosecuted for this<Cite id="dopingautoriteit-doping-en-de-wet" /> | A first violation can bring anything from a warning to a four-year ban<Cite id="dopingautoriteit-positieve-test" /> |
| You have doping substances for your own use | The government tolerates this<Cite id="dopingautoriteit-doping-en-de-wet" /> | See [sport and doping](/en/legal/sport-and-doping/) |
| You trade in doping substances | Punishable under the Economic Offences Act since 1999<Cite id="dopingautoriteit-doping-en-de-wet" /> | See [sport and doping](/en/legal/sport-and-doping/) |

</Diagram>

In Dutch gyms there are hardly any doping tests.<Cite id="dopingautoriteit-eigen-kracht" /> In Belgium there are, also at a low level.<Cite id="dopingautoriteit-controles-belgie" /> See [Belgium](/en/legal/be/).

## Where to report

<Lead question="Where can I report a side effect or an illegal seller?">Side effects go to Lareb. Websites that sell medicines illegally go to the IGJ.</Lead>

<JurisdictionCard code="NL" />

**Side effects.** Anyone can report a side effect to Lareb, the Dutch side-effect centre, also for someone else. This includes medicines bought online. Lareb does not pass your report to your doctor or pharmacist.<Cite id="lareb-bijwerkingen-melden" />

**Illegal websites.** You can report a website that seems to sell medicines illegally to the Landelijk Meldpunt Zorg, the national care reporting point. The IGJ then decides whether it should handle it.<Cite id="igj-online-medicijnen" />
