<Lead question="What does a 'research use only' label mean?">It is the seller's own statement that a product is for lab work. It is not a licence.</Lead>

<Diagram id="ruo-label-origin" caption="Where the words come from, and what they do and do not tell you.">

| | What we know |
|---|---|
| Where the wording comes from | In US rules, "For Research Use Only" is a statement for lab test kits that are still being developed. It is not a category of medicine.<Cite id="ecfr-21-809-10" /> |
| How it is used on peptide vials | Sellers put "research purposes only" or "not for human consumption" on vials of semaglutide, tirzepatide and other peptides that people inject.<Cite id="belcourt-2025-research-purposes,fda-warning-letters-index,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" /> |
| What it does not mean | It does not mean a regulator approved the product, checked its quality, or found it suitable to inject.<Cite id="mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,au-tga-2026-06-peptide-compliance-priority" /> |

</Diagram>

A licensed medicine has a number from the regulator on its pack, such as a DIN in Canada or an AUST R number in Australia. It comes with an official leaflet, and with a maker who must answer for its quality. A "research" vial has none of these.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,au-tga-prescription-medicines-overview,au-tga-about-artg,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-a-2026-10-24" />

## Why the label does not decide what a product is

<Lead question="Does the label decide whether a product is a medicine?">No. In all four countries, the law looks at what a product is for and what it does.</Lead>

<Diagram id="ruo-what-decides" caption="What each country's law looks at to decide whether a product is a medicine.">

| Country | What the law looks at |
|---|---|
| United States | The use the seller really intends. Labels, adverts, what the seller says and how the product is sold all count.<Cite id="ecfr-21-201-128" /> |
| United Kingdom | Whether the product is presented as treating or preventing disease, or can change how the body works through a drug-like effect.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-2" /> |
| Canada | Whether it is made, sold or presented for treating disease, or for "restoring, correcting or modifying organic functions".<Cite id="ca-fda-s2-definitions" /> |
| Australia | Whether it is presented, or likely to be taken, for a health use. That includes "influencing, inhibiting or modifying a physiological process".<Cite id="au-tg-act-s3-definitions" /> |

</Diagram>

Three regulators say it plainly. Health Canada: the label "does not make these products legal".<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> The TGA, Australia's medicines regulator: such words "do not change whether a product is regulated as a therapeutic good".<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> The FDA treats such products as drugs (see below). We found no MHRA statement about the label itself.<Cite id="uk-govuk-search-legal-en-b-2026-10-24" />

<InDetail title="Where the law is less clear">

The tests in the table are general rules. We did not search court records for rulings about "research use only" labels as such.

In the UK, the MHRA has not commented on the label in what we found. It does say that products sold as retatrutide outside clinical trials are supplied illegally.<Cite id="uk-govuk-search-legal-en-b-2026-10-24,mhra-2026-10-counterfeit-pens" />

In our reading, two things often point to a medicine: a vial made for injecting, and a website that promises healing, fat loss or muscle. This is our reading of the rules in the table, not a court decision about any one product. Our legal reviewer will check it.<Cite id="ecfr-21-201-128,uk-hmr-2012-reg-2,ca-fda-s2-definitions,au-tg-act-s3-definitions" />

</InDetail>

## The United States: what the seller intends

<Lead question="Does the label protect sellers in the US?">No. The FDA looks at what the seller really intends, shown by all their words and actions.</Lead>

<Diagram id="ruo-us-intended-use" caption="How the US medicines agency decides what a product is for.">

1. **Intended use decides.** In US rules, the use a product is meant for is judged from the seller's real, "objective" intent.<Cite id="ecfr-21-201-128" />
2. **Everything counts as evidence.** Labels, adverts, what the seller says, and how the product is sold. Even a use the seller knows about but does not advertise can count.<Cite id="ecfr-21-201-128" />
3. **The FDA has applied this to peptide sellers.** It sent them warning letters from 2024 to 2026. A "research use only" statement does not stop a product being a drug, it said. What counts is that the seller's website shows it is meant for people.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index" />
4. **The result.** The FDA calls such products unapproved new drugs. Selling them across state lines breaks federal law.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index" />

</Diagram>

More on US law: [United States](/en/legal/us/).

<InDetail title="What the FDA's letters to peptide sellers say">

In its warning letters, the FDA quotes the sellers' own "research use only" and "not for human consumption" statements. It then sets them against the health claims on the sellers' own websites. Its conclusion: the website shows the products are meant as drugs for people, so they are unapproved new drugs.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index" /> In one letter from August 2026, the FDA said that bacteriostatic water sold to prepare such products is itself a drug.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index" />

</InDetail>

## What the label changes in each country

<Lead question="What does the label change for me?">Nothing. What can happen to a parcel sent to you differs by country.</Lead>

| | United States | United Kingdom | Canada | Australia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does the label make it legal to sell? | <Verdict is="no" /> The FDA treats such products as unapproved drugs.<Cite id="fda-warning-letters-index" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Selling a medicine without a UK licence is an offence. The label does not change what the product is.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,uk-hmr-2012-reg-2" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Health Canada says the label does not make it legal.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> The TGA says such labels change nothing.<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> |

## What the label means for you

<Lead question="What does it mean for me if my product has this label?">Nobody had to check what is in it, how clean it is, or how it was made.</Lead>

<Compare title="A licensed medicine comes with checks. A research-labelled vial comes with none that anyone must do." detail="What the rules in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia require of a licensed medicine, against what is known about products sold as 'research only'.">

| | Licensed medicine from a pharmacy | Product labelled "research use only" |
|---|---|---|
| Made under medicine quality rules (GMP)? | <Verdict is="yes" /> The maker needs a licence and must follow GMP. Regulators inspect the factories.<Cite id="mhra-gmp-gdp-guidance,au-tga-manufacturing,ca-fdr-c02-018" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> No regulator has checked it. You cannot tell from the vial.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,au-tga-2026-06-peptide-compliance-priority" /> |
| Made under the rules for sterile products? | <Verdict is="yes" /> A medicine meant to be sterile must be made in a way shown to keep it sterile. In the US, each batch is tested.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c02-029,ecfr-21-211-167" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Unknown. In one lab test of three vials, sterility was not tested.<Cite id="piatkowski-2026-retatrutide-product-testing" /> |
| Does the amount match the label? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Each batch is tested for what it contains, and how much, before release.<Cite id="ecfr-21-211-165,ca-fdr-c02-018" /> | <Verdict is="unknown" /> In that test, from about half to almost double the label.<Cite id="piatkowski-2026-retatrutide-product-testing" /> |
| Must someone collect and report side effects? | <Verdict is="yes" /> The licence holder must collect reports and pass them to the regulator. US and Canadian rules set 15 days for the most serious ones.<Cite id="ecfr-21-314-80,ca-fdr-c01-017-manufacturer-adr" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> In practice, no one. You can still report a side effect yourself.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c01-017-manufacturer-adr,fda-medwatch,mhra-yellow-card,ca-hc-medeffect-reporting,au-tga-report-adverse-event" /> |
| Comes with an official leaflet about warnings and interactions? | <Verdict is="yes" /> An official leaflet and product information, checked by the regulator.<Cite id="uk-mhra-substance-index-status-a-2026-10-24,au-tga-about-artg" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> There is no official leaflet. The product was never assessed.<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> |

</Compare>

<KeyNumber title="The amount in a vial may not match the label" value="51% to 190%" label="of the amount on the label, in three vials sold as retatrutide" counted="Three products sold as retatrutide in Australia, tested in an accredited lab (Piatkowski 2026); sterility and endotoxin were not tested" source="piatkowski-2026-retatrutide-product-testing" />

If your product did not come from a pharmacy, these are practical next steps:

<Checklist>

- Keep the vial, the label and any paper with it. Write down the batch number. This helps if you need to report a side effect or a problem.
- A certificate of analysis covers only the sample that was tested, and only the tests it lists. If it lists no sterility or endotoxin test, those were not shown. See [what a lab report can and cannot tell you](/en/safety/product-risk/).
- Read [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/) and [sterile technique](/en/safety/sterile-technique/) before you inject anything.
- Report a side effect to FDA MedWatch (US), the Yellow Card scheme (UK), Health Canada's Canada Vigilance Program or the TGA (Australia). Anyone can report. Each one is linked on [if something goes wrong](/en/safety/if-something-goes-wrong/).
- Tell your doctor what you use. See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

</Checklist>

<Cite id="piatkowski-2026-retatrutide-product-testing,ca-hc-complaints,fda-medwatch,mhra-yellow-card,ca-hc-medeffect-reporting,au-tga-report-adverse-event" />

<InDetail title="The lab test of three vials, in more detail">

In 2026, researchers at the University of Queensland reported on three products sold as retatrutide in Australia. They were sent in anonymously to a private drug-testing service and tested in an independent accredited lab. All three contained retatrutide. But the amount ranged from 51% to 190% of what the label said. Lead and other metals were found only at low levels. Sterility and endotoxin, a toxic part of some bacteria, were not tested. Three samples cannot show how common these problems are.<Cite id="piatkowski-2026-retatrutide-product-testing" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Why sellers use the label, and what it costs you">

Three pharmacists wrote an editorial about online sales of semaglutide and tirzepatide "for research purposes". They describe what the label does. It lets buyers skip the prescriber and the pharmacist. That makes it hard to spot risky drug combinations, or to check on the person afterwards. Quality standards may not be met.<Cite id="belcourt-2025-research-purposes" />

A doctor can still help you with those checks. See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

</InDetail>
