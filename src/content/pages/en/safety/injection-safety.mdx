This page explains where peptide medicines are injected and how to avoid damaging the same spot. It also helps you tell a normal reaction from a problem. It is not a step-by-step guide and gives no amounts. A nurse, a pharmacist or your doctor can show you how to inject a prescribed medicine.

Read [sterile technique](/en/safety/sterile-technique/) first. Clean hands and a new needle matter more than anything on this page.

## Under the skin, never into a vein

<Lead question="Where do peptide medicines go?">Most go into the fat just under the skin. Never inject them into a vein.</Lead>

<Diagram id="injection-layer-and-places" caption="Most peptide medicines go into the fat under the skin, at a few places on the body">

- Most peptide medicines are given as a subcutaneous injection. That means into the layer of fat just under the skin.
- Authorised peptide medicines such as Wegovy are injected under the skin of the belly, the thigh or the upper arm. The Wegovy label says it must not be injected into a vein or a muscle.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" />
- The tesamorelin label adds: not into the navel.<Cite id="dailymed-egrifta-sv-label" />

</Diagram>

Why not into a vein? Anything in the liquid, including germs and particles, would go straight into the blood.

## Change the spot every time

<Lead question="Why change the spot each time?">Injecting in one place can cause fatty lumps. A medicine in a lump is absorbed unevenly.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Nearly two in three people in one insulin study had lumps" value="64.4%" label="had lumps where they inject, found when a nurse checked" who="Observational study in Spain: 430 people with diabetes who injected insulin" source="blanco-2013-lipohypertrophy" level="human-limited" />

<KeyNumber title="Changing spots the right way went with far fewer lumps" value="5%" label="had lumps among the people who changed spots correctly" who="The same study of 430 people who injected insulin" source="blanco-2013-lipohypertrophy" level="human-limited" />

Most of the evidence comes from insulin. Whether other peptides cause the same lumps has hardly been studied. Changing spots costs nothing, so it makes sense for any injection.

How to change spots:

- Inject on a different patch of skin each time.<Cite id="frid-2016-fitter-recommendations" />
- Some labels ask you to change the spot with every injection.<Cite id="fda-label-ozempic,fda-label-wegovy,dailymed-egrifta-sv-label" />
- Changing spots also lowers the chance of red, itchy or sore injection spots.<Cite id="dailymed-egrifta-sv-label" />
- Never inject into a lump. Let a doctor look at it.<Cite id="frid-2016-fitter-recommendations" />

<InDetail title="What the lumps are, and what the insulin study found">

If you inject into the same small area again and again, the fat there can thicken into lumps. Doctors call this lipohypertrophy.<Cite id="frid-2016-fitter-recommendations" />

One observational study looked at 430 people in Spain who injected insulin. Nurses found these lumps in 64.4%, nearly two in three. Almost all of the people with lumps had not changed their injection spot, or had changed it the wrong way. Among people who changed spots correctly, only 5% had lumps. Reusing needles more than five times also made lumps more likely.<Cite id="blanco-2013-lipohypertrophy" />

The lumps matter because a medicine injected into them is absorbed unevenly.<Cite id="frid-2016-fitter-recommendations" /> In the Spanish study, people with lumps more often had unexplained low blood sugar and big swings in blood sugar.<Cite id="blanco-2013-lipohypertrophy" />

</InDetail>

## Skin to avoid

<Lead question="Where should I never inject?">Into skin that is red, swollen, painful, bruised, scarred, hard, broken or has eczema.</Lead>

<Diagram id="skin-to-avoid" caption="Skin to avoid when you inject, and why">

Do not inject into skin that is:

- red, hot, swollen or painful, because it may already be infected;<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis" />
- bruised, scarred or hard;<Cite id="dailymed-egrifta-sv-label,frid-2016-fitter-recommendations" />
- broken, cut or affected by eczema, because germs get in more easily there.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis,nhs-skin-abscess" />

</Diagram>

<InDetail title="Cleaning the skin: soap and water, or alcohol?">

Wash skin that is dirty with soap and water, and dry it well. For injections under the skin, WHO says wiping the skin with alcohol is not needed.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

Some product leaflets do advise an alcohol swab before injecting. The Wegovy instructions allow an alcohol swab or soap and water.<Cite id="dailymed-egrifta-sv-label,fda-label-wegovy" /> If you use alcohol, let it dry completely before the needle goes in.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

</InDetail>

## Avoid needle-stick injuries

<Lead question="How do I avoid pricking myself or someone else?">Do not put the cap back on a used needle. Put it straight into a sharps container.</Lead>

A needle-stick injury is a prick from a needle that has already been used. It can pass on HIV and hepatitis B or C.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" /> Used needles that are not thrown away safely put other people at risk, such as family members and waste workers.<Cite id="frid-2016-fitter-recommendations,fda-sharps-dos-donts" />

<Checklist>

- **Do not put the cap back on a used needle.** Putting a cap back with two hands is a known cause of needle-stick injuries. If you really must, use one hand only: put the cap on the table and slide the needle into it.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit,fda-sharps-no-container" />
- **Do not bend or break a needle.**<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit,fda-sharps-dos-donts" />
- **Put the needle and syringe straight into a sharps container**, within arm's reach. Close it when it is three-quarters full.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit,fda-sharps-disposal-steps" />
- **Keep needles and the container away from children and pets.**<Cite id="fda-sharps-dos-donts" />

</Checklist>

See [sharps disposal](/en/safety/sharps-disposal/) for what to do with a full container, and what to do after a needle-stick injury.

## What is normal, and what is not

<Lead question="Is a red spot after an injection normal?">A small spot that fades is common. Spreading redness, pus or fever is not.</Lead>

A normal reaction stays small and gets better, not worse. An infection grows.

<Diagram id="normal-reaction-or-infection" caption="A normal injection reaction compared with signs that need a doctor">

| What you notice | What it may be | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| A small red spot, a little itch or a small bruise that fades | A normal reaction | Watch it. Use a different spot next time. |
| Redness, heat, swelling and pain that keep growing | A skin infection (cellulitis) | Get medical help straight away: your doctor, or a health advice line or urgent care when your doctor is closed |
| A painful, warm lump that fills with pus | An abscess | Contact your doctor today. Do not squeeze it. |
| Red lines running from the spot | Infection spreading along the lymph vessels | Get medical help straight away: your doctor, or a health advice line or urgent care when your doctor is closed |
| Fever, shivering or feeling very ill | Germs may have reached the blood | Get medical help straight away. Call your local emergency number if you also have a fast heartbeat or fast breathing. |
| Confusion, very fast breathing, blotchy skin | Possible sepsis | Call your local emergency number |
| Pain much worse than it looks, dark patches or blisters | A rare, fast-spreading deep infection | Call your local emergency number |
| A lump that does not go away or keeps growing | An abscess, a reaction to the product, or something else | See your doctor |

</Diagram>

<IconArray title="A small reaction where the needle went in is common. A small reaction where the needle went in is common, also with authorised medicines. Tesamorelin is an authorised peptide medicine. In its trials, 25 in 100 people had a reaction at the spot in the first 26 weeks. This means redness, itching, pain, irritation or bruising. With a dummy injection (placebo) it was 14 in 100.<Cite id="dailymed-egrifta-sv-label" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Abscesses without germs">

Not every abscess contains germs. After vaccinations, abscesses usually come from germs that got in. Less often they are sterile: a reaction to an ingredient of the liquid itself. One report describes two children who had this after vaccines that contained aluminium.<Cite id="klein-2009-sterile-abscesses" /> From the outside, both are a painful lump. So let a doctor check any lump that grows, hurts or does not go away.<Cite id="nhs-skin-abscess" />

</InDetail>

## Allergic reactions

<Lead question="What if I react badly to it?">Mild rash: stop and contact your doctor. Swollen throat or hard breathing: call your local emergency number.</Lead>

<Diagram id="mild-or-severe-allergy" caption="A mild allergic reaction compared with a severe one">

- **A mild reaction** is an itchy, raised rash, or puffy eyes or lips, with normal breathing. Stop using the product and contact your doctor. Do not use it again until a doctor has looked at it.<Cite id="dailymed-egrifta-sv-label,fda-label-mounjaro" />
- **A severe reaction (anaphylaxis)** makes the throat or tongue swell, makes breathing hard or wheezy, or makes you feel faint. It usually starts within minutes. Call your local emergency number. If you have an adrenaline pen, use it.<Cite id="nhs-anaphylaxis" />

</Diagram>

A severe reaction usually starts within minutes.<Cite id="nhs-anaphylaxis" /> So the first time you use a new product, it helps to have someone nearby who can help or call for help.

<InDetail title="Why the body can react to a peptide">

The body can react against the peptide itself. Antibodies and allergic reactions have been described for some peptide medicines.<Cite id="diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics" /> In tesamorelin trials, allergic reactions such as rash and hives were reported. The label tells people to get medical help at once if they suspect one.<Cite id="dailymed-egrifta-sv-label" /> Clumped peptide may also make an immune reaction more likely.<Cite id="rosenberg-2006-aggregates-immunology" />

</InDetail>
