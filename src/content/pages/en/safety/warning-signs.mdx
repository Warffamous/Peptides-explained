This page lists the signs that mean something may be going wrong after a peptide injection. It does not tell you what is causing them: a doctor can find that out with you. What you can do is notice the sign and get the right help quickly.

<Callout type="stop" title="Call your local emergency number now if" id="stop-now">

- someone is not breathing, or cannot be woken up;
- the lips, tongue or throat swell, or breathing is hard;
- there is chest pain or pressure that does not go away;
- one side of the face or body suddenly becomes weak, or speech is slurred;
- someone is confused, very drowsy or has a fit.

Your local emergency number is the one for an ambulance in the country you are in.<Cite id="us-medlineplus-when-to-use-er,nhs-anaphylaxis,nhs-chest-pain,nhs-stroke-symptoms,nhs-sepsis,medlineplus-malignant-hypertension" />

</Callout>

## The three levels of help

<Lead question="How fast do I need to act?">It depends on the sign: now, today, or within days.</Lead>

Every sign in the list below has one of three labels. Each label tells you how fast to act.

<Diagram id="three-levels-of-help" caption="Three levels of help, from the most urgent to the least urgent">

- **Call your local emergency number now.** A life may be in danger. Call an ambulance. Do not drive yourself.<Cite id="us-medlineplus-when-to-use-er,nhs-sepsis" />
- **Get medical help today.** Act straight away, not tomorrow. Contact your own doctor. When they are closed, use a health advice line or an urgent-care service. They are named for each country below.<Cite id="uk-nhs-111-online,uk-nhs-what-happens-when-you-call-111,au-healthdirect-helpline,us-medlineplus-when-to-use-er,au-healthdirect-cellulitis" />
- **Stop and contact your doctor soon.** Stop using the product. Make an appointment with your doctor within days, not weeks.

</Diagram>

If you are not sure which level fits, call a health advice line or your doctor. They will tell you what to do.<Cite id="uk-nhs-111-online,uk-nhs-what-happens-when-you-call-111,au-healthdirect-helpline,us-medlineplus-when-to-use-er" /> Calling too often is better than calling too late.

<InDetail title="Medical help today: who to contact in each country">

| Country | When your own doctor is closed |
|---|---|
| United States | There is no national advice line. Your doctor or health insurer may offer a nurse advice line. An urgent-care centre treats problems that are not life-threatening.<Cite id="us-medlineplus-when-to-use-er" /> |
| United Kingdom | NHS 111 in England, NHS 111 Wales, NHS 24 in Scotland, and the GP out-of-hours service in Northern Ireland. They tell you where to go.<Cite id="uk-nhs-111-online,uk-nhs-what-happens-when-you-call-111,uk-wales-nhs-111,uk-scot-suicide-prevention-support,uk-ni-gp-out-of-hours" /> |
| Canada | The health advice line of your province or territory, for example HealthLink BC, Health811 in Ontario or Info-Santé 811 in Québec.<Cite id="ca-help-healthlink-bc,ca-help-health811-ontario,ca-help-quebec-info-sante,ca-help-nl-healthline,ca-help-nwt-811" /> |
| Australia | healthdirect, with registered nurses day and night. It is called Nurse-on-Call in Victoria; Queensland also has 13 HEALTH.<Cite id="au-healthdirect-helpline,au-vic-nurse-on-call,au-qld-13health" /> |

The links are in the cards on [if something goes wrong](/en/safety/if-something-goes-wrong/#help-and-reporting-in-your-country), with notes for Ireland and New Zealand.

## Why these signs matter

<Lead question="Why do these signs matter?">Most come from an infection, an allergy or the substance itself. Some get dangerous within hours.</Lead>

Most of these signs have one of a few causes. Knowing them helps you understand why speed matters.

### Infection

<Diagram id="infection-spreads" caption="How an infection can spread from the injection spot to the blood">

1. A needle can carry germs from the skin, the vial or the air into the tissue under the skin. There they can grow.
2. First the skin around the spot becomes red, hot and swollen. This is a skin infection called cellulitis. Sometimes a pocket of pus forms. This is an abscess.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis,nhs-skin-abscess" />
3. If the infection is not treated, it can spread along the lymph vessels and into the blood. Red lines running from the spot are a sign of this.<Cite id="medlineplus-lymphangitis" />
4. In the blood, an infection can cause sepsis. In sepsis, the body's reaction to the germs starts to damage its own organs. Sepsis can kill, and it can develop within hours.<Cite id="nhs-sepsis,au-healthdirect-cellulitis" />

</Diagram>

### Allergy

The body can react to the peptide itself, to something else in the vial, or to a contamination. A mild reaction gives an itchy rash. A severe reaction, called anaphylaxis, can make the throat swell and make you faint. It usually starts within minutes and needs emergency treatment straight away.<Cite id="nhs-anaphylaxis" /> Allergic reactions have been described with some peptide medicines.<Cite id="diao-2013-peptide-pharmacokinetics,dailymed-egrifta-sv-label" />

### Effects of the substance itself

Some peptides act on the heart, the blood vessels, the blood sugar or the gut. GLP-1 medicines such as semaglutide and tirzepatide can cause an inflamed pancreas and loss of fluid through vomiting.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy,fda-label-mounjaro" /> Tanning peptides such as melanotan II have been linked to long, painful erections, muscle breakdown and changing moles in case reports.<Cite id="dreyer-2019-melanotan-priapism,nelson-2012-melanotan-rhabdomyolysis,cardones-2009-eruptive-nevi" /> In the UK, a few people were treated in hospital for very low blood sugar after using fake "weight-loss pens". The regulator said the pens may have contained insulin instead of semaglutide.<Cite id="mhra-2023-fake-weight-loss-pens" />

A product from outside a pharmacy may not contain what the label says. So a sign in this list matters even if it "should not" happen with the peptide you think you used. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

## What to do while you wait

<Lead question="What do I do while I wait for help?">Do not drive yourself. Stay with the person, and keep the product for the doctor.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- **Do not drive yourself** to a hospital. Ask someone else to drive, or call for an ambulance.<Cite id="nhs-sepsis" />
- **Severe allergic reaction:** if the person has their own adrenaline pen, use it. Let them lie down. Do not let them stand or walk, even if they feel better.<Cite id="nhs-anaphylaxis" />
- **Very low blood sugar:** if the person can swallow safely, give something sugary, such as fruit juice or glucose tablets. If they cannot swallow or do not respond, give nothing by mouth. Put them on their side and call your local emergency number.<Cite id="nhs-low-blood-sugar,ada-2026-hypoglycaemia" />
- **Stay with the person** until help arrives.
- **Keep the product.** Do not throw away the vial, the pen or the packaging. A doctor may need to see it.

</Checklist>

## What to bring and what to say

<Lead question="What do I bring, and what do I tell the doctor?">Bring the product and its label. Tell the doctor the truth about what you used.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- The vial, pen or box you used, with its label. Put a used vial in a closed bag or container.
- The package leaflet or any paper that came with it.
- What it was sold as, and where it came from (a pharmacy, a website, a gym, a friend).
- When you last injected, and where on the body.
- How much you used and how often. The doctor needs this to understand what happened.
- All other medicines, supplements and drugs you take.
- Any allergies you know of.

</Checklist>

### Tell the whole story

Tell the doctor the truth, even if it feels awkward. The tests and treatment depend on it. For example, some fake "weight-loss pens" may have contained insulin. A doctor who knows that your pen did not come from a pharmacy can check your blood sugar.<Cite id="mhra-2023-fake-weight-loss-pens" />

Doctors have a duty to keep what you tell them private. In the US, a federal law limits who can see your health information. In the UK, doctors have ethical and legal duties to protect it. Canada's provinces and territories, and Australia's privacy law, protect health information too. There are a few exceptions. In the UK, for example, a doctor may share information to protect someone from serious harm.<Cite id="hhs-hipaa-your-rights,gmc-confidentiality-duties,gmc-confidentiality-framework,opc-privacy-laws-summary,oaic-health-information" /> The doctor's job is to treat you, not to judge you. See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

## After the emergency

<Lead question="What do I do when the danger has passed?">Stop the product if it may be the cause. You can also report what happened.</Lead>

When the danger has passed, think about what caused it. If the product may have caused it, stop using it. Read [if something goes wrong](/en/safety/if-something-goes-wrong/) for the next steps.

You can report a side effect yourself.

<Diagram id="where-to-report" caption="Where to report a side effect yourself, by country">

| Country | Where to report |
|---|---|
| United States | FDA MedWatch. You can report it yourself; your doctor does not have to.<Cite id="fda-medwatch,us-fda-reporting-serious-problems" /> |
| United Kingdom | The Yellow Card scheme. Anyone can report.<Cite id="mhra-yellow-card" /> |
| Canada | The Canada Vigilance Program at Health Canada. Anyone can report, even if you are not sure the product caused it.<Cite id="ca-hc-medeffect-reporting,ca-hc-canada-vigilance" /> |
| Australia | The TGA, Australia's medicines regulator. You can report online, or ask your doctor or pharmacist to help.<Cite id="au-tga-report-adverse-event,au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers" /> |

</Diagram>

The links, and notes for Ireland and New Zealand, are in the cards on [if something goes wrong](/en/safety/if-something-goes-wrong/#help-and-reporting-in-your-country). Reports help regulators see patterns that one doctor cannot.

Many of these problems can be prevented. Read [sterile technique](/en/safety/sterile-technique/) and [injection safety](/en/safety/injection-safety/). Before you start anything, read [before you consider a peptide](/en/safety/before-you-consider/) and go through the [checks before you use a product](/en/safety/product-risk/#if-you-already-have-a-product).

## Where this list comes from

<Lead question="Where does this list come from?">We used official health advice for patients. Most of it is from the UK, the US and Australia.</Lead>

The labels follow official patient guidance. When the guidance differed between countries, we mostly chose the faster level of help. Where we kept the slower level, the sign says when to call your local emergency number instead. An example is fever after an injection. Australian advice for a skin infection with fever is to get urgent medical help.<Cite id="au-healthdirect-cellulitis" /> UK advice for a skin infection with a very high temperature or shivering is to call an ambulance or go to an emergency department.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis" />

| **Heart and blood vessels** | NHS pages on chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, fainting and priapism; MedlinePlus on very high blood pressure.<Cite id="nhs-chest-pain,nhs-shortness-of-breath,nhs-heart-palpitations,nhs-fainting,nhs-priapism,medlineplus-malignant-hypertension" /> |

| **Moles** | NHS.<Cite id="nhs-moles" /> |

This list is not complete. If something feels seriously wrong, trust that feeling and get help.<Cite id="nhs-sepsis" />
