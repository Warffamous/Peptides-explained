This page is for when you feel unwell after using a peptide product, or think something has gone wrong. It covers the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia, with short notes for Ireland and New Zealand.

<Callout type="stop" title="Call your local emergency number now if" id="stop-now">

- someone is not breathing, or cannot be woken up;
- the lips, tongue or throat swell, or breathing is hard;
- there is chest pain or pressure that does not go away;
- one side of the face or body suddenly becomes weak, or speech is slurred;
- someone is confused, very drowsy or has a fit, for example with very low blood sugar.

Do not drive yourself to hospital.

## Where to get help

<Lead question="Who do I call?">It depends on how fast you need help: your local emergency number, help today, or your own doctor.</Lead>

<Diagram id="where-to-get-help" caption="Where to get help, from most to least urgent">

| How urgent is it? | Who to contact |
|---|---|
| A life may be in danger | Your local emergency number, straight away, day or night |
| It cannot wait until tomorrow | Your own doctor. When they are closed: a health advice line or an urgent-care service |
| You are not sure how urgent it is | A health advice line, or your doctor. They tell you where to go |
| It can wait a few days | Make an appointment with your doctor |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="us-medlineplus-when-to-use-er,uk-nhs-when-to-call-999,nhs-111,au-healthdirect-helpline" />

A health advice line is a phone and online service, usually answered by nurses. It tells you how urgent a problem is and where to go.<Cite id="nhs-111,au-healthdirect-helpline" /> The United States has no national line: your doctor or health insurer may offer one.<Cite id="us-medlineplus-when-to-use-er" /> If you are not sure what to do, calling too often is better than calling too late.

| Country | Health advice when your doctor is closed | Advice about a possible poisoning |
|---|---|---|
| United States | No national line. Your doctor or health insurer may offer a nurse advice line. It connects you to your local poison center.<Cite id="us-hrsa-poison-help,us-hrsa-poison-help-about" /> |
| United Kingdom | NHS 111 in England, NHS 111 Wales, NHS 24 in Scotland, and the GP out-of-hours service in Northern Ireland.<Cite id="nhs-111,uk-wales-nhs-111,uk-scot-suicide-prevention-support,uk-ni-gp-out-of-hours" /> | The national poisons service does not advise the public. Use the same services, your doctor or a pharmacist.<Cite id="uk-npis-members-of-public,nhs-poisoning" /> |
| Canada | Most provinces and territories run their own health advice line; there is no national one. ## What to tell the doctor

<Lead question="What do I tell the doctor?">What you used, when, how much and where, and everything else you take.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- **The name** on the label, and what it was sold as.
- **Where it came from**: a pharmacy, a website, a gym, a friend or a clinic.
- **When** you last used it, and since when you have used it.
- **How much and how often.** The doctor needs this to understand what happened.
- **How you took it**, and where on your body you injected.
- **Everything else you take**: medicines, supplements, alcohol and drugs.
- **Allergies** you know of.

</Checklist>

<Cite id="nhs-poisoning,vic-health-2026-retatrutide-liver" />

<InDetail title="Why each detail matters">

- **The product may not be what the label says.** Some fake semaglutide held insulin, which makes blood sugar fall. If the doctor knows your product did not come from a pharmacy, they can think of this. Then they can check your blood sugar.<Cite id="stammers-2026-insulin-falsified-semaglutide,antonacci-2026-falsified-semaglutide-coma,mhra-2023-fake-weight-loss-pens" />
- **Doctors are asked to record these details.** In Australia, liver injuries were linked to a product sold as retatrutide. The health department of Victoria then asked doctors to note the product name, its ingredients, the dose and where it was bought.<Cite id="vic-health-2026-retatrutide-liver" />
- **Say it even if it does not feel like a medicine.** In a Swiss case, a woman was in hospital with days of vomiting. Only careful questions found the likely cause: weekly injections from a "life coach". She had not thought of them as medication. Her doctors advise asking patients directly about injections and peptides.<Cite id="buzzi-2026-retatrutide-case-report" />
- **Other medicines can change the risk.** For example, GLP-1 type products together with insulin can push blood sugar too low.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" />

</InDetail>

## Bring the product, and keep it

<Lead question="Should I bring the vial?">Yes. Bring the vial or pen with its label and box. A lab may test it.</Lead>

<Diagram id="keep-the-product" caption="Why the vial or pen helps, from your bag to the lab">

1. **Pack it safely.** Put the vial, pen or box in a closed bag or container. A used needle goes in a sharps container, not in your bag.<Cite id="nhs-poisoning,fda-sharps-disposal-steps" />
2. **Show it to the doctor.** The label tells them what the product was meant to be.<Cite id="nhs-poisoning" />
3. **A lab can test what was in it.** In Italy, a lab found insulin in a vial sold as semaglutide. In Australia, the regulator tested a vial sold as retatrutide. It held semaglutide and no retatrutide.<Cite id="antonacci-2026-falsified-semaglutide-coma,au-tga-2026-09-retatrutide-counterfeit" />
4. **What is found can protect others.** Doctors who treated two people for insulin poisoning from fake semaglutide stress that quick reports to the regulator are essential.<Cite id="stammers-2026-insulin-falsified-semaglutide" />

</Diagram>

Keep the rest of the product, unused, until you have recovered and decided whether to report it. See [sharps disposal](/en/safety/sharps-disposal/) for used needles.

## Why you can be open with a doctor

<Lead question="Will the doctor report me?">Doctors must keep what you tell them private, apart from a few exceptions.</Lead>

<Diagram id="doctors-keep-it-private" caption="The rules that protect what you tell a doctor, by country">

| Country | What protects what you tell a doctor |
|---|---|
| United States | The HIPAA Privacy Rule, a federal law. It sets rules and limits on who can see your health information. It covers most doctors, clinics, hospitals and pharmacies.<Cite id="hhs-hipaa-your-rights" /> |
| United Kingdom | Doctors have ethical and legal duties to protect your personal information. The General Medical Council (GMC), which registers doctors, sets out when they may share it.<Cite id="gmc-confidentiality-duties" /> |
| Canada | The provinces and territories have their own laws that protect health information.<Cite id="opc-privacy-laws-summary" /> |
| Australia | Privacy law has strict rules on how a health service may collect, use and share your health information.<Cite id="oaic-health-information" /> |

</Diagram>

The doctor's job is to treat you, not to judge you. See also [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

<InDetail title="Why confidentiality matters, and what we did not check">

- **Why it matters.** UK guidance for doctors says it plainly: people may avoid getting help, or leave out symptoms, if they think their information will be shared.<Cite id="gmc-confidentiality-duties" /> The same guidance says that private medical care is in the public interest, because it helps people come forward for treatment.<Cite id="gmc-confidentiality-framework" />
- **The exceptions.** In the UK, a doctor may share information with your consent, when the law requires or allows it, or in the public interest, for example to protect someone from serious harm.<Cite id="gmc-confidentiality-framework" />
- **What we did not check.** We did not check each country's rules on when a doctor may or must tell the police. If you are worried, ask the doctor what they will write down and who can see it.
- **Your record.** Your doctor will probably write down what you used. That is normal.

</InDetail>

## Report what happened

<Lead question="Should I report it?">Yes, if you can. Report the side effect, and a product you think is fake.</Lead>

<Diagram id="where-to-report" caption="Where to report a side effect or a fake product, by country">

| Country | A side effect | A product you think is fake, or a seller you doubt |
|---|---|---|
| United States | FDA MedWatch. You can report it yourself; your doctor does not have to.<Cite id="fda-medwatch,us-fda-reporting-serious-problems" /> | The FDA's [report-a-problem form](https://www.fda.gov/safety/report-problem-fda), for product quality or labelling problems.<Cite id="fda-report-a-problem" /> |
| United Kingdom | The Yellow Card scheme. Anyone can report.<Cite id="mhra-yellow-card" /> | Also the Yellow Card scheme, if you think a product is not genuine.<Cite id="mhra-2023-fake-weight-loss-pens" /> |
| Canada | The Canada Vigilance Program. Anyone can report, even if you are not sure the product caused it.<Cite id="ca-hc-medeffect-reporting,ca-hc-canada-vigilance" /> | Health Canada's [complaint process](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/compliance-enforcement/problem-reporting/health-product-complaint-process-summary.html), for fake products, unauthorised sales and misleading adverts.<Cite id="ca-hc-complaints" /> |
| Australia | The TGA, online or with help from your doctor or pharmacist.<Cite id="au-tga-report-adverse-event,au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers" /> | The TGA's [breach report](https://www.tga.gov.au/safety/report-problem/report-breach), for fake products and suspicious or illegal activity.<Cite id="au-tga-report-breach" /> |

</Diagram>

A medicines regulator is the government body that checks medicines and their safety. The links to report a side effect are in the [cards for each country](#help-and-reporting-in-your-country).

<InDetail title="Why reports matter">

- **They can stop a fake product from spreading.** Doctors treated two people for insulin poisoning from fake semaglutide. They stress that quick reports to the regulator are essential.<Cite id="stammers-2026-insulin-falsified-semaglutide" />
- **They can start a lab test.** In Australia, a report of a serious side effect led the regulator to test the product. The vial sold as retatrutide held semaglutide instead.<Cite id="au-tga-2026-09-retatrutide-counterfeit" />
- **The World Health Organization asks everyone** to stop using suspicious medicines and to report them.<Cite id="who-2024-falsified-semaglutide" />

{/* residue-ok: evidence (a study of the EudraVigilance side-effect database) */}
Reports also show patterns that no single doctor can see. A study of Europe's side-effect database found 234 reports about possible fake semaglutide. In them, low blood sugar was reported more often than with the real medicine.<Cite id="zinzi-2026-counterfeit-semaglutide-eudravigilance" />

</InDetail>

## Help and reporting in your country

<Lead question="Where are the contact details for my country?">In the cards below: help lines, poison advice and where to report.</Lead>

### Ireland and New Zealand

In Ireland, the HSE website shows where to get urgent care: GP out-of-hours services, injury units and emergency departments. The National Poisons Information Centre has a public line from 8am to 10pm. Anyone can report a side effect to the HPRA, Ireland's medicines regulator.<Cite id="ie-hse-urgent-emergency-care,ie-npic-poisons,ie-npic-contact,ie-hpra-report-side-effect" />

In New Zealand, Healthline gives free health advice by phone, day and night. The National Poisons Centre is free and open day and night. You can report a side effect to Medsafe, New Zealand's medicines regulator, with a CARM report (CARM is the centre that monitors side effects).<Cite id="nz-healthnz-healthline,nz-national-poisons-centre,nz-medsafe-report-a-problem" />
