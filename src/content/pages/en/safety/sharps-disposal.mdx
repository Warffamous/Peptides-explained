"Sharps" are needles and anything else that can cut or prick the skin. A used needle can carry blood. Blood can carry HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C, even when you cannot see it.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

Your own used needle is mostly a danger to other people. A loose needle in a bin or a bag can prick a child, a partner, a cleaner or a waste worker.<Cite id="frid-2016-fitter-recommendations,au-healthdirect-needlestick" /> Safe disposal takes a few seconds. It protects everyone around you.

## Never loose in a bin, the recycling or the toilet

<Lead question="Can I put a used needle in the bin?">Not loose. A used needle goes into a sharps container first, never into the recycling or the toilet.</Lead>

<Diagram id="needles-never-here" caption="Used needles never go loose in the bin, and never in the recycling, the toilet or the sink">

- **Not loose in household waste.** Someone who empties or sorts the bin can prick themselves on a loose needle.<Cite id="fda-sharps-dos-donts,au-healthdirect-needlestick" />
- **Not in the recycling.** Needles cannot be recycled.<Cite id="fda-sharps-dos-donts,nz-healthnz-safe-sharps-disposal" />
- **Not down the toilet or the sink.**<Cite id="fda-sharps-dos-donts" />

</Diagram>

In some places, even a closed sharps container must never go in the household bin. In others it may, under local rules. See [where a full container goes](#where-a-full-container-goes).<Cite id="au-act-recyclopaedia-syringes,au-qld-needle-syringe-program,nz-healthnz-safe-sharps-disposal" />

## A sharps container, and how to use it

<Lead question="What do I put used needles in?">A sharps container: a hard box that needles cannot pierce, with a lid that closes tightly.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- Keep the container within reach every time you inject.
- Put the needle and syringe in straight after use. Do not put the cap back on first.
- Do not push the contents down to make room. Do not try to take anything out.
- Close it for good when it is three-quarters full, or at the fill line.
- Keep it upright, closed and out of reach of children and pets.

</Checklist>

<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit,fda-sharps-disposal-steps,fda-sharps-dos-donts" />

Where to get a container differs by country. Pharmacies, needle and syringe programmes and the doctor who prescribes your medicine are common places to ask.<Cite id="ca-mb-safe-needle-disposal,au-qld-needle-syringe-program,uk-ni-pha-harm-reduction-booklet,fda-sharps-dos-donts" />

<InDetail title="What a sharps container is, and what to use until you have one">

A sharps container is made of hard plastic that needles cannot easily pierce. Its lid closes tightly.<Cite id="fda-sharps-containers" />

No container yet? Then use a strong, hard plastic container with a tight, screw-on lid for now, such as an empty laundry detergent bottle. Do not use glass, a thin drinks bottle or a can. Label it clearly as used needles.<Cite id="fda-sharps-containers,au-nsw-community-sharps,au-healthdirect-needlestick,ca-bccdc-sharps-disposal" />

Some places accept a container like this, others ask for an approved sharps container. Ask your pharmacy.<Cite id="nz-healthnz-safe-sharps-disposal" />

</InDetail>

## Where a full container goes

<Lead question="Where does a full container go?">It depends on your country, and often on your town. Ask your pharmacy, or check your local rules.</Lead>

<Diagram id="where-full-sharps-go" caption="Where a full sharps container can go, by country">

| Country | What official guidance says |
|---|---|
| United States | Follow your local rules. Options include drop-off sites such as pharmacies, hospitals and health departments, household hazardous waste sites, mail-back programmes and pick-up services.<Cite id="fda-sharps-disposal-steps" /> |
| United Kingdom | In England and Wales, some councils collect used needles from homes, sometimes for a charge. In Scotland, an injecting equipment provider takes them back. In Northern Ireland, a needle and syringe exchange does.<Cite id="govuk-clinical-waste-collection,uk-scot-nhsinform-injecting-advice,uk-ni-pha-harm-reduction-booklet" /> |
| Canada | Each province sets its own rules. In Ontario, for example, the law requires free collection of sharps in a sharps container, at pharmacies and other sites.<Cite id="ca-on-reg-298-12-sharps" /> |
| Australia | Each state and territory sets its own rules. Options include public sharps bins, pharmacies and needle and syringe programs.<Cite id="au-nsw-community-sharps,au-vic-needle-syringe-program,au-act-recyclopaedia-syringes" /> |

</Diagram>

Your pharmacist can usually tell you what to do where you live.<Cite id="fda-sharps-dos-donts" />

<InDetail title="The rules in each country in detail">

### United States

The FDA, the US medicines regulator, advises two steps. First, put needles in a sharps container straight after use. Second, when it is about three-quarters full, follow your local rules. Options include drop-off sites such as pharmacies, hospitals and health departments, household hazardous waste sites, mail-back programmes and special pick-up services.<Cite id="fda-sharps-disposal-steps" /> If you have no approved container, a heavy-duty plastic household container with a tight, puncture-resistant lid can be used.<Cite id="fda-sharps-containers" />

### United Kingdom

- **England and Wales.** Some councils collect used syringes and other clinical waste from homes. There may be a charge. You can find your council's service on GOV.UK.<Cite id="govuk-clinical-waste-collection" />
- **Scotland.** Used injecting equipment can be returned to an injecting equipment provider (IEP).<Cite id="uk-scot-nhsinform-injecting-advice" />
- **Northern Ireland.** Needle and syringe exchanges are free, confidential and anonymous, and open to people who inject performance and image enhancing drugs. They give out sharps containers and take back used equipment.<Cite id="uk-ni-pha-harm-reduction-booklet" /> We found no Northern Ireland government page on home disposal for other people who inject at home.

Your doctor or pharmacist can tell you about a sharps bin and local collection.

### Canada

We found no national Health Canada page on household sharps (checked October 2026). Each province and territory has its own rules. Three examples:

- **Ontario.** By law, the companies that supply sharps in Ontario must run free collection at pharmacies and other sites. A sharp can be refused if it is not in a sharps container.<Cite id="ca-on-reg-298-12-sharps" />
- **Manitoba.** Participating pharmacies give out sharps containers free to anyone who asks. Harm-reduction programmes also give out containers and take back used needles.<Cite id="ca-mb-safe-needle-disposal" />
- **British Columbia.** Harm-reduction sites give out free sharps containers. Take a full container to a health unit, a harm-reduction site or a pharmacy. Only if you cannot, close the lid and put it in a secure garbage bin.<Cite id="ca-bccdc-sharps-disposal" />

In the other provinces and territories, ask your pharmacy or local health unit.

### Australia

All states and territories have a needle and syringe program (NSP).<Cite id="au-healthdirect-needlestick" /> The rules for a full container differ:

- **New South Wales.** Public sharps bins are at some pharmacies, medical centres and council sites. At home, a puncture-proof container with a screw top is acceptable. Take a full one to a public sharps bin, or ask a pharmacy, medical centre or hospital.<Cite id="au-nsw-community-sharps" />
- **Victoria.** NSPs include participating pharmacies, and a state hotline tells you where to dispose of needles safely.<Cite id="au-vic-needle-syringe-program" />
- **Queensland.** If you inject a medical, cosmetic or therapeutic substance, ask your prescriber or a pharmacy for a container. In some council areas, a sealed sharps container may legally go in the household bin. Check with your council first.<Cite id="au-qld-needle-syringe-program" />
- **Western Australia.** People who inject can return used equipment to a needle and syringe exchange, or use a disposal unit.<Cite id="au-wa-healthywa-needle-disposal" />
- **Australian Capital Territory.** Used sharps must never go in household bins, even in a sealed container. Use a public sharps container or a drop-off point.<Cite id="au-act-recyclopaedia-syringes" />

We did not check South Australia, Tasmania or the Northern Territory. Ask your pharmacy or council there.

### Ireland and New Zealand

- **Ireland.** We found no current official Irish page on sharps at home. HSE needle exchange services are free and confidential. Ask your pharmacy or doctor where to take a full container.<Cite id="ie-hse-anabolic-steroids-reducing-harm" />
- **New Zealand.** Use an approved sharps container. Never put sharps in household, public or recycling bins, or down the toilet. Take them to your local pharmacy; pharmacies have different rules.<Cite id="nz-healthnz-safe-sharps-disposal" />

</InDetail>

## After a needle-stick injury

<Lead question="What if I am pricked by a needle someone else used?">Wash it, cover it, and see a doctor or go to an emergency department straight away.</Lead>

A needle-stick injury is a prick from a needle that someone has already used. The risk of catching a virus this way is very low. It is higher if the person who used the needle has a virus such as HIV or hepatitis, or injects drugs.<Cite id="au-healthdirect-needlestick" />

<Callout type="stop" title="Pricked by a needle someone else used?">

1. **Let the wound bleed freely.** Do not squeeze or rub it.
2. **Wash it** with soap and plenty of water, for at least 30 seconds.
3. **Cover it** with a clean dressing or plaster.
4. **Go straight to a doctor or the nearest emergency department.** Do not wait until the next day.

Medicine that prevents HIV must start within 72 hours. The sooner, the better.

</Callout>

<Cite id="au-healthdirect-needlestick,who-2010-injection-toolkit,cdc-hiv-pep,nhs-hiv-and-aids" />

If only you used the needle, you cannot catch a virus from your own blood. Clean the wound and watch for signs of infection, such as spreading redness or pus.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis" /> See the [warning signs](/en/safety/warning-signs/).

<InDetail title="What the doctor will do">

The doctor decides what you need. It depends on whose needle it was, whether there was blood on it, and when it happened. The doctor may also ask whether you are vaccinated against hepatitis B.<Cite id="au-healthdirect-needlestick,who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

- **HIV:** you may get PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis). PEP is medicine that helps prevent an HIV infection after a prick like this. You take it every day for 28 days. It must start within 72 hours, and every hour counts.<Cite id="cdc-hiv-pep,who-2010-injection-toolkit" /> In the US, you can ask your own doctor, an emergency department or an urgent-care centre.<Cite id="cdc-hiv-pep" /> In the UK, sexual health clinics and emergency departments offer it.<Cite id="nhs-hiv-and-aids" />
- **Hepatitis B:** if you are not vaccinated, you may get a vaccine, and sometimes antibodies, as soon as possible. If you were fully vaccinated before, you usually do not need this.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />
- **Hepatitis C:** there is no preventive treatment. Instead, you get blood tests in the following weeks and months, so that an infection can be found and treated early.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

The doctor may also suggest a blood test to check for these viruses.<Cite id="au-healthdirect-needlestick" /> If the person whose needle it was is known, they can be asked, with their consent, to have an HIV test. This helps the doctor decide whether you need to continue PEP. PEP is not delayed while you wait for the result.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

</InDetail>
