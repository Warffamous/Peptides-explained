<Lead question="What is this page?">Our view on how the rules should change. Its facts are sourced like every other page.</Lead>

Every other page on this site describes. This one argues. We write for people who may already have a peptide in the fridge, and we think some current rules work against the people they are meant to protect. Our proposals are principles, not one country's law: the problems look much the same in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia, and for each proposal we name something like it that already exists in one of them. Here is our case, with the case against it.

## Our position in brief

<Lead question="What is your position?">Better facts and better access do more good than bans and poor facts.</Lead>

<Diagram id="reform-position-in-brief" caption="Six problems with the current situation, and what we would do instead.">

| What happens now | What we propose |
|---|---|
| Untested peptides are bought and used anyway<Cite id="mhra-2026-05-largest-seizure,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,au-tga-2026-04-peptide-responsibilities" /> | Public, independent trials of the compounds people actually use |
| Tests of products keep finding the wrong amount or the wrong substance<Cite id="weber-2017-swiss-border,ashraf-2024-online-semaglutide,au-tga-2026-09-retatrutide-counterfeit" /> | Testing services for peptides, like drug-checking services |
| Most harms go unreported, so regulators see few warnings<Cite id="hazell-2006-adr-under-reporting,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" /> | Side-effect reporting that welcomes reports about any product |
| Products labelled "for research purposes" are sold for use in people, with dosing instructions<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" /> | Enforcement aimed at sellers and false claims, not at users |
| Use is often hidden from doctors, as a study of steroid users found<Cite id="pope-2004-steroid-users-physicians" /> | Guidance that helps doctors check on these patients |
| Promising compounds can wait years for a sponsor, or never get one<Cite id="ema-2021-repurposing-pilot,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> | Faster conditional routes, open to non-commercial sponsors |

</Diagram>

None of this means weaker rules for sellers. Every proposal keeps the ban on selling and advertising unapproved medicines. We want to change what happens to people who use these products anyway: whether they can know what they take, whether anyone notices harm, and whether anyone finds out if it works.

## The case for caution, taken seriously

<Lead question="Why not just let adults decide for themselves?">Untested medicines can do great harm. Thalidomide is the case most people know.</Lead>

<Photo id="kelsey-kennedy-1962" />

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, thalidomide was widely given to pregnant women for nausea. It caused severe birth defects in thousands of children.<Cite id="kim-2011-thalidomide-review" /> In the United States, an FDA reviewer, Frances Kelsey, refused to approve it because the company's evidence was inadequate. It was never sold there.<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history" /> The near-disaster helped pass the 1962 Kefauver-Harris amendments: for the first time, makers had to prove to the FDA that a medicine works before selling it.<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history,fda-history-milestones" />

<InDetail title="Why evidence before marketing matters">

Other countries learned the same lesson. In Australia, a doctor, William McBride, reported the link in The Lancet in December 1961,<Cite id="mcbride-1961-thalidomide-letter,au-tga-mcewen-2007-history" /> and in 1963 Australia appointed an independent expert committee to evaluate medicines.<Cite id="au-tga-mcewen-2007-history" /> In Canada, a 1963 report counted 115 children born in 1961 and 1962 with malformations linked to the drug.<Cite id="webb-1963-canadian-thalidomide" />

The rule that came out of thalidomide is simple: the maker must show that a medicine works and is acceptably safe, and an independent body checks, before it is sold. Early results and confident experts are poor guides.

<KeyNumber title="Most drug plans that reach trials in people never end in approval" value="13.8%" label="of drug plans that started tests in people were approved in the end: about 1 in 7" counted="41,040 industry drug programmes (one drug for one illness) with trials from 2000 to 2015 (Wong 2019)" source="wong-2019-clinical-trial-success" />

Laboratory results often do not hold up either: when scientists at one company tried to confirm 53 "landmark" cancer studies, they succeeded with 6.<Cite id="begley-2012-preclinical-standards" /> Even approved medicines can reach the market with uncertain benefit. Of 93 cancer uses given accelerated approval by the FDA from 1992 to 2017, the follow-up trials showed longer survival for only 19.<Cite id="gyawali-2019-accelerated-approval" /> A study of cancer approvals by the EMA, the European regulator, found a similar picture: of 68 cancer uses approved from 2009 to 2013, only 24 had shown longer survival at the time of approval; after a median of more than five years, 33 still had no clear evidence of longer or better life.<Cite id="davis-2017-ema-cancer-approvals" /> {/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The serious arguments against our position">

We take these seriously, and our proposals are shaped by them.

- **Information can look like approval.** A careful page about an untested peptide may make it seem normal.
- **Testing can become marketing.** A seller could use a test result as a stamp of quality.
- **Help can attract new users.** Services for people who already use a product might draw in others.
- **Money is limited.** Public money for trials of unapproved compounds is not spent on care known to work.
- **A regulator's strength is saying no.** Kelsey kept thalidomide off the US market despite constant pressure from its maker.<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history" /> Rules that bend to demand lose that strength.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Thalidomide's second lesson">

Thalidomide did not disappear. It later proved useful in leprosy and in multiple myeloma, a blood cancer.<Cite id="kim-2011-thalidomide-review" /> But in rural areas without strong medical surveillance, its use by pregnant women with leprosy has kept causing birth defects.<Cite id="kim-2011-thalidomide-review" />

The same molecule can harm or help. Information, control and follow-up decide which. We think that lesson applies here too.

</InDetail>

## People are already using these products

<Lead question="Does the ban stop people using them?">No. Seized goods, warnings and side-effect reports all show that people use them.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Thousands of illegal weight-loss pens were seized in the UK in one year" value="5,680" label="weight-loss pens seized in 2025. In all, almost 20 million doses of illegal medicines were seized." counted="GLP-1 pens seized by the MHRA, the UK medicines regulator, and its law-enforcement partners in 2025" source="mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025" />

{/* residue-ok: EU side-effect database as evidence */}
<KeyNumber title="Fake weight-loss jabs may show up in EU side-effect reports" value="234" label="reports about what may be fake semaglutide. Most side effects in them were serious: 89 in 100" counted="Reports in EudraVigilance, the EU side-effect database, from 2018 to 2025 (Zinzi 2026)" source="zinzi-2026-counterfeit-semaglutide-eudravigilance" />

How many people use peptides outside elite sport is not known: a 2026 review of peptide use in sport and bodybuilding says that studies of how common it is are lacking.<Cite id="coutinho-2026-doping-review" /> But the signs point one way. In 2026, Health Canada listed injectable peptides it had seized, and Australia's TGA said it had received reports of severe allergic reactions after unapproved peptide products.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,au-tga-2026-04-peptide-responsibilities" /> In May 2026 the UK regulator raided a site it believed was making retatrutide, tirzepatide and other peptide products.<Cite id="mhra-2026-05-largest-seizure" />

<InDetail title="What these numbers do and do not show">

Seizures measure enforcement as well as trade, and side-effect reports only what someone chose to report. So these numbers show that use is real; they cannot say how large it is.

Most of the UK's almost 20 million seized doses were not peptides: they were mainly sedatives, painkillers and erection medicines.<Cite id="mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025" /> Of 1,190 doping products seized at the Swiss border, 146 were labelled as peptide hormones or growth factors.<Cite id="weber-2017-swiss-border" />

</InDetail>

## What a ban without information does

<Lead question="What goes wrong when use is hidden?">People get unknown products, skip check-ups, and harms go unreported. Each problem feeds the next.</Lead>

<Diagram id="hidden-use-chain" caption="Three things that happen when use is hidden, and why each one matters.">

| What happens | Why | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| The product is unknown | No regulator checks its contents or whether it is free of germs | Wrong amounts and substances: under 20 in 100 seized doping products held what the label said, in that amount<Cite id="weber-2017-swiss-border" /> |
| Use is hidden from doctors | Users may doubt what doctors know about these products | No check-ups: 56 in 100 steroid users in one US study had never told a doctor<Cite id="pope-2004-steroid-users-physicians" /> |
| Harms go unreported | The user stays quiet, and no doctor knows | Few warnings: even for prescribed medicines, a median 94 in 100 side effects went unreported<Cite id="hazell-2006-adr-under-reporting" /> |

</Diagram>

Each link makes the next one worse. Someone who hides their use from a doctor will not report a side effect through that doctor. A harm never reported never becomes a warning for the next person.

<InDetail title="Unknown products: what laboratories found">

- **Semaglutide bought online without a prescription.** A Hungarian university made test purchases, and three vials arrived. The powder was only about 8% to 14% semaglutide, although the labels claimed 99% purity. Yet each vial held about 29% to 39% more semaglutide than its label said.<Cite id="ashraf-2024-online-semaglutide" />
- **Products sold as retatrutide, Australia.** Researchers tested three products: they held 51%, 165% and 190% of the amount on the label.<Cite id="piatkowski-2026-retatrutide-product-testing" /> After a report of a serious side effect, the TGA tested one more vial sold as retatrutide. It held semaglutide and no retatrutide.<Cite id="au-tga-2026-09-retatrutide-counterfeit" />
- **Falsified peptide medicines, Europe.** A national public laboratory tested the ten falsified peptide medicines most often found on its country's market, bought from suspected illegal websites. It found amounts that varied widely, low purity, and arsenic and lead in some samples.<Cite id="janvier-2018-falsified-peptides-belgium" /> {/* residue-ok: evidence (a public laboratory's tests) */}

Careful injecting cannot fix any of these. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Hidden from doctors">

The US study interviewed 80 weightlifters, 43 of whom used anabolic steroids. The users rated doctors as no more reliable on steroids than friends, websites or their dealers, and 56% had never told a doctor about their use.<Cite id="pope-2004-steroid-users-physicians" />

The study is about steroids, not peptides, and is more than 20 years old; we found no comparable study of peptide users. We expect the same pattern, but nobody has measured it: when use is something to hide, people hide it from the person who could help most. In the US, the UK, Canada and Australia, doctors must keep what you tell them confidential, apart from a few exceptions set by law.<Cite id="hhs-hipaa-your-rights,gmc-confidentiality-duties,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,mba-good-medical-practice-2020" /> See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Why unreported harm stays invisible">

A systematic review of 37 studies from 12 countries found that a median of 94% of side effects of medicines in normal care were not reported to the national reporting systems.<Cite id="hazell-2006-adr-under-reporting" /> For products used outside the medical system, the share is unknown. The FDA itself says side effects of compounded GLP-1 medicines are likely under-reported, because ordinary compounding pharmacies are not required to report them to it.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />

Reports work when people make them. In EudraVigilance, reports about possibly counterfeit semaglutide mentioned low blood sugar more often than reports about the genuine medicine.<Cite id="zinzi-2026-counterfeit-semaglutide-eudravigilance" /> That fits what regulators found when they tested fake pens: in Australia, counterfeit pens labelled as Ozempic contained insulin and appeared to be relabelled insulin pens.<Cite id="au-tga-2024-09-counterfeit-ozempic-pens" />

</InDetail>

## The cost of waiting

<Lead question="Is slow regulation harmless?">No. Waiting has real costs. And some compounds may never be tested, because no one will pay.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="By one estimate, a new medicine costs about a billion dollars to bring to market" value="$985 million" label="the middle estimate of what it costs to make one new medicine, with failed tries counted" counted="63 of the 355 new medicines approved by the FDA from 2009 to 2018 for which cost data were public (Wouters 2020)" source="wouters-2020-rd-investment" />

A company spends that kind of money only when it expects to earn it back. For a compound that is cheap to make and already sold widely, that may never happen. The EMA, the European medicines regulator, has itself written that companies sometimes lack the incentive to study new uses of old medicines that are no longer protected by a patent or data exclusivity, and that this "could be a wasted opportunity for public health".<Cite id="ema-2021-repurposing-pilot" /> {/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}

In public records, the developer's programme for BPC-157 did not go beyond one small phase 2 trial in the 2000s. That trial found no clear benefit, and it was published only as a short conference summary.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

<InDetail title="Why some compounds may never get a sponsor, and why the picture is not simple">

The estimate comes mainly from smaller companies and varies with its assumptions.<Cite id="wouters-2020-rd-investment" /> Nor is the incentive problem absolute. Even without a patent, a medicine with a new active ingredient gets years of protection from copies. In the US, a copy that relies on its approval cannot even be filed for five years (four, if it challenges a patent).<Cite id="ecfr-21-314-108" /> In Canada, such a copy cannot be approved for eight years.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c08-004-1-data-protection" />

So the barrier is cost and uncertain return. A compound with weak early evidence, many existing sellers and no clear patient group is unlikely to attract a sponsor, and that is often the kind people buy outside pharmacies. Without public money, "does it work, and what harm does it do?" may never be answered.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Costs for people now">

An approved medicine is not always one people can get. In England, NICE recommended tirzepatide for some adults with obesity, and the NHS must fund it, but NICE accepted that bringing it to everyone eligible may take up to 12 years, with access by clinical need.<Cite id="nice-ta1026-tirzepatide" /> Those are real limits of money and capacity. Meanwhile, the UK regulator and its partners seized 5,680 weight-loss pens in 2025.<Cite id="mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025" /> Whether the two are linked has not been measured.

Retatrutide shows the gap from the other side. In a phase 3, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 2,339 adults with obesity and without diabetes, funded by its maker, <Claim level="human-controlled">average weight loss at 80 weeks was 17.6% to 25.0%, depending on dose, against 3.9% with placebo</Claim>.<Cite id="jastreboff-2026-triumph-1" /> In September 2026 Australia's regulator said it had not been approved by the TGA or by any comparable regulator,<Cite id="au-tga-2026-09-retatrutide-counterfeit" /> while products sold as retatrutide were already being made and seized.<Cite id="mhra-2026-05-largest-seizure" />

</InDetail>

## What we propose

<Lead question="What should change?">Six legal steps. Each gives people better facts. None makes it easier to sell bad products.</Lead>

<Diagram id="six-proposals" caption="Our six ideas. Each one already exists in some form in the US, the UK, Canada or Australia.">

| Proposal | Something like it already exists |
|---|---|
| 1. Public, independent trials of widely used compounds | In the UK, a publicly funded trial in 176 NHS hospital organisations tested existing medicines for COVID-19<Cite id="recovery-2021-dexamethasone,recovery-2020-hydroxychloroquine" /> |
| 2. Testing services for peptides | Government-backed drug-checking services, in Canberra and in Wales<Cite id="au-act-drug-checking,uk-wales-wedinos-about" /> |
| 3. Side-effect reporting that welcomes every product | Australia's regulator takes reports about unapproved products, and one report led it to test a fake vial<Cite id="au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers,au-tga-2026-09-retatrutide-counterfeit" /> |
| 4. Enforcement aimed at sellers and false claims | Regulators act against sellers: FDA warnings, UK seizures, Canadian border checks, an Australian court case<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns,mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,au-tga-media-releases-peptide-log-2026-10-24" /> |
| 5. Guidance for doctors whose patients use these products | Alberta's medical regulator asked doctors in 2026 to ask patients about injections nobody prescribed<Cite id="ca-cpsa-2026-04-injectable-peptides" /> |
| 6. Faster conditional routes, open to non-commercial sponsors | Conditional or provisional approval exists in all four countries; the applicant is usually a company<Cite id="fda-accelerated-approval-program,mhra-conditional-marketing-authorisation,ca-hc-noc-c-fact-sheet,au-tga-provisional-registration" /> |

</Diagram>

None of these needs new science or weaker rules. Each answers one of the arguments for caution: testing reports problems but never certifies a product; trials replace guesses with evidence; enforcement stays aimed at the people who profit.

<InDetail title="1. Public trials of the compounds people actually use">

Once a substance is widely used, whether it works is a public health question. Most drugs tested in people never reach approval,<Cite id="wong-2019-clinical-trial-success" /> which is why the test is worth paying for: a clear negative result protects people too.

The UK's RECOVERY trial shows what a public trial can do. Funded by public research bodies, among others, it randomly assigned people in hospital with COVID-19 to existing medicines or to usual care, in an open-label platform design. In 6,425 patients, <Claim level="human-controlled">dexamethasone, a steroid, lowered deaths within 28 days (22.9% against 25.7%); the benefit was in people who needed oxygen or a ventilator, not in those who did not</Claim>.<Cite id="recovery-2021-dexamethasone" /> In 4,716 patients, <Claim level="human-controlled">hydroxychloroquine, proposed on the basis of lab results, uncontrolled studies and small trials, did not lower deaths (27.0% against 25.0%)</Claim>.<Cite id="recovery-2020-hydroxychloroquine" /> Both answers protected people.

We propose the same idea, with public funding, for the few unapproved compounds used most widely, chosen openly. The trials need not be as large; they need to be fair, and every result needs to be published.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="2. Testing services for peptides">

Some governments already support drug checking for people who use drugs. In Canberra, Australia's first fixed-site drug-checking service opened in July 2022 and is funded until June 2027. It is free and confidential, and it publishes warnings about particularly dangerous substances it finds.<Cite id="au-act-drug-checking" /> In Wales, WEDINOS, a Public Health Wales project, tests unknown substances that anyone in Wales can send in.<Cite id="uk-wales-wedinos-about" /> A systematic review of 90 studies found that such services <Claim level="human-limited">appear to influence what people intend to do and do, especially after an unexpected result</Claim>. Most studies were cross-sectional, so the evidence is limited, and they are about recreational drugs, not peptides.<Cite id="maghsoudi-2022-drug-checking" /> Public laboratories already test peptide products that were seized or bought from illegal websites.<Cite id="janvier-2018-falsified-peptides-belgium,weber-2017-swiss-border" />

The ACT Government says it plainly: drug checking does not make drugs safe.<Cite id="au-act-drug-checking" /> To stop testing becoming marketing, a peptide service should report problems, never approval: warnings about dangerous batches, nothing a seller could show as a certificate, and a plain statement that one test describes one sample, not the next vial.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="3. Side-effect reporting that welcomes every product">

In all four countries, anyone can report a side effect to the medicines regulator: FDA MedWatch in the US, the Yellow Card scheme in the UK, the Canada Vigilance Program and the TGA in Australia.<Cite id="fda-medwatch,mhra-yellow-card,ca-hc-medeffect-reporting,au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers" /> The FDA asks for reports about "these or any medications", and the TGA's reporting page covers unapproved goods.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns,au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers" /> Reports lead somewhere: in 2026, one report of a serious side effect led the TGA to test the product. Sold as retatrutide, it held semaglutide and no retatrutide.<Cite id="au-tga-2026-09-retatrutide-counterfeit" />

The systems exist; what is missing is trust and use. We propose saying clearly and often that reports about any product are welcome, explaining what happens to the reporter's details, and publishing what the reports show for unapproved peptides. See [if something goes wrong](/en/safety/if-something-goes-wrong/).

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="4. Enforcement aimed at sellers and false claims">

In all four countries it is against the law to sell or supply a medicine that has not been approved.<Cite id="usc-21-355,uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences" /> What the law says about a person who imports or keeps one for their own use differs by country, and in Australia by state. See the pages on the law in the [US](/en/legal/us/), the [UK](/en/legal/uk/), [Canada](/en/legal/ca/) and [Australia](/en/legal/au/).

We support the rules on selling. Enforcement should concentrate on sellers, above all those who make false health claims, and regulators already do this. The FDA has warned companies that sold unapproved weight-loss drugs falsely labelled "for research purposes", with dosing instructions.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" /> The UK regulator says it disrupted more than 1,500 websites and social media accounts in 2025.<Cite id="mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025" /> Health Canada works with the border agency to stop shipments of unauthorised peptides.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> In September 2026 the TGA announced Federal Court proceedings alleging that a business had unlawfully advertised peptides such as BPC-157.<Cite id="au-tga-media-releases-peptide-log-2026-10-24" /> Users are better reached with information than with fear, because fear keeps them away from doctors and from reporting.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="5. Guidance for doctors">

In 2014 the Endocrine Society, a medical society, noted that most users of performance-enhancing drugs are not athletes but people who lift weights, that their health risks are greatly underappreciated, and that observational studies are needed because trials cannot ethically copy such use.<Cite id="pope-2014-ped-endocrine-statement" />

Professional bodies have started to respond. UK guidance asks doctors who prescribe to ask about medicines bought online, and to encourage patients to be open about them.<Cite id="gmc-2021-deciding-safe-to-prescribe" /> In April 2026, Alberta's medical regulator asked doctors to ask patients about injections they were not prescribed, watch for complications and report side effects to Health Canada.<Cite id="ca-cpsa-2026-04-injectable-peptides" /> In September 2026, Australia's regulator of health practitioners published guidance on prescribing unapproved and compounded medicines: patient demand alone is not a reason to prescribe.<Cite id="au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance,au-ahpra-2026-09-peptide-prescribing" />

We propose the next step: practical guidance from professional bodies on which checks make sense for a patient who uses an unapproved peptide, and why. A doctor who knows what to look for is someone a patient can tell. See [monitoring](/en/safety/monitoring/).

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="6. Clearer, faster conditional routes">

Each of the four countries has a faster, conditional route for promising medicines for serious diseases. The FDA's accelerated approval can rest on a marker that is expected to predict benefit, and the company must still run studies to confirm it.<Cite id="fda-accelerated-approval-program" /> A UK conditional marketing authorisation is for an unmet need when full data are not yet complete; it lasts a year at a time, and the holder must meet set obligations.<Cite id="mhra-conditional-marketing-authorisation" /> Health Canada's notice of compliance with conditions requires the sponsor to run more trials to confirm the benefit.<Cite id="ca-hc-noc-c-fact-sheet" /> Australia's provisional registration lasts two years at a time, for at most six, while the sponsor collects the missing data.<Cite id="au-tga-provisional-registration" />

But all of them assume an applicant who will deliver full data later, usually a company. Access for single patients depends on companies too: in the US, no law requires a company to provide an unapproved product outside a trial,<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients" /> and in Australia, suppliers are under no obligation to supply one.<Cite id="au-tga-sas-individual-patients" />

We propose routes a public body or university can use, with the same duties to keep collecting evidence, and enforced: cancer approvals show how long uncertainty can last after a medicine reaches the market.<Cite id="gyawali-2019-accelerated-approval,davis-2017-ema-cancer-approvals" /> See [legal routes to treatment](/en/legal-routes/).

</InDetail>

## What we do not argue

<Lead question="Are you saying these products are harmless?">No. We do not argue for weaker rules, or that anyone should use anything.</Lead>

<Diagram id="what-we-do-not-argue" caption="Six things this page does not say.">

| We do not argue | Why not |
|---|---|
| That the rules for selling medicines should be loosened | Licensing protects people from unknown products and false claims |
| That any compound on this site is free of risk, or that it works | For most, nobody knows; each compound page says what is known |
| That anyone should use any product | We inform; the choice is yours |
| That the ban on advertising should go | It keeps commerce out of health information |
| That regulators act in bad faith | Their caution has good reasons, set out above |
| That the US compounding route is a model | It gives access without trials or required side-effect reporting |

</Diagram>

We are not anti-regulation and not anti-peptide. We are for knowing more: what is in a product, what it does, and what harm it causes, so that people can act on facts rather than marketing.

<InDetail title="Why the US vote on BPC-157 is not our model">

On 23 July 2026 the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) voted 8–6, with one abstention, to recommend BPC-157 for the "503A list" of substances US pharmacies may use to compound medicines on prescription. It considered seven peptides and recommended six: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax and epitalon. These results are as reported from the public meeting; the FDA has not posted minutes.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-questions" /> The vote is advisory and non-binding, and it went against FDA staff's own proposal.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" /> Legal compounding remains prohibited until the FDA formally adopts the recommendation and completes rulemaking.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" />

None of these peptides is an approved medicine in the US, the UK, Canada or Australia; Semax is registered as a medicine in Russia.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-semax-briefing,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,mhra-products-search-status-d-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-canada-legal-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-d-2026-10-23" /> A 503A listing would allow pharmacy compounding on prescription in the US. It is not a marketing authorisation, it says nothing about whether they work, and it changes nothing in the UK, Canada or Australia, where each country's own law applies.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1,uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences" />

The FDA told its committee that a product compounded from a listed substance is not FDA-approved and need not follow the usual manufacturing rules (only the ban on unclean conditions applies).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1" /> The pharmacy also need not report its side effects to the FDA.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1" /> That is access without the evidence and warning signals we argue for.
