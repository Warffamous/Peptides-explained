For an approved medicine, such as semaglutide or tirzepatide, the first route works in all four countries: a prescription. This page describes the legal channels. It does not help anyone get round the law. Your doctor is the starting point for every route. See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

More detail per country: [the US](/en/legal/us/), [the UK](/en/legal/uk/), [Canada](/en/legal/ca/) and [Australia](/en/legal/au/). For Ireland and New Zealand, see [the notes page](/en/legal/ireland-and-new-zealand/).

## Prescription

<Lead question="How do I get an approved peptide medicine?">From a prescriber who has assessed you, and a pharmacy. This is the same in all four countries.</Lead>

<Diagram id="prescription-four-countries" caption="Which peptide medicines on this site are approved in each country, and where you can check.">

| | Approved peptide medicines on this site | Where you can check | The rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | Semaglutide, tirzepatide, tesamorelin, bremelanotide (PT-141) and elamipretide (SS-31) | Drugs@FDA, the FDA's database of approved drugs | A prescription drug may be handed out only on a licensed prescriber's prescription.<Cite id="fda-drugsatfda-ozempic,fda-drugsatfda-mounjaro,fda-drugsatfda-egrifta,fda-drugsatfda-vyleesi,fda-drugsatfda-forzinity,fda-drugsatfda-database,usc-21-353" /> |
| United Kingdom | Semaglutide and tirzepatide | MHRA Products, which lists every UK product with its leaflet | A prescription-only medicine may be sold or supplied only on a prescription.<Cite id="mhra-glp1-what-you-need-to-know,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-a-2026-10-24,uk-hmr-2012-reg-214" /> |
| Canada | Semaglutide and tirzepatide. Tesamorelin was approved in 2014 but is no longer sold | The Drug Product Database. An approved prescription drug has an eight-digit DIN on its label | Prescription drugs may be sold to the public only by pharmacists, on a prescription.<Cite id="ca-sbd-ozempic,ca-sbd-mounjaro,ca-noc-egrifta,ca-dpd-egrifta-din-02438712,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,ca-hc-pdl-overview" /> |
| Australia | Semaglutide and tirzepatide | The ARTG. </Diagram>

A doctor does not have to prescribe something just because you ask. UK doctors must check that they know enough about your health before they prescribe. In Australia, Ahpra, the regulator of health practitioners, says patient demand alone is not a valid reason.<Cite id="gmc-2021-deciding-safe-to-prescribe,au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" /> A "research" vial with the name of an approved medicine is a different, unapproved product.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns,mhra-glp1-what-you-need-to-know,ca-hc-2026-01-glp1-advisory,au-tga-2026-03-counterfeit-glp1-products" /> See [semaglutide](/en/compounds/semaglutide/).

<InDetail title="Prescribing online, and NHS access in the UK">

### Prescribing online

**United States.** The FDA has warned telehealth companies about how they promote compounded weight-loss medicines. A compounded drug is not FDA-approved and is not a generic. The FDA does not approve or license the facilities that make such drugs.<Cite id="fda-telehealth-promoting-compounded-drugs,fda-2026-03-telehealth-warning-letters" /> Its warning signs include no check by a licensed doctor and no doctor to contact afterwards.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />

**United Kingdom.** The General Pharmaceutical Council (GPhC) regulates pharmacies in Great Britain. Its guidance says weight-loss medicines are not suitable for prescribing on a questionnaire alone. The prescriber must check your weight, height or BMI another way: by video, in person, from your records or through your GP. A phone call is not enough.<Cite id="gphc-2025-distance-pharmacy-guidance" /> A doctor must prescribe only when it is right to do so, with enough information about your health.<Cite id="gmc-2021-deciding-safe-to-prescribe" />

**Canada.** Provincial bodies oversee how doctors practise. We did not research their rules on prescribing online.<Cite id="ca-hc-2019-investigational-use-marketed-drugs" /> Under federal law, a prescription drug may be sold only by someone allowed to dispense it under provincial law, on a prescription they received.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c01-041-rx-sale" />

**Australia.** For off-label, unapproved or compounded medicines, Ahpra says a brief online consultation is not enough for a thorough assessment.<Cite id="au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" />

### NHS access in the UK

Each part of the UK has its own NHS rules for weight-loss medicines. In England, <Term id="nice-uk">NICE</Term>, which tells the NHS which new medicines to fund, recommends tirzepatide for adults with a BMI of at least 35 and at least one weight-related condition. Access is staged over up to 12 years, by clinical need. So meeting the criteria does not mean you get it at once.<Cite id="nice-ta1026-tirzepatide" /> Semaglutide for weight loss is recommended within a specialist weight-management service, for at most 2 years.<Cite id="nice-ta875-semaglutide" />

In Scotland, the Scottish Medicines Consortium, which advises NHS Scotland, has accepted Wegovy pens and Mounjaro for restricted use.<Cite id="smc-2497-semaglutide-wegovy,smc-2653-tirzepatide-mounjaro" /> In Wales, NICE says, weight-loss medicines are prescribed only within a specialist service.<Cite id="nice-ta1026-tirzepatide" /> We did not check Northern Ireland.

</InDetail>

## Off-label

<Lead question="Can a doctor prescribe an approved medicine for another use?">Often, if the doctor judges it right for you. It only works for approved medicines.</Lead>

<Diagram id="off-label-four-countries" caption="What the rules in each country say about using an approved medicine in another way.">

| Country | What the rules say |
|---|---|
| United States | Once the FDA approves a drug, a doctor may generally prescribe it for another use when that is medically right for you. The FDA has not found the drug safe and effective for that use.<Cite id="fda-off-label-use" /> |
| United Kingdom | Doctors should usually prescribe licensed medicines within their licence. Another use needs a medical reason. The doctor must be satisfied by the evidence and takes responsibility.<Cite id="gmc-2021-prescribing-unlicensed,mhra-2009-dsu-off-label" /> |
| Canada | Off-label use means a drug authorised for sale in Canada, used another way. Provincial bodies oversee how doctors practise.<Cite id="ca-hc-2019-investigational-use-marketed-drugs" /> |
| Australia | Off-label use only when no medicine in the ARTG is available or suitable, and the evidence supports it. Patient demand alone is not a reason.<Cite id="au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" /> |

</Diagram>

Off-label always starts with an approved medicine. It cannot make BPC-157 or another unapproved peptide available.<Cite id="fda-off-label-use,mhra-gn14-specials" /> We did not research each Canadian province's own rules for doctors.

<InDetail title="What your doctor should tell you">

In the UK, a doctor who prescribes outside the licence must give you enough information to decide. The doctor must answer your questions honestly, and explain when a use is not routine or when licensed options exist.<Cite id="gmc-2021-prescribing-unlicensed" /> The MHRA says a prescriber's responsibility "may be greater" for off-label use than within the licence.<Cite id="mhra-2009-dsu-off-label" />

In Australia, Ahpra says you must be told that the medicine is not approved for that use. Consent includes the cost to you.<Cite id="au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" />

In the US, the FDA suggests asking whether your insurance covers an off-label use, and whether a trial is open to you.<Cite id="fda-off-label-use" />

</InDetail>

## US compounding

<Lead question="Can a US pharmacy make a peptide medicine for me?">Only with allowed ingredients. BPC-157 and peptides like it are not on that list.</Lead>

<Diagram id="us-503a-ingredients" caption="What a US pharmacy needs before it makes a medicine for one patient (section 503A).">

1. **A prescription for one patient.** The pharmacy makes the medicine for a named patient, on a valid prescription.<Cite id="usc-21-353a" />
2. **An allowed ingredient.** The raw ingredient must have an official USP standard, be part of an FDA-approved drug, or be on the 503A list.<Cite id="usc-21-353a" />
3. **The 503A list is short.** It names six substances, none of them a peptide. It has not changed since March 2019.<Cite id="ecfr-21-216-23" />
4. **New ingredients need a formal rule.** The FDA consults its advisers, then publishes a proposed rule for public comment, then a final rule.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,fda-503a-bulks-page" />

</Diagram>

In July 2026, FDA advisers voted to recommend BPC-157 and five other peptides for the list, as reported from the public meeting. The vote was advice only, against FDA staff's proposal. Compounding with them stays prohibited until the FDA completes a rule. On 23 October 2026 the Federal Register held no such rule.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro,federalregister-fda-search-2026-10-23" /> See [what's changing](/en/legal/whats-changing/).

<InDetail title="The July 2026 advisory vote, point by point">

1. **The vote.** On 23 July 2026 the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8–6, with one abstention, to recommend BPC-157 for the 503A list. The count is as reported from the public meeting; the FDA has posted no minutes.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-questions" />
2. **Seven considered, six recommended.** The committee considered BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, emideltide (DSIP), Semax and epitalon. As reported from the meeting, it recommended all except emideltide.<Cite id="fr-2026-07361-pcac-notice,fda-pcac-2026-questions" />
3. **Advice only, against FDA staff.** FDA staff had proposed that none be added. The advice is not binding.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro,fda-pcac-2027-early-announcement" />
4. **Still prohibited.** Legal compounding stays prohibited until the FDA adopts the advice and completes rulemaking.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page,usc-21-353a" />
5. **Not an approval.** None of these is an approved medicine in the US, the UK, Canada or Australia (Semax is registered as a medicine in Russia).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-semax-briefing,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,mhra-products-search-status-d-2026-10-23,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,ca-dpd-search-canada-legal-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-d-2026-10-23" /> A listing would let US pharmacies compound them on prescription. It would not be an approval, would say nothing about whether they work, and would change nothing outside the US.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1" />

Meanwhile, the FDA lists these peptides as "previously in category 2", because their nominations were withdrawn. None of them is in category 1, the group of ingredients the FDA does not plan to take action against. Of the peptides on this site, only GHK-Cu is in category 1, and only for routes other than injection.<Cite id="fda-category-2-safety-risks,fda-503a-categories-2026-05" /> In 2023 the FDA told a pharmacy that products made with BPC-157 acetate did not qualify for the 503A rules.<Cite id="fda-compounding-actions-index" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Copies of semaglutide and tirzepatide">

The shortages that allowed wider compounding are over: for tirzepatide since 19 December 2024, for semaglutide since 21 February 2025.<Cite id="fda-glp1-compounding-policy" /> A 503A pharmacy may not regularly make what are "essentially copies" of an approved drug.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,fda-glp1-compounding-policy" /> Outsourcing facilities (503B) may not use them either: the FDA proposed to keep them off its list, and had made no final decision by 23 October 2026.<Cite id="fr-2026-08552-503b-glp1,fr-2026-12937-503b-comment-extension,federalregister-fda-search-2026-10-23" />

Retatrutide "cannot be used in compounding under federal law", the FDA says.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" /> In March 2026 it sent 30 warning letters to telehealth companies about how they marketed compounded GLP-1 medicines.<Cite id="fda-2026-03-telehealth-warning-letters" />

This section covers federal law. State pharmacy boards add their own rules, which we did not research.

</InDetail>

## UK specials

<Lead question="Can a UK doctor ask for an unlicensed medicine for me?">Yes, as a "special", for a clinical need that no licensed medicine can meet.</Lead>

<Diagram id="uk-specials-rules" caption="When a UK prescriber may ask for an unlicensed medicine, a 'special', for one patient.">

1. **A clinical need.** The prescriber decides that you have a special clinical need. Cost or convenience do not count.<Cite id="mhra-specials-guidance,mhra-gn14-specials" />
2. **Licensed first.** If a UK-licensed medicine can meet the need, even used off-label, it should be used instead.<Cite id="mhra-gn14-specials" />
3. **A licensed maker or importer.** A licence holder makes or imports the special, at the prescriber's request, for a patient in their care. It may not be advertised.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-167" />
4. **Products that are not medicines anywhere come last.** The MHRA calls them the least acceptable. They "should be avoided whenever possible".<Cite id="mhra-gn14-specials" />

</Diagram>

The MHRA guidance does not mention peptides. In our reading, a vial sold "for research" is closest to that last group. A UK pharmacist may also prepare a medicine on a prescription without a licence. We found no MHRA guidance on preparing peptides this way.<Cite id="uk-ma-1968-s10,uk-hmr-2012-reg-4" />

<InDetail title="Pharmacy preparation in the UK">

The Medicines Act 1968 lets a pharmacist in a registered pharmacy prepare a medicine on a prescription without a manufacturer's licence or a product licence. Other parts of the same section cover preparing a medicine for a person who asks for it at the pharmacy.<Cite id="uk-ma-1968-s10,uk-hmr-2012-reg-4" /> These exemptions lift only the licence rules. A prescription-only medicine still needs a prescription.<Cite id="uk-ma-1968-s10,uk-hmr-2012-reg-214" />

An amendment will let pharmacy technicians do some of this work, from a day the government sets. On 24 October 2026 the official text did not yet include it, and we could not confirm whether that day has been set.<Cite id="uk-ma-1968-s10" /> Which quality rules would apply to a peptide prepared this way is a question for our legal reviewer.

</InDetail>

## Compounding in Canada and Australia

<Lead question="Can a pharmacy make a peptide medicine for me in Canada or Australia?">Only for one person who has a prescription. In Canada, BPC-157 powder was called back.</Lead>

<Diagram id="compounding-ca-au" caption="The rules for a pharmacy that makes a medicine for one person, in both countries.">

| | Canada | Australia |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets the rules | The provinces regulate compounding. It must not be used to get round federal approval.<Cite id="ca-hc-pol-0051-compounding" /> | A pharmacist may make a medicine for one person, after the prescription arrives.<Cite id="au-tg-regs-sch-5-item-6-compounding,au-tga-compounded-medicines-guidance" /> |
| Ingredients and checks | It should be made from an ingredient of an authorised drug, or one in an official pharmacopoeia.<Cite id="ca-hc-pol-0051-compounding" /> | The TGA does not check compounded medicines before they are supplied.<Cite id="au-tga-compounded-medicines-guidance" /> |
| Peptides and GLP-1 medicines | In May 2026, BPC-157 and GHK-Cu powder sold to pharmacies was recalled as unauthorised ingredients.<Cite id="ca-hc-recalls-database" /> | Since 1 October 2024, pharmacists may not compound GLP-1 medicines such as semaglutide.<Cite id="au-tg-regs-sch-5-item-6-compounding,au-tga-2024-06-glp1-compounding-update" /> |
| Advertising | Compounders must follow the Food and Drugs Act rules on advertising.<Cite id="ca-hc-pol-0051-compounding" /> | Compounded medicines may not be advertised to the public.<Cite id="au-tga-compounded-medicines-guidance,au-tg-amendment-2026-measures-1" /> |

</Diagram>

In June 2025, Health Canada said that compounding injectable GLP-1 medicines without a shortage is not permitted.<Cite id="ca-hc-2025-glp1-compounding-position" /> In Australia, the TGA has said it will take legal action against compounding pharmacies and others that break the law on peptides.<Cite id="au-tga-media-releases-peptide-log-2026-10-24" />

## Canada: special access

<Lead question="What is Canada's Special Access Program?">A doctor can ask Health Canada for a drug not sold in Canada, for a serious illness.</Lead>

<Diagram id="canada-sap-steps" caption="How Canada's Special Access Program works for drugs.">

1. **A serious or life-threatening condition.** Usual treatments have failed, do not suit you, or are not available in Canada.<Cite id="ca-hc-sap-drugs" />
2. **Your doctor makes the request.** The doctor gives details of the medical emergency and what is known about the drug's use, safety and effect.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c08-010-special-access" />
3. **Credible evidence.** The decision to prescribe must be supported by evidence from medical literature or from the maker.<Cite id="ca-hc-sap-drugs" />
4. **The maker supplies it.** The drug comes straight from the manufacturer, which sets the cost. If the maker cannot supply it, the request is cancelled.<Cite id="ca-hc-sap-drugs" />
5. **Your doctor reports back.** The doctor tells you the risks and benefits, keeps records, and reports results and side effects.<Cite id="ca-hc-sap-drugs,ca-fdr-c08-010-special-access" />

</Diagram>

You cannot apply yourself. Health Canada has not authorised BPC-157, TB-500 or the other experimental peptides on this site, and has not assessed their safety, effectiveness or quality.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,ca-dpd-search-canada-legal-2026-10-23" />

<InDetail title="What the law says">

The legal basis is sections C.08.010 and C.08.011 of the Food and Drug Regulations. Health Canada may write to the manufacturer of a new drug, allowing it to sell a set amount to a doctor for the emergency treatment of a patient. Such a sale is exempt from the other rules of the Act. The drug is sent to a doctor or a pharmacist.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c08-010-special-access" />

</InDetail>

## Australia: SAS

<Lead question="What is Australia's Special Access Scheme (SAS)?">The routes a doctor can use to give one patient a medicine that is not approved in Australia.</Lead>

<Diagram id="australia-sas-routes" caption="The Special Access Scheme and the Authorised Prescriber route, and what each needs.">

| Route | Who can use it | What it needs | For the peptides on this site |
|---|---|---|---|
| SAS Category A | A doctor, for a patient who is seriously ill: death is likely within months, or early death without early treatment | Tell the TGA within 28 days | Possible in law, for such patients<Cite id="au-tg-regs-reg-12a-sas-category-a,au-tga-sas-individual-patients" /> |
| SAS Category B | A registered health practitioner | TGA approval first, with a clinical reason | The TGA wants human studies for that peptide, form, route and use<Cite id="au-tg-act-s19-access-approvals,au-tga-sas-individual-patients,au-tga-peptides-health-practitioners" /> |
| SAS Category C | Listed practitioners, for goods on a TGA list | Tell the TGA within 28 days | Not possible: no peptide on this site is on the list<Cite id="au-tga-established-history-lists-2026-10-24" /> |
| Authorised Prescriber | A doctor, for a group of patients with the same condition | Approval by an ethics committee, or a specialist college's support | Possible in law; no peptide on this site is on the fast-track list<Cite id="au-tg-regs-reg-12b-authorised-prescriber,au-tga-authorised-prescriber,au-tga-established-history-lists-2026-10-24" /> |

</Diagram>

Patients cannot apply. The doctor is responsible for the product and the outcome, and may say no.<Cite id="au-tga-access-unapproved-goods-practitioners" /> A TGA approval does not oblige a company to supply the product. The doctor must find it. Approval does not override state law.<Cite id="au-tga-sas-individual-patients" />

<InDetail title="What Ahpra expects from prescribers">

In September 2026 Ahpra, which regulates health practitioners, published new guidance and said it had acted against unsafe prescribing of unapproved peptides.<Cite id="au-ahpra-2026-09-peptide-prescribing" /> Prescribers should use an unapproved or compounded medicine only when no ARTG medicine is available or suitable, and the evidence supports it. "Patient demand alone is not a valid reason." You must be told that the medicine has not been assessed by the TGA. Prescribers must report side effects to the TGA.<Cite id="au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" />

Ahpra's first example case is a doctor who prescribed unapproved peptides on request, without checking the evidence or saying they were unapproved. Two patients had side effects and stopped.<Cite id="au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" />

</InDetail>

## Early access in the US and the UK

<Lead question="Can I get a medicine still in testing early, in the US or the UK?">Sometimes, if you are very ill, and only if the maker agrees to give it.</Lead>

<Diagram id="early-access-us-uk" caption="Three early-access routes in the US and the UK, and what each one needs.">

| Route | For whom | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| US expanded access, sometimes called compassionate use | A serious or immediately life-threatening condition, with no good alternative and no trial you can join | Your doctor asks. The company, an ethics board (IRB) and the FDA must all agree.<Cite id="fda-expanded-access,fda-expanded-access-patients,usc-21-360bbb" /> |
| US Right to Try | A life-threatening condition, when approved treatments are used up and you cannot join a trial | A drug that has finished a phase 1 trial and is still in development. The company does not have to supply it.<Cite id="usc-21-360bbb-0a,fda-right-to-try" /> |
| UK Early Access to Medicines Scheme (EAMS) | Life-threatening or seriously disabling conditions with a high unmet need | A company holds an MHRA scientific opinion and can supply the medicine. The scheme is voluntary.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-167c,mhra-eams-overview" /> |

</Diagram>

The FDA says it lets most expanded access requests go ahead. For retatrutide, its maker lists single-patient access for some adults with severe obesity and serious complications.<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients,ctgov-nct07629401" /> We found no early-access record for BPC-157 or the other unapproved peptides on this site, apart from retatrutide.<Cite id="ctgov-expanded-access-search-2026-10-23,mhra-eams-scientific-opinions" />

<InDetail title="What early access needs from the product">

**Expanded access.** The request must describe the manufacturing site and give data on the product's identity, quality, purity and strength. It must also show the product is reasonably safe at the planned dose.<Cite id="ecfr-21-312-subpart-i" /> No law requires a company to provide access. Insurance often does not cover the costs. A company that charges may only recover its costs.<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients" />

**Right to Try.** The FDA does not review or approve these requests, and no ethics board review is needed. "Life-threatening" has a narrow legal meaning, for example a disease where death is likely unless its course is stopped.<Cite id="fda-right-to-try,ecfr-21-312-81" /> Whether a condition qualifies is for your doctor and the company to judge.

**EAMS.** On 23 October 2026 three medicines had a current EAMS opinion. None is a peptide on this site.<Cite id="mhra-eams-scientific-opinions" />

**Registered records.** Only two early-access records are registered on ClinicalTrials.gov for the peptides on this site: retatrutide and elamipretide (SS-31). Not every arrangement is registered.<Cite id="ctgov-expanded-access-search-2026-10-23,ctgov-nct04689360" />

</InDetail>

## Personal import

<Lead question="Can I import a peptide medicine for myself?">The rules differ by country. In all four, the border can stop a parcel and destroy it.</Lead>

<Diagram id="personal-import-four-countries" caption="Importing a medicine for yourself: the rule in each country, and what the border can do.">

| | The rule for your own use | What can happen at the border |
|---|---|---|
| United States | In most cases it is illegal. The FDA may let some parcels through, but says this is not a right. A parcel sent by a seller abroad generally counts as commercial, outside that leeway.<Cite id="fda-personal-importation,fda-rpm-ch9-personal-importations" /> | Customs sets aside parcels that seem to hold drugs, and the FDA checks them. Refused drugs can be destroyed.<Cite id="fda-rpm-ch9-personal-importations" /> |
| United Kingdom | You need no import licence for a medicine for yourself or your household. Selling or supplying an unlicensed medicine is still an offence.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-17,uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,uk-hmr-2012-reg-214" /> | The UK Border Force seizes unlicensed medicines. Amounts judged more than personal use have been destroyed.<Cite id="mhra-foi-22-431-personal-import-seizures" /> |
| Canada | Residents may generally not bring in prescription drugs, in person or by post. Health Canada says injectable peptides are generally prescription drugs.<Cite id="ca-hc-gui-0116-personal-import,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> | The border agency can hold a parcel for Health Canada, which can refuse, seize or test it. It can order you to send it back or destroy it, at your cost.<Cite id="ca-cbsa-d19-9-1,ca-hc-gui-0116-personal-import,ca-fda-s27-2-removal-destruction" /> |
| Australia | Only under the Personal Importation Scheme: for you or close family, at most 3 months' supply, a clearly labelled pack, and for a prescription-only medicine a doctor's written authority.<Cite id="au-tg-regulations-1990-sch-5-item-1,au-tga-personal-importation-scheme" /> | Unlabelled peptide vials are not released. The Australian Border Force seizes and destroys them, and you lose the money.<Cite id="au-tga-2026-05-peptide-import-warning,au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> |

</Diagram>

The US row is federal law. A parcel from a seller inside the US is not an import, and we did not check state rules or whether receiving one for your own use breaks federal law. See [the US](/en/legal/us/#having-one-or-receiving-one-from-a-seller-in-the-us).<Cite id="usc-21-331" />

In Australia, BPC-157 and many other peptides are prescription-only (Schedule 4). In 2024 the TGA fined a person who imported a peptide without authority.<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10,au-tga-2024-04-peptide-import-infringement" /> Having one may also be an offence under state law, for example in Victoria. We did not check every state.<Cite id="au-vic-dpcs-act-1981" /> See [Australia](/en/legal/au/).

<InDetail title="More on each country's rules">

**United States.** The FDA's leeway "should not be interpreted as a license to individuals to bring in such shipments". More than about three months' supply suggests commercial use.<Cite id="fda-rpm-ch9-personal-importations" /> The FDA refuses parcels that appear to carry a serious risk, health fraud or products on an import alert.<Cite id="fda-personal-importation" /> We found no FDA figures on how many peptide parcels are stopped.

**United Kingdom.** The only official figures we found are from 2022. Border Force had seized about 6.3 million doses of unlicensed medicines in a year. Some contested parcels were released as personal use.<Cite id="mhra-foi-22-431-personal-import-seizures" /> We found no official source that defines a "personal" amount.

**Canada.** A personal amount is at most a 90-day supply or one course of treatment, whichever is less. If you give the product to someone else, you count as a commercial importer, even without money changing hands.<Cite id="ca-hc-gui-0116-personal-import" /> Health Canada says it works with the border agency to stop unauthorised peptide shipments.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> Whether a resident breaks the law by importing an unlisted peptide for their own use is a question for our legal reviewer.

**Australia.** Since 8 September 2026 the packaging rule is in the law, and travellers also need a written authority.<Cite id="au-tg-amendment-2026-measures-1,au-tga-2026-09-personal-import-packaging" /> An electronic prescription does not count, and an authority obtained after the parcel is held does not count either.<Cite id="au-tga-personal-importation-scheme" /> In New South Wales, having one is an offence only for medicines on the state's own list, unless prescribed for you.<Cite id="au-nsw-ptg-act-1966,au-nsw-health-s4d-2026" />

</InDetail>

## Trials

<Lead question="Can I join a trial?">Perhaps. A trial is the main legal way to use a medicine still being tested, if you qualify.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Almost no trial tests BPC-157 in these four countries" value="1" label="planned trial of BPC-157 in the four countries; it had not yet started" counted="ClinicalTrials.gov, ISRCTN and ANZCTR searches of 23 October 2026. The one planned trial is in the US. None was found for TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c or CJC-1295 either" source="ctgov-us-uk-peptide-search-2026-10-23,ca-ctgov-canada-peptide-search-2026-10-23,au-anzctr-search-status-c-2026-10-23,ctgov-search-status-c-2026-10-23,ctgov-search-status-b-2026-10-23,au-anzctr-search-status-b-2026-10-23,ctgov-search-status-d-2026-10-23,au-anzctr-search-status-d-2026-10-23,ctgov-nct07803250" />

<KeyNumber title="Retatrutide is not yet approved, but many trials test it" value="26" label="trials with a US site; also 8 in the UK, 10 in Canada and 6 in Australia" counted="Trials of retatrutide on ClinicalTrials.gov, 23 and 24 October 2026; many trials have sites in more than one country, and one recruiting trial has sites in all four" source="ctgov-us-uk-peptide-search-2026-10-23,ca-ctgov-retatrutide-ca-au-2026-10-24,ctgov-nct07165028" />

Taking part is always your choice, and you can stop at any time.<Cite id="ecfr-21-50-25,nhs-clinical-trials" /> Before a trial starts, an ethics board must approve it, and the regulator must authorise it or be told about it.<Cite id="ecfr-21-312-20,ecfr-21-56-103,uk-si-2004-1031-reg-12,ca-fdr-c05-clinical-trials,au-tg-regs-sch-5a-item-3-clinical-trials" />

<InDetail title="How to look for a trial, and what to expect">

**Where to look.** Ask your doctor or a patient organisation. ClinicalTrials.gov lists trials in all four countries.<Cite id="nhs-clinical-trials,ctgov-us-uk-peptide-search-2026-10-23" /> In the UK you can also search Be Part of Research, a website that lists UK health studies.<Cite id="nhs-clinical-trials" /> Health Canada has a portal of the trials it has authorised.<Cite id="ca-hc-ctsp-retatrutide-2026-10-24" /> In Australia, the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ANZCTR) also shows ClinicalTrials.gov records.<Cite id="au-anzctr-search-status-a-2026-10-24" />

**What you must be told.** In the US, the consent form must explain the purpose, the risks, the possible benefits and the other options. Saying no, or stopping later, may not cost you any benefits.<Cite id="ecfr-21-50-25" /> In the UK, leaving a trial does not affect the care you receive.<Cite id="nhs-clinical-trials" />

**What a trial can and cannot do for you.** Early trials mainly test safety and the right dose: phase 1 trials give a new medicine to a small number of people, who may be healthy volunteers. A new treatment may turn out to be no better, or worse, than the standard one.<Cite id="nhs-clinical-trials" /> In the US, an ethics board may approve a trial only if its risks are reasonable in relation to the expected benefits, if any.<Cite id="ecfr-21-56-111" />

**Who checks the trial.** In Canada, a sponsor may start 30 days after applying unless Health Canada objects, and a research ethics board must approve each site.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c05-clinical-trials" /> In Australia, trials follow Good Clinical Practice, with a study plan approved by an ethics committee.<Cite id="au-tg-regs-sch-5a-item-3-clinical-trials" />

**Retatrutide.** Its largest trial, TRIUMPH-Outcomes, aims to enrol about 10,000 people with overweight and disease of the heart, blood vessels or kidneys. It has sites in the UK, Canada and Australia, and is no longer recruiting.<Cite id="ctgov-nct06383390,ca-ctgov-retatrutide-ca-au-2026-10-24" />

**Other peptides on this site.** Elamipretide (SS-31) had open trials with sites in the US, the UK and Canada.<Cite id="ctgov-us-uk-peptide-search-2026-10-23,ca-ctgov-canada-peptide-search-2026-10-23" /> The FDA says a medicine still in testing should be used in a trial whenever possible.<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients" />

</InDetail>

## What this means for you

<Lead question="So what can I do?">Start with your doctor. Ask about approved medicines for your goal. If none fits, ask about trials.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- Tell your doctor what you hope to achieve. There may be an approved treatment for it.
- Ask whether an approved medicine could be prescribed, on-label or off-label.
- If approved medicines did not help, ask whether a special route could fit your illness.
- Ask your doctor or a patient organisation about trials that you might join.
- If you use something already, say so. Your doctor can still help you check your health.
- If you are prescribed online, expect a real check of your health, not just a form.

</Checklist>

<Cite id="fda-off-label-use,gmc-2021-deciding-safe-to-prescribe,gphc-2025-distance-pharmacy-guidance,ca-hc-sap-drugs,au-tga-sas-individual-patients,nhs-clinical-trials" />

UK doctors are told to ask about medicines you bought online, and to encourage you to be open about them.<Cite id="gmc-2021-deciding-safe-to-prescribe" /> See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

Each of these routes is narrow. For someone whom approved treatments did not help, that can be a real cost. We discuss this on [the case for reform](/en/the-case-for-reform/).
