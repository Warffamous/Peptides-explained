<Lead question="Why talk to a doctor at all?">A doctor can make a risky plan less risky. In the Netherlands, what you say stays private.</Lead>

You may expect a lecture, or worry that you will get into trouble. This page helps you have the talk anyway. It gives you words you can use, tells you what to bring, and explains your rights. The rights described here are Dutch law; other countries have their own rules. The last part is for GPs, physiotherapists and trainers.

## What to bring

<Lead question="What should I bring?">The product and its label, a list of everything you take, your goal, and your questions.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- **The product**, or a clear photo of its label. Bring any paper or certificate that came with it.
- **A list of everything you take**: medicines from your doctor or pharmacy, things you bought yourself, and supplements.<Cite id="thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" />
- **What you use, and how**: the name, since when, how much, how often, and where you inject.
- **Your goal**: what you hope the product will do for you.
- **Your questions**, written down.<Cite id="thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" />
- **Someone you trust**, if you like. Two people remember more than one.<Cite id="thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" />

</Checklist>

If you have a lot to discuss, ask for a longer appointment when you book.<Cite id="thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" />

<InDetail title="Why each item helps">

- **The label** tells your doctor what the product is meant to be. It does not tell anyone what is really inside. If it did not come from a pharmacy, no regulator has checked that.<Cite id="igj-online-medicijnen" />
- **The list of everything you take** lets your doctor spot risky combinations. For example, GLP-1 type products (such as semaglutide) together with insulin can push blood sugar too low.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy" /> See [before you consider a peptide](/en/safety/before-you-consider/).
- **Your goal** matters because there may be an approved medicine, or another treatment that has been tested, for it. Your doctor can only suggest one if they know what you want.

</InDetail>

## How to start the conversation

<Lead question="How do I bring it up?">Say it early and plainly. Doctors are used to talking about difficult subjects.</Lead>

| Your situation | Words you could use |
|---|---|
| You are thinking about it | "I am thinking about using a peptide called [name]. I want to understand the risks before I decide." |
| You already use it | "I have been injecting [name] since [month]. I got it from [where]. I want to be honest, and I want to check that I am OK." |
| You have a symptom | "Since I started [name], I have had [symptom]. Could it be from that?" |
| You want checks | "I plan to go on with it. Which checks would make sense for me?" |
| You want another option | "What I really want is [goal]. Is there an approved medicine or another treatment for that?" |

Tell your doctor what you have already thought of yourself. You can also say what you expect from them.<Cite id="thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" /> For example: "I am not asking you to approve. I want help to lower the risk." A tip: bring it up early in the appointment, so there is time to talk about it.

<InDetail title="If you feel ashamed or afraid">

GPs are used to talking about difficult things. You are not the first person to tell them something like this. Often, just talking about it already helps.<Cite id="thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" />

Being open also helps your care. Dutch law expects patients to give their doctor the information the doctor needs to treat them well.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-452-patient-information" /> And what you say stays private (see [your rights](#your-rights-privacy-and-consent) below).<Cite id="knmg-beroepsgeheim,nl-wgbo-art-457-confidentiality" />

</InDetail>

## If you already use something

<Lead question="What if I already use it?">Tell your doctor what you use, since when, and why. Bring the product if you can.</Lead>

| Tell your doctor | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| The name, and show the label | Your doctor can look up what it is meant to be. What is really inside stays unknown.<Cite id="igj-online-medicijnen" /> |
| Since when, how much and how often | It helps your doctor link a symptom to the product, or rule it out. |
| How and where you inject | Injecting raises the risk of skin infections. Your doctor can check the spots.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis" /> |
| Any symptoms, even small ones | A skin infection called cellulitis needs antibiotics. Without them, it can spread.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis" /> |
| Every other medicine and supplement | Some combinations are risky, such as GLP-1 type products with insulin.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy" /> |
| Whether you are tested for doping | Most of these peptides are banned in sport at all times.<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en" /> |

Your doctor will probably write this in your medical file. That is normal: a doctor must keep a file with what is needed for good care. The file is private, and you can ask to see it and to get a copy.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-454-456-records,nl-wgbo-art-457-confidentiality" />

## What to expect

<Lead question="What will the doctor do?">Listen, explain the risks and other options, and maybe advise against it. They will usually not prescribe it.</Lead>

<Compare title="Your doctor can help in many ways, even without prescribing the product." detail="What a doctor in the Netherlands can and cannot do. Checked on 22 October 2026.">

| | Your doctor in the Netherlands |
|---|---|
| Keeps what you say private? | <Verdict is="yes" /> By law, apart from a few rare exceptions.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-457-confidentiality,knmg-beroepsgeheim-doorbreken" /> |
| Explains the risks, and other options? | <Verdict is="yes" /> The law says they must, also the risks of doing nothing.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-448-information" /> |
| Can do something to you without your consent? | <Verdict is="no" /> Your consent is needed.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-450-consent" /> |
| Can prescribe BPC-157 or another unlicensed peptide? | <Verdict is="no" /> Not in the normal way. The exceptions are narrow (see below).<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning,igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring" /> |
| Can refer you to a specialist or for a second opinion? | <Verdict is="yes" /> A doctor should normally agree to refer you for a second opinion.<Cite id="knmg-2024-second-opinion" /> |
| Will agree with your plan? | <Verdict is="unknown" /> Maybe not. Telling you the risks as they see them is part of their job.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-448-information" /> |

</Compare>

Your doctor does not have to prescribe what you ask for. They must give the care a good doctor would give, by their professional standards.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-453-good-care" /> You still decide about your own body. And you do not have to decide straight away.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-450-consent,thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" />

<InDetail title="What a doctor can do instead of prescribing">

- **Check whether you should not use it at all.** Some illnesses and medicines make these products much riskier. See [before you consider a peptide](/en/safety/before-you-consider/).
- **Suggest an approved medicine.** A doctor may prescribe a licensed medicine for its approved use. Prescribing it for another use (off-label) is allowed only if doctors have professional guidelines for that use. While such guidelines are still being written, the doctor must first talk it through with the pharmacist.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-68-off-label" />
- **Do checks**, such as blood tests, and treat side effects. See [monitoring](/en/safety/monitoring/).
- **Report a side effect** to Lareb, the Dutch side-effect centre. You can report it yourself too, also for a product bought online.<Cite id="lareb-bijwerkingen-melden" />

</InDetail>

## If you feel brushed off

<Lead question="What if I feel brushed off?">Be specific. Ask about approved options and checks. You can ask for a referral or a second opinion.</Lead>

| If you hear | You could say |
|---|---|
| "I cannot prescribe that." | "I understand. Can you check me, and tell me what to watch for?" |
| "Just stop using it." | "I will think about that. Until I decide, what should I look out for?" |
| "I do not know anything about that." | "Could you refer me to someone who does?" |
| "There is nothing for that." | "Is there an approved medicine, or another treatment, for my goal?" |
| You still disagree | "I would like a second opinion." |

A second opinion is a check by a second, independent doctor in the same field. If you ask your doctor for a referral, they should normally give one. They may refuse only for serious reasons. Most health insurers only pay for a second opinion if you have a referral.<Cite id="knmg-2024-second-opinion" />

## Your rights: privacy and consent

<Lead question="Will my doctor keep this private?">Yes. Dutch doctors must keep what you tell them secret, apart from a few rare exceptions.</Lead>

<Diagram id="who-can-find-out" caption="Who can find out what you tell your doctor in the Netherlands">

| Who | Can they find out? |
|---|---|
| The police | No, apart from the rare exceptions below. Your doctor must keep silent, and has the right to refuse to answer police questions.<Cite id="knmg-beroepsgeheim-doorbreken" /> |
| Your employer, your family or anyone else | No, not without your permission.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-457-confidentiality" /> |
| Other carers who treat you | Yes, when they need it for your care, for example in a referral letter. You can object to this.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-457-confidentiality,knmg-beroepsgeheim-doorbreken" /> |
| You | Yes. You can see your own file and get a copy.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-454-456-records" /> |

</Diagram>

The rare exceptions include serious danger to other people, child abuse or domestic violence, and some infectious diseases that must be reported.<Cite id="knmg-beroepsgeheim-doorbreken" /> All doctors in the Netherlands have this duty of secrecy.<Cite id="thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" />

<InDetail title="Informed consent, and the law behind it">

Informed consent means: your doctor explains, and then you decide. The Dutch law on medical treatment (the WGBO, part of the Civil Code) sets this out.

- **Your doctor must inform you**, clearly and in time. That includes what a treatment is for, its risks, the risks of no treatment, and other possible treatments. Your doctor must invite your questions. If you ask, they must give you the information in writing, on paper or digitally.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-448-information" />
- **Nothing may be done to you without your consent.** If you are 12 to 15 years old, your parents or guardian usually have to agree as well.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-450-consent" />
- **Your doctor keeps a file**, and adds a statement from you if you ask. You can see it, get a copy and, with some exceptions, ask for it to be destroyed.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-454-456-records" />
- **Your doctor keeps your information secret**, unless you agree or a law requires them to share it.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-457-confidentiality" />

</InDetail>

## What the law lets your doctor prescribe

<Lead question="Can my doctor prescribe BPC-157 or a similar peptide?">Usually not. A doctor can prescribe medicines that have a licence. Other ways are rare.</Lead>

<Diagram id="prescribing-routes" caption="The ways a Dutch doctor can get a medicine to you, from common to rare">

| Route | Is it possible? |
|---|---|
| A licensed medicine, for its approved use | Yes. This is the normal route, through a pharmacy.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-61-prescription" /> |
| A licensed medicine, for another use (off-label) | Only if professional guidelines for that use exist. While they are still being written, the doctor must first talk it through with the pharmacist.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-68-off-label" /> |
| A medicine a pharmacist makes in their own pharmacy | The law makes an exception for a medicine that a pharmacist prepares in their own pharmacy, on a small scale. It has no licence.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> |
| An unlicensed medicine for one patient | Only with an exemption from the health inspectorate (IGJ). The doctor must state that approved medicines cannot be used, for medical reasons. The doctor takes responsibility for the risk.<Cite id="igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring,nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> |
| A medicine in a clinical trial | Only as part of a clinical trial.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> |
| A new medicine before approval (compassionate use) | Only through a programme that the Dutch medicines board (CBG) has approved. It is for a group of patients with a serious illness that approved medicines cannot treat well enough. The maker must have applied for a licence, or trials must be running.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning,cbg-compassionate-use-criteria,cbg-compassionate-use-list" /> |
| An online prescription from a doctor you never met | No. A doctor may not prescribe over the internet to someone they have never met.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-67-internet-prescribing" /> |

</Diagram>

So a GP cannot simply write a prescription for BPC-157, TB-500 or KPV. None of them is a licensed medicine.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,ema-medicines-search" /> For all legal routes, see [legal routes to treatment](/en/legal-routes/).

## For professionals who keep getting asked

<Lead question="I am a GP, physio or trainer. How do I respond?">Without judgement. Listen, give facts, point to safer options, and keep the door open.</Lead>

| Step | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1. Ask openly | "Are you using anything, or thinking about it?" Without judgement, so the answer can be honest. |
| 2. Find out what and why | Which product, from where, for which goal. |
| 3. Give facts, not fear | What is known and not known about the evidence and the product. Each [compound page](/en/compounds/) on this site uses the same structure. |
| 4. Point to safer options | An approved treatment for the goal. For people who go ahead: clean steps, checks and the warning signs. |
| 5. Keep the door open | Say they can come back, especially if something goes wrong. |

We found no Dutch guideline from the NHG or the KNMG on patients who use unlicensed peptides (searched 22 October 2026). The closest model is in the anti-doping field. The Dutch Doping Authority would rather see no use at all. But for people who choose to use anyway, it calls this kind of prevention essential. It works on information about health effects that does not judge.<Cite id="dopingautoriteit-2025-harm-reduction-chair" />

<InDetail title="For doctors: rules and resources that apply">

- **Autonomy and shared decisions.** The KNMG code of conduct asks doctors to respect the patient's autonomy, invite shared decision-making and enable an informed decision. The KNMG uses this to explain why a referral for a second opinion should normally be given.<Cite id="knmg-2024-second-opinion" />
- **What you must tell the patient.** The WGBO requires information on risks, on the risks of no treatment, and on other options.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-448-information" />
- **Off-label prescribing** of an authorised medicine needs professional protocols or standards. While these are still being developed, you must consult the pharmacist.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-68-off-label" />
- **Unlicensed medicines** can be supplied for one patient only with an IGJ exemption, based on your signed declaration.<Cite id="igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring" />
- **Side effects** can be reported to Lareb, also for products bought online.<Cite id="lareb-bijwerkingen-melden" />
- **A model from the anti-doping field.** With the Ministry of Health, the Doping Authority works on value-free information about substances used to perform better or look different. It is meant for people in strength and combat sports outside the sports federations, their trainers and care professionals. The first Dutch clinic for people with health problems after using anabolic steroids opened in 2010.<Cite id="dopingautoriteit-2025-harm-reduction-chair" /> We know of no such clinic for peptide users.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="For trainers and physiotherapists: where the line is">

- **Selling or handing over a medicine that has no licence** is against Dutch law, also to clients.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> Whether a product sold "for research" counts as a medicine is explained on [research use only](/en/legal/research-use-only/).
- **Giving injections as part of your work** is a reserved procedure under Dutch law (the Wet BIG). Only doctors and some other care professionals may do it, or people who work on their instructions.<Cite id="nl-wet-big-art-35-36" />
- **Recommending a product or a seller** means vouching for something no regulator has checked. The Dutch inspectorate says medicines ordered online get no check like the one a doctor or pharmacist gives.<Cite id="igj-online-medicijnen" />
- **What you can do:** listen without judgement, share facts from independent sources, and suggest that the client talks to a doctor.
- **If an injury does not heal as expected**, or a client has a [warning sign](/en/safety/warning-signs/), send them to a doctor.

</InDetail>
