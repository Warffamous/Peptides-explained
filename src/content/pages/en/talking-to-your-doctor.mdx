<Lead question="Why talk to a doctor at all?">A doctor can make a risky plan less risky. What you say stays private, with few exceptions.</Lead>

You may expect a lecture, or worry that you will get into trouble. This page helps you have the talk anyway. It gives you words you can use, tells you what to bring, and explains your rights in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia. The last part is for doctors, physiotherapists and trainers.

## What to bring

<Lead question="What should I bring?">The product and its label, a list of everything you take, your goal, and your questions.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- **The product**, or a clear photo of its label. Bring any paper or lab report that came with it.
- **A list of everything you take**: medicines from your doctor or pharmacy, things you bought yourself, vitamins and other supplements.<Cite id="au-healthdirect-questions-to-ask-your-doctor" />
- **What you use, and how**: the name, since when, how much, how often, and where you inject.
- **Your goal**: what you hope the product will do for you.
- **Your questions**, written down.<Cite id="au-healthdirect-questions-to-ask-your-doctor" />
- **Someone you trust**, if you like. A friend or relative can help you remember what was said.<Cite id="au-healthdirect-questions-to-ask-your-doctor" />

</Checklist>

If your doctor says something you do not understand, ask them to say it again in another way, or to write it down.<Cite id="au-healthdirect-questions-to-ask-your-doctor" />

<InDetail title="Why each item helps">

- **The label** tells your doctor what the product is meant to be. It does not tell anyone what is really inside. If it did not come from a pharmacy, no regulator has checked that.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025" />
- **The list of everything you take** lets your doctor spot risky combinations. For example, GLP-1 type products (such as semaglutide) together with insulin can push blood sugar too low.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" /> See [before you consider a peptide](/en/safety/before-you-consider/).
- **Your goal** matters because there may be an approved medicine, or another treatment that has been tested, for it. When your doctor knows what you want, they can suggest one.

</InDetail>

## How to start the conversation

<Lead question="How do I bring it up?">Say it early and plainly. Doctors are used to talking about difficult subjects.</Lead>

| Your situation | Words you could use |
|---|---|
| You are thinking about it | "I am thinking about using a peptide called [name]. I want to understand the risks before I decide." |
| You already use it | "I have been injecting [name] since [month]. I got it from [where]. I want to be honest, and I want to check that I am OK." |
| You have a symptom | "Since I started [name], I have had [symptom]. Could it be from that?" |
| You want checks | "I plan to go on with it. Which checks would make sense for me?" |
| You want another option | "What I really want is [goal]. Is there an approved medicine or another treatment for that?" |

Tell your doctor what matters to you, and what you hope they can do. Doctors are asked to find out what matters to you before they talk through the options.<Cite id="gmc-2020-consent-seven-principles,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics" /> For example: "I am not asking you to approve. I want help to lower the risk." A tip: bring it up early in the appointment, so there is time to talk about it.

<InDetail title="If you feel ashamed or afraid">

You are not the only one. Doctors are being asked to talk about this. UK guidance asks doctors who prescribe to ask about medicines bought online, and to encourage patients to be open about them.<Cite id="gmc-2021-deciding-safe-to-prescribe" /> In Alberta, Canada, the medical regulator asked doctors in 2026 to ask patients about injections they were not prescribed, such as peptides.<Cite id="ca-cpsa-2026-04-injectable-peptides" />

Privacy rules exist for exactly this reason. People may stay away from a doctor, or leave out symptoms, if they think what they say will be passed on.<Cite id="gmc-confidentiality-duties" /> What you say stays private (see [your rights](#your-rights-privacy-and-consent) below).

</InDetail>

## If you already use something

<Lead question="What if I already use it?">Tell your doctor what you use, since when, and why. Bring the product if you can.</Lead>

| Tell your doctor | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| The name, and show the label | Your doctor can look up what it is meant to be. What is really inside stays unknown.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> |
| Since when, how much and how often | It helps your doctor link a symptom to the product, or rule it out. |
| How and where you inject | Injecting raises the risk of skin infections. Your doctor can check the spots.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis" /> |
| Any symptoms, even small ones | A skin infection called cellulitis needs antibiotics. Without them, it can spread.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis" /> |
| Every other medicine and supplement | Some combinations are risky, such as GLP-1 type products with insulin.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" /> |
| Whether you are tested for doping | Most of these peptides are banned in sport at all times.<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en" /> |

Your doctor will probably write this in your medical record. That is normal: a clear record is part of good care. The record is private. You can usually ask to see it and get a copy, with a few exceptions. The rules differ by country (see [your rights](#your-rights-privacy-and-consent) below).<Cite id="mba-good-medical-practice-2020,ecfr-45-164-524,nhs-constitution-england,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,oaic-access-health-information" />

## What to expect

<Lead question="What will the doctor do?">Listen, explain the risks and other options, and maybe advise against it. They will usually not prescribe it.</Lead>

Your doctor does not have to prescribe what you ask for. In Australia, the rules say so plainly: "Patient demand alone is not a valid reason to prescribe a medicine." In the UK, a doctor should not give a treatment they think would not meet your needs, even if you ask for it.<Cite id="au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance,gmc-2020-consent-paras-48-49" /> You still decide about your own body. You can say no to a treatment, and you can ask for time to decide.<Cite id="medlineplus-informed-consent-adults,gmc-2020-consent-paras-48-49,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,au-healthdirect-questions-to-ask-your-doctor" />

<InDetail title="What a doctor can do instead of prescribing">

- **Check whether you should not use it at all.** Some illnesses and medicines make these products much riskier. See [before you consider a peptide](/en/safety/before-you-consider/).
- **Suggest an approved medicine.** A doctor may prescribe an approved medicine for its approved use. A doctor may sometimes use it in another way (off-label), under each country's own rules. That only works for medicines that are already approved.<Cite id="fda-off-label-use,gmc-2021-prescribing-unlicensed,ca-hc-2019-investigational-use-marketed-drugs,au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" /> See [off-label use](/en/legal-routes/#off-label).
- **Do checks**, such as blood tests, and treat side effects. See [monitoring](/en/safety/monitoring/).
- **Report a side effect** to the medicines regulator: FDA MedWatch in the US, the Yellow Card scheme in the UK, Health Canada (the Canada Vigilance Program) and the TGA in Australia. You can report it yourself too.<Cite id="fda-medwatch,mhra-yellow-card,ca-hc-medeffect-reporting,au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers" /> See [if something goes wrong](/en/safety/if-something-goes-wrong/).

</InDetail>

## If you feel brushed off

<Lead question="What if I feel brushed off?">Be specific. Ask about approved options and checks. You can ask for a referral or a second opinion.</Lead>

| If you hear | You could say |
|---|---|
| "I cannot prescribe that." | "I understand. Can you check me, and tell me what to watch for?" |
| "Just stop using it." | "I will think about that. Until I decide, what should I look out for?" |
| "I do not know anything about that." | "Could you refer me to someone who does?" |
| "There is nothing for that." | "Is there an approved medicine, or another treatment, for my goal?" |
| You still disagree | "I would like a second opinion." |

A second opinion is a check by a second, independent doctor. In Canada and Australia, the rules for doctors ask them to respect or support your wish for one. A UK doctor who will not give a treatment should explain why, and mention your right to a second opinion.<Cite id="gmc-2020-consent-paras-48-49,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,mba-good-medical-practice-2020" />

## Your rights: privacy and consent

<Lead question="Will my doctor keep this private?">Yes. In all four countries, doctors must keep what you say private, apart from a few exceptions.</Lead>

<Diagram id="who-can-find-out" caption="Who can find out what you tell your doctor, in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia">

| Who | Can they find out? |
|---|---|
| The police | Not as a rule. A doctor may share information with the police only in cases the law sets out, for example a court order, or to prevent serious harm to someone.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-512,gmc-confidentiality-framework,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,oaic-handling-health-information" /> |
| Your employer, your family or anyone else | Not without your permission, apart from the same legal exceptions.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-508,gmc-confidentiality-framework,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,oaic-handling-health-information" /> In the US, a family member or friend who helps with your care may be told what they need for that, unless you object.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-510" /> |
| Other health workers who treat you | Yes, when they need it for your care.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-506,gmc-confidentiality-duties,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,mba-good-medical-practice-2020" /> |
| You | Yes. You can ask to see your own record and get a copy, with a few exceptions.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-524,nhs-constitution-england,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,oaic-access-health-information" /> |

</Diagram>

Informed consent means: your doctor explains your choices, with their risks, and then you decide.<Cite id="medlineplus-informed-consent-adults,mba-good-medical-practice-2020" /> Each country writes these rights down in its own way. The details are below.

<InDetail title="Your rights in each country">

- **United States.** A federal rule, the HIPAA Privacy Rule, limits who can see and receive your health information. It covers most doctors, clinics, hospitals and pharmacies.<Cite id="hhs-hipaa-your-rights" /> Apart from your care and some cases the rule lists, your doctor needs your written permission to share it.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-506,ecfr-45-164-508,ecfr-45-164-512" /> A doctor may tell a family member or friend who helps with your care what they need to know for that, unless you object.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-510" /> You can see your record and get a copy, usually within 30 days.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-524" /> By law, your doctor must explain your condition and your treatment choices, including other options. You may refuse a treatment.<Cite id="medlineplus-informed-consent-adults" />
- **United Kingdom.** Doctors have a legal and an ethical duty to protect what you tell them.<Cite id="gmc-confidentiality-duties" /> The duty is not absolute: a doctor may share information with your consent, when the law requires it, or to protect people from serious harm.<Cite id="gmc-confidentiality-framework" /> You have the right to take part in decisions, and to hear the benefits and harms of each option, including doing nothing.<Cite id="gmc-2020-consent-seven-principles" /> In England, the NHS Constitution gives you the right to see your health records and to have mistakes corrected.<Cite id="nhs-constitution-england" />
- **Canada.** Provinces and territories have their own laws that protect health information.<Cite id="opc-privacy-laws-summary" /> The Canadian Medical Association's code of ethics asks doctors to keep your information confidential and share it only for your care, unless you agree or a law says otherwise. It also asks them to help you weigh the reasonable options, and to give you a copy of your record when you ask. Alberta's medical regulator requires its doctors to follow this code. We did not check the other provinces' rules.<Cite id="cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics" />
- **Australia.** Privacy law has strict rules on how a health service collects, uses and shares your health information. It also gives you a general right to ask to see it. Public hospitals are covered by state or territory laws.<Cite id="oaic-health-information,oaic-handling-health-information,oaic-access-health-information" /> The Medical Board's code asks doctors to keep your information confidential unless the law requires or allows them to share it, and to get your informed consent.<Cite id="mba-good-medical-practice-2020" />

</InDetail>

## What the law lets your doctor prescribe

<Lead question="Can my doctor prescribe BPC-157 or a similar peptide?">Usually not. Doctors can prescribe approved medicines. The other ways are rare, and each country has its own.</Lead>

<Diagram id="prescribing-routes" caption="The ways a doctor can get a medicine to you, from common to rare. Rules differ by country.">

| Route | Is it possible? | Read more |
|---|---|---|
| An approved medicine, for its approved use | Yes, in all four countries. This is the normal route, through a pharmacy.<Cite id="usc-21-353,uk-hmr-2012-reg-214,ca-fdr-c01-041-rx-sale,au-tga-prescription-medicines-overview" /> | [Prescription](/en/legal-routes/#prescription) |
| An approved medicine, for another use (off-label) | Sometimes, under each country's own rules. It only works for medicines that are already approved.<Cite id="fda-off-label-use,gmc-2021-prescribing-unlicensed,ca-hc-2019-investigational-use-marketed-drugs,au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" /> | [Off-label](/en/legal-routes/#off-label) |
| A medicine a pharmacy makes for one patient | The rules differ. In the US, a pharmacy may use only allowed ingredients, and BPC-157 is not one of them.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,ecfr-21-216-23" /> A UK doctor can ask for an unlicensed "special" only for a medical need that no licensed medicine meets.<Cite id="mhra-gn14-specials" /> | [US compounding](/en/legal-routes/#us-compounding), [UK specials](/en/legal-routes/#uk-specials) |
| A medicine that is not approved, for one patient | Rare. In the US, the company must agree to supply it.<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients" /> In Australia, the TGA says a request for a peptide should be backed by studies in people, of that peptide and for that use.<Cite id="au-tga-sas-individual-patients,au-tga-peptides-health-practitioners" /> | [Legal routes](/en/legal-routes/) |
| A medicine in a clinical trial | Only as part of a trial. | [Trials](/en/legal-routes/#trials) |
| An online prescription without a proper check | No. In the UK, a doctor must be able to assess you safely before prescribing. In Australia, Ahpra says a brief online consultation is not enough for an unapproved medicine.<Cite id="gmc-2021-deciding-safe-to-prescribe,au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" /> | [Prescription](/en/legal-routes/#prescription) |

</Diagram>

So a doctor cannot simply write a prescription for BPC-157, TB-500 or KPV. None of them is an approved medicine in the US, the UK, Canada or Australia.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23" /> Health Canada has not authorised any of them.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> To ask your doctor about checks or an approved option, see [legal routes to treatment](/en/legal-routes/) for your country.

## For professionals who keep getting asked

<Lead question="I am a doctor, physio or trainer. How do I respond?">Without judgement. Listen, give facts, point to safer options, and keep the door open.</Lead>

| Step | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1. Ask openly | "Are you using anything, or thinking about it?" Without judgement, so the answer can be honest. |
| 2. Find out what and why | Which product, from where, for which goal. |
| 3. Give facts, not fear | What is known and not known about the evidence and the product. Each [compound page](/en/compounds/) on this site uses the same structure. |
| 4. Point to safer options | An approved treatment for the goal. For people who go ahead: clean steps, checks and the warning signs. |
| 5. Keep the door open | Say they can come back, especially if something goes wrong. |

Professional bodies have started to say something about this. UK guidance asks doctors who prescribe to take a history that includes medicines bought online.<Cite id="gmc-2021-deciding-safe-to-prescribe" /> In April 2026, Alberta's medical regulator asked doctors to ask patients about injectables they were not prescribed, watch for complications and report side effects to Health Canada.<Cite id="ca-cpsa-2026-04-injectable-peptides" /> In September 2026, Ahpra in Australia said a patient wanting peptides does not mean they need them.<Cite id="au-ahpra-2026-09-peptide-prescribing" />

<InDetail title="For doctors: rules and resources that apply">

- **Shared decisions.** UK guidance gives patients the right to take part in decisions, and asks doctors to discuss the benefits and harms of the options, including doing nothing.<Cite id="gmc-2020-consent-seven-principles" /> If you will not provide what a patient asks for, explain your reasons, explore other options and mention their right to a second opinion.<Cite id="gmc-2020-consent-paras-48-49" /> The Canadian Medical Association's code and the Medical Board of Australia's code say the same about options, consent and second opinions.<Cite id="cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,mba-good-medical-practice-2020" />
- **Unlicensed and off-label prescribing.** In the UK, prescribe outside the licence only for medical reasons, when the evidence is sufficient, and tell the patient.<Cite id="gmc-2021-prescribing-unlicensed" /> In Australia, use an off-label, unapproved or compounded medicine only when no ARTG medicine is available or suitable and the evidence supports it. "Patient demand alone is not a valid reason to prescribe a medicine."<Cite id="au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" />
- **Side effects** can be reported to FDA MedWatch, the Yellow Card scheme, Health Canada and the TGA.<Cite id="fda-medwatch,mhra-yellow-card,ca-hc-medeffect-reporting,au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers" /> The FDA, Health Canada, Alberta's medical regulator and the TGA also ask for reports about unapproved products. Australian prescribers must report side effects of unapproved or compounded medicines to the TGA.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,ca-cpsa-2026-04-injectable-peptides,au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers,au-ahpra-prescribing-unapproved-guidance" />
- **The wider picture.** In 2014 the Endocrine Society, a medical society, noted that most users of performance-enhancing drugs are not athletes but people who lift weights, and that the health risks are greatly underappreciated. It asked doctors and policymakers to treat this as a public health problem.<Cite id="pope-2014-ped-endocrine-statement" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="For trainers and physiotherapists: where the line is">

- **Selling or handing over a medicine that is not approved** is against the law in all four countries, also to clients.<Cite id="usc-21-355,uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences" /> In Canadian law, "sell" includes handing out for free.<Cite id="ca-fda-s2-definitions" /> Whether a product sold "for research" counts as a medicine is explained on [research use only](/en/legal/research-use-only/).
- **Giving injections.** In the UK, only a prescriber, or someone acting on their instructions, may inject another person with a prescription-only medicine.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-214" /> We did not check this rule in the other countries.
- **Recommending a product or a seller** means vouching for something no regulator has checked. In Australia, it is generally against the law to advertise unapproved goods or prescription-only medicines to the public, and the TGA says influencers and others may be held to account.<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> Health Canada says a message about an unauthorised product can count as advertising, even in an educational setting.<Cite id="ca-hc-advertising-distinction-2023" />
- **What you can do:** listen without judgement, share facts from independent sources, and suggest that the client talks to a doctor.
- **If an injury does not heal as expected**, or a client has a [warning sign](/en/safety/warning-signs/), send them to a doctor.

</InDetail>
