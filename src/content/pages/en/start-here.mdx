<Lead question="Who is this page for?">For anyone who is thinking about a peptide, or already uses one. We do not judge you.</Lead>

This page does not tell you what to do. It gives you six things to know, in the order you need them. It gives no doses and names no sellers. Every fact links to its source, so you can check it.

## 1. Most have never been tested properly in people

<Lead question="Have these peptides been tested in people?">Most have not. Only a few, such as semaglutide, went through large trials.</Lead>

<Compare title="One approved peptide was tested in thousands of people. Three others, not approved, were not." detail="Semaglutide compared with BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV. Checked on 24 October 2026.">

| | Semaglutide (an approved medicine) | BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV (experimental) |
|---|---|---|
| Tested in large, fair trials in people? | <Verdict is="yes" /> One weight trial alone had 1,961 adults.<Cite id="wilding-2021-step-1" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No study of KPV, or of TB-500 alone, in people is published. BPC-157 had two small trials, known only from short conference abstracts.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> |
| Side effects counted, with how often they happen? | <Verdict is="yes" /> They are on the official label, with how often each one happens.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No proper study has counted them.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> |
| Safety followed over years? | <Verdict is="yes" /> One trial followed 17,604 adults for more than three years on average.<Cite id="lincoff-2023-select" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> The formal safety tests of BPC-157 with daily doses, in rats and dogs, lasted 28 days.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> |
| Benefits and risks weighed by a regulator? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Before it was approved in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia.<Cite id="fda-drugsatfda-wegovy,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-a-2026-10-24,ca-pm-wegovy-2026-10-02,au-artg-wegovy-356286" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> None of them is an approved medicine in these four countries.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23" /> |

</Compare>

"Never tested properly" does not mean "certain to harm you". It means nobody knows. Nobody knows if it works in people. Nobody knows which side effects it causes, or how often. Nobody knows what months of use do. Most research on these three peptides was done in cells, rats and mice, not in people.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" />

<InDetail title="Why animal results are not enough">

A result in rats is a reason to test something in people. It is not proof that it works in people. Lab results also often fail when other scientists repeat them. Scientists at one company could confirm only 6 of 53 key cancer studies.<Cite id="begley-2012-preclinical-standards,errington-2021-replicability" /> See [the translation gap](/en/evidence/the-translation-gap/).

Even after trials in people have started, most attempts to bring a medicine to patients fail. A study counted each medicine for one illness as one attempt. Only about 1 in 7 of these attempts ended in approval.<Cite id="wong-2019-clinical-trial-success" />

<KeyNumber title="Most attempts that reach trials in people never lead to an approved medicine" value="13.8%" label="of drug programmes that started human trials were approved in the end: about 1 in 7" counted="41,040 industry drug programmes (one drug for one illness) with trials from 2000 to 2015 (Wong 2019)" source="wong-2019-clinical-trial-success" />

For KPV, the FDA found no study in people at all. For TB-500 it found no human data. Two small reports gave people TB-500, or a related "TB4", only together with other substances.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,froum-2026-nerve-cases,lee-2021-knee" />

BPC-157 has been studied in people a little. In the 2000s, two small controlled trials tested it as an enema. One was in 32 healthy men, one in 53 people with bowel inflammation (ulcerative colitis). The results exist only as conference abstracts: short summaries for a meeting.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> Four small reports on 33 people in total were published later. None had a comparison group. In two of them, some or all people also got other substances.<Cite id="lee-2021-knee,lee-2024-interstitial-cystitis,lee-2025-intravenous,froum-2026-nerve-cases" /> See [BPC-157](/en/compounds/bpc-157/), [TB-500](/en/compounds/tb-500/) and [KPV](/en/compounds/kpv/).

</InDetail>

## 2. The vial is not the molecule

<Lead question="Is a vial from outside a pharmacy the same as the medicine?">No. No regulator has checked what is in it, how much, or whether it is free of germs.</Lead>

The picture shows what may be wrong with a vial that did not come from a pharmacy.

<Infographic id="online-vial-risks" />

This is true even when the name is right. Semaglutide is an approved medicine. But "research semaglutide" sold online is not that medicine: no regulator has checked it.<Cite id="mhra-glp1-what-you-need-to-know,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" /> Clean technique cannot fix what is inside a vial. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

<InDetail title="What can be wrong, with real examples">

- **The wrong amount.** In 2026, researchers in Australia tested three products sold as retatrutide. They held from about half to almost twice the amount on the label.<Cite id="piatkowski-2026-retatrutide-product-testing" />
- **The wrong substance.** In 2024, Australia's medicines regulator found fake pens labelled as Ozempic that held insulin, not semaglutide. One person had a life-threatening reaction.<Cite id="au-tga-2024-09-counterfeit-ozempic-pens" /> Insulin can make blood sugar fall too low.<Cite id="nhs-low-blood-sugar" /> Someone who thinks they are injecting semaglutide does not expect that.
- **Germs before the vial is opened.** In the United States in 2012, injections made by one pharmacy were contaminated with a fungus. Fungus was found in vials that had never been opened. By July 2013, 749 people were ill and 61 had died.<Cite id="smith-2013-fungal-methylprednisolone" /> Careful technique by the people who gave the injections could not have prevented it.

Health Canada warns that unapproved peptide products can hold too much, too little or none of the active ingredient. They can also hold dirt such as solvents, heavy metals or bits of glass and plastic.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="What you can check yourself, and what you cannot">

You can read the label and any paper that came with the product. You cannot see what is inside the vial, or how much of it. Products from outside the legal supply chain have not been checked for quality by a regulator.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025" />

<Checklist>

- **Is there a lab report?** It is often called a certificate of analysis. It lists the tests a lab did, and their results.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **Does it test for germs and bacterial toxins?** For anything injected, the FDA calls three tests key: no germs (sterility), no bacterial toxins (endotoxin) and no loose particles.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing" />
- **Does it give more than purity?** Purity alone does not say which impurities are there, or how much peptide is in the vial.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing" />
- **Does the name match exactly?** One name can cover different salt forms, or even a different substance.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing" />

</Checklist>

The FDA looked at certificates for BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV. Most of the ones it found gave little more than a purity result. None showed a test for germs.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> Not all lab reports tell you the same. Our page on [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/) shows what a lab report can and cannot tell you, from the most useful kind to the least, and what each one leaves unknown.

</InDetail>

## 3. The law in plain words

<Lead question="Is it legal?">Selling an unapproved medicine is against the law in all four countries. BPC-157 is not approved.</Lead>

<Diagram id="law-four-countries" caption="The short answer in each country, checked on 24 October 2026. Each country page has the details.">

| Country | The short answer | Read more |
|---|---|---|
| United States | A new medicine may not be sold until the FDA approves it. BPC-157 is not approved, and US pharmacies may not use it to make medicines.<Cite id="usc-21-355,usc-21-353a,ecfr-21-216-23,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> | [US law](/en/legal/us/) |
| United Kingdom | A medicine needs a UK licence before it is sold or supplied. BPC-157 has none.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23" /> | [UK law](/en/legal/uk/) |
| Canada | A new drug may not be sold until Health Canada authorises it. Health Canada has not authorised BPC-157.<Cite id="ca-food-and-drug-regulations,ca-fdr-c08-001-new-drug,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" /> | [Canadian law](/en/legal/ca/) |
| Australia | A peptide sold for a health purpose must be on the national register (the ARTG). BPC-157 is not. It is also a prescription-only medicine.<Cite id="au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides,au-tga-2026-04-peptide-responsibilities,au-poisons-standard-2026-10" /> | [Australian law](/en/legal/au/) |

</Diagram>

A licence to sell a medicine is called a marketing authorisation. Sellers often write "for research use only" on their products. That does not make a product legal, and it does not make it fit to inject.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" /> We do not give legal advice. For the legal ways to get a medicine, see [legal routes to treatment](/en/legal-routes/).

<InDetail title="An approved peptide and an unapproved one, side by side">

<Compare title="An approved peptide comes on prescription from a pharmacy. An unapproved one has no normal legal route." detail="Wegovy (semaglutide) and BPC-157 in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia, checked on 24 October 2026.">

| | An approved peptide medicine (for example Wegovy) | An unapproved peptide (for example BPC-157) |
|---|---|---|
| Licensed to be sold as a medicine? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Approved in all four countries.<Cite id="fda-drugsatfda-wegovy,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-a-2026-10-24,ca-pm-wegovy-2026-10-02,au-artg-wegovy-356286" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Not approved in any of the four.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23" /> |
| A legal way to get it? | <Verdict is="yes" /> With a prescription, from a pharmacy.<Cite id="usc-21-353,uk-hmr-2012-reg-214,ca-fdr-c01-041-rx-sale,au-tga-prescription-medicines-overview" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No normal way. The few exceptions, such as a clinical trial, are narrow. See [legal routes](/en/legal-routes/).<Cite id="usc-21-355,uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences" /> |
| Allowed in competitive sport? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Semaglutide is not banned. The anti-doping agency keeps track of its use.<Cite id="wada-2026-monitoring-program" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Banned at all times.<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en" /> |

</Compare>

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="What the law says in each country, in more detail">

- **United States.** No new drug may be sold without FDA approval.<Cite id="usc-21-355" /> A pharmacy may make a medicine for one patient only from allowed ingredients. BPC-157 is not one of them.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,ecfr-21-216-23" /> In July 2026 an FDA advisory committee voted on it, but that vote changed no rule (see the questions below).<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-503a-bulks-page" />
- **United Kingdom.** A medicine may not be sold, supplied or offered without a UK licence.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-46" /> A prescription-only medicine may be supplied only on a prescription.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-214" />
- **Canada.** No one may sell or advertise a new drug until Health Canada has approved it. In Canadian law, "sell" includes giving away.<Cite id="ca-food-and-drug-regulations,ca-fdr-c08-001-new-drug,ca-fda-s2-definitions" /> Health Canada says that peptides are "generally regulated as prescription drugs" in Canada.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" />
- **Australia.** BPC-157, TB-500 and many other peptides are in Schedule 4 of the national Poisons Standard. Schedule 4 means "prescription only medicine".<Cite id="au-poisons-standard-2026-10" /> Each state and territory decides whether having one without a prescription is an offence. In Victoria it is, for any Schedule 4 medicine. New South Wales has a shorter list, which includes TB-500. We did not check the other states and territories.<Cite id="au-vic-dpcs-act-1981,au-nsw-ptg-act-1966,au-nsw-health-s4d" />

{/* residue-ok: Ireland, approvals for the whole EU apply there (content brief, "What may stay" 4) */}
**Ireland and New Zealand.** In Ireland, it is illegal to sell prescription medicines online.<Cite id="ie-hpra-buying-prescription-medicines-online" /> Medicines approved for the whole European Union are approved in Ireland too.<Cite id="ie-hpra-licensing-procedures" /> In New Zealand, BPC-157 and several other peptides are classified as prescription medicines.<Cite id="nz-gazette-2026-sl4278-classification" /> See [Ireland and New Zealand](/en/legal/ireland-and-new-zealand/).

</InDetail>

## 4. The real promise, and the real uncertainty

<Lead question="Is there real promise?">Yes, for some. A few peptide medicines work well. For many others, the promise is only in animals.</Lead>

<Diagram id="evidence-ladder-start-here" caption="How far six peptides have come, from the lab to an approved medicine. Checked on 24 October 2026.">

| Peptide | How far it has come | What that means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Semaglutide | Large trials in people, then approved in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia<Cite id="wilding-2021-step-1,fda-drugsatfda-wegovy,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-a-2026-10-24,ca-pm-wegovy-2026-10-02,au-artg-wegovy-356286" /> | Effects and side effects are well known. A doctor can prescribe it. |
| Tirzepatide | Large trials in people, then approved in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia<Cite id="fda-drugsatfda-mounjaro,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-a-2026-10-24,ca-sbd-mounjaro,au-artg-mounjaro-439430" /> | Effects and side effects are well known. A doctor can prescribe it. |
| Retatrutide | Large trials in people, but not approved in any of the four countries yet<Cite id="jastreboff-2026-triumph-1,sec-lilly-2026-q2-results,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-a-2026-10-24,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,au-tga-2026-04-peptide-responsibilities" /> | Weight loss in trials was large, but no regulator has weighed the results yet. In practice, it is available almost only inside a trial.<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients,ctgov-nct07629401" /> |
| BPC-157 | Mostly rats; a few small reports in people<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> | Nobody knows if it works in people, or what harm it can do. |
| TB-500 | Lab and animal studies; no study of TB-500 alone in people<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing" /> | Nobody knows if it works in people, or what harm it can do. |
| KPV | Cells and mice only<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> | Nobody knows if it works in people, or what harm it can do. |

</Diagram>

Well-tested peptide medicines can do a lot. In a fair trial of 1,961 adults with obesity or overweight, without diabetes, <Claim level="human-controlled">people on semaglutide lost about 15% of their weight on average after 68 weeks, against 2.4% with a dummy injection</Claim>.<Cite id="wilding-2021-step-1" /> For BPC-157, <Claim level="animal">many rat studies report faster healing</Claim>. But most of these studies come from one group in Zagreb, and BPC-157 has not been tested properly in people.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" /> See [the Croatian lab](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/).

<InDetail title="Slow rules have real costs, and so do skipped trials">

Waiting for trials has real costs. Some people suffer while the evidence is collected. And an approved medicine is not always easy to get. In England, for example, the NHS must pay for tirzepatide for some adults with obesity. But NICE, which advises the NHS, accepted that bringing it in may take up to 12 years, starting with the people in most need.<Cite id="nice-ta1026-tirzepatide" />

Skipping trials has costs too. Without a fair trial with a comparison group, nobody can tell which harms a product causes, or how often. Without a check on quality, nobody can be sure what is in the product. Our [position on reform](/en/the-case-for-reform/) says what we think should change. That page is an opinion, kept apart from the facts here.

Retatrutide shows both sides. In a fair trial of 2,339 adults with obesity, without diabetes, <Claim level="human-controlled">average weight loss after 80 weeks was 17.6% to 25.0%, depending on the dose, against 3.9% with a dummy injection</Claim>.<Cite id="jastreboff-2026-triumph-1" /> In August 2026 its maker said it planned to ask the FDA for approval in early 2027.<Cite id="sec-lilly-2026-q2-results" /> Until a regulator approves it, a product sold as retatrutide is an unchecked product. See [retatrutide](/en/compounds/retatrutide/).

</InDetail>

## 5. Bring a doctor into it

<Lead question="Should I tell a doctor?">Yes. A doctor can check your risks, look for an approved option, and help if something goes wrong.</Lead>

<Diagram id="what-a-doctor-can-do" caption="Five things a doctor can do for you, even if they disagree with your plan">

| A doctor can | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Check whether you should not use these products at all | Some health problems and medicines raise the risk. For example, semaglutide together with insulin can make blood sugar fall too low.<Cite id="fda-label-wegovy" /> See [before you consider a peptide](/en/safety/before-you-consider/). |
| Tell you about approved options for your goal | Doctors must explain the risks and the other options, so that you can decide.<Cite id="medlineplus-informed-consent-adults,gmc-2020-consent-seven-principles,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,mba-good-medical-practice-2020" /> |
| Do checks, such as blood tests | Checks show how your body is doing, not only how you feel. See [monitoring](/en/safety/monitoring/). |
| Treat side effects early | A skin infection called cellulitis needs antibiotics. Without them, it can spread.<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis" /> See the [warning signs](/en/safety/warning-signs/). |
| Report a side effect | Anyone can report a side effect to the medicines regulator. In Australia, this includes unapproved products.<Cite id="fda-medwatch,mhra-yellow-card,ca-hc-medeffect-reporting,au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers" /> |

</Diagram>

In all four countries, doctors must keep what you tell them private, apart from a few exceptions set by law.<Cite id="hhs-hipaa-your-rights,gmc-confidentiality-duties,gmc-confidentiality-framework,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,mba-good-medical-practice-2020,oaic-handling-health-information" /> In the UK, a doctor who prescribes should ask about medicines bought online, and encourage patients to be open about them.<Cite id="gmc-2021-deciding-safe-to-prescribe" /> Our page on [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/) has words you can use.

## Questions people ask

<Lead question="What do people often ask?">Here are short, honest answers to common questions. Each one links to more.</Lead>

<Faq>

<FaqItem q="Is it legal to get peptides online?">

Selling an unapproved medicine is against the law in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia.<Cite id="usc-21-355,uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences" /> Ordering one for yourself is treated differently in each country:

- **US:** in most cases, it is illegal to import a medicine for your own use.<Cite id="fda-personal-importation" />
- **UK:** you need no import licence for your own use. But Border Force can seize parcels it thinks are too large for one person.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-17,mhra-foi-22-431-personal-import-seizures" />
- **Canada:** residents generally may not bring in prescription drugs, also not by mail. Health Canada says peptides are generally prescription drugs, and it works with the border agency to stop such parcels.<Cite id="ca-hc-gui-0116-personal-import,ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides" />
- **Australia:** a prescription-only medicine can be imported only with an Australian doctor's prescription or written authority.<Cite id="au-tga-personal-importation-scheme,au-tg-regulations-1990-sch-5-item-1" />

Your country's page explains more: [US](/en/legal/us/), [UK](/en/legal/uk/), [Canada](/en/legal/ca/), [Australia](/en/legal/au/).

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem q="Did the FDA approve BPC-157 in 2026?">

No. On 23 July 2026, an advisory committee of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) voted on BPC-157. As reported from the public meeting, it voted 8 to 6, with one abstention, to recommend BPC-157 for the "503A list". That list names substances that US pharmacies may use to make medicines on prescription. The committee looked at seven peptides. By the same reports, it recommended six: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax and epitalon. The vote was only advice, and it went against the FDA staff's own advice.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-questions,fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" /> US pharmacies may not legally make these peptides until the FDA accepts the advice and makes a formal rule.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" /> None of them is an approved medicine in the US, the UK, Canada or Australia. Semax is registered as a medicine in Russia.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-semax-briefing,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,mhra-products-search-status-d-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-canada-legal-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-d-2026-10-23" /> A listing would not make them approved medicines. It says nothing about whether they work. It changes nothing in the UK, Canada or Australia, where each country's own law applies.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1,usc-21-353a,uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences" />

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem q="My product came with a lab report. Does that prove it is clean?">

No. A report shows only the tests that were done, on the sample that was tested. The FDA looked at certificates for BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV. Most of the ones it found gave little more than a purity result, and none showed a test for germs.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing" /> "Clean" would need a germ test (sterility) and a toxin test (endotoxin) for that batch.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing" /> Even then, a report cannot show how your vial was stored or shipped. See [what you can check yourself](#2-the-vial-is-not-the-molecule), and [what a lab report can and cannot tell you](/en/safety/product-risk/) on our page on product risk.

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem q="Will my doctor tell the police or my employer?">

Usually not. In all four countries, doctors must keep what you tell them private.<Cite id="hhs-hipaa-your-rights,gmc-confidentiality-duties,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,mba-good-medical-practice-2020" /> They may share it with your consent, when a law requires it, or in rare cases to protect someone from serious harm.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-512,gmc-confidentiality-framework,cpsa-cma-code-of-ethics,oaic-handling-health-information" /> In the US, for example, outside your care and a few cases set by law, your doctor needs your written permission to share your health information.<Cite id="ecfr-45-164-506,ecfr-45-164-508" /> See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/#your-rights-privacy-and-consent).

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem q="I compete in sport. Can I use peptides?">

Most unapproved peptides are banned at all times.<Cite id="wada-2026-list-en" /> If a banned substance is found in your body, that counts as a doping offence, even if you did not mean to take it.<Cite id="wada-code-2021,wada-code-2027" /> A product with the wrong contents can end a career. See [sport and doping](/en/legal/sport-and-doping/).

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem q="Why does this site not give doses?">

Because for unapproved peptides, nobody knows a safe or useful amount for people. For BPC-157, for example, the FDA called the few studies in people small and short, with amounts that were probably just a first try.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> Amounts used in rats cannot simply be scaled up to people. See [the translation gap](/en/evidence/the-translation-gap/) and our [editorial policy](/en/about/editorial-policy/).

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem q="Where can I report a side effect?">

To your country's medicines regulator. Anyone can report, also if you are not sure the product caused it:

- **US:** FDA MedWatch.<Cite id="fda-medwatch" />
- **UK:** the Yellow Card scheme.<Cite id="mhra-yellow-card" />
- **Canada:** Health Canada (the Canada Vigilance Program).<Cite id="ca-hc-medeffect-reporting" />
- **Australia:** the TGA. Its form covers unapproved products too.<Cite id="au-tga-report-adverse-events-consumers" />
- **Ireland:** the HPRA. **New Zealand:** Medsafe, through its CARM form.<Cite id="ie-hpra-report-side-effect,nz-medsafe-report-a-problem" />

The links are on [if something goes wrong](/en/safety/if-something-goes-wrong/). If you feel unwell, see a doctor as well.

</FaqItem>
