So each page works differently. For an approved medicine, it reports what the label and the trials show. For the others, it reports what was studied, in which animals or people, and what is still unknown. A medicine approved in only some of the four countries is in the approved group too, and its page says where. Each country decides for itself: an approval in one country does not let a doctor in another prescribe it in the normal way.<Cite id="usc-21-355,uk-hmr-2012-reg-46,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tga-how-we-regulate-peptides" /> Medicines approved only by other countries' regulators have their own group. Products that are not peptides are listed under "Other".

An approved name does not make every product with that name approved. A vial sold online as "research semaglutide" is not the approved medicine.<Cite id="mhra-glp1-what-you-need-to-know,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" /> See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

## How to read the evidence profile

<Lead question="What does the evidence profile on each card show?">Four separate answers about the research. We never add them up into one score.</Lead>

| Part of the profile | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Human clinical evidence | How far has it been tested in people? |
| Preclinical volume | How much lab and animal research exists? |
| Source independence | How many separate research groups produced it? |
| Safety characterisation | How well do we know the harms in people? |

The parts are kept apart on purpose. Many animal studies from one lab are not the same as one good trial in people. See [how we grade evidence](/en/evidence/evidence-grading/), and [the Croatian lab](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/) for an example.
