<Lead question="Why a page about one research group?">One group did most of the research on BPC-157. Other labs have rarely checked its results.</Lead>

<EvidenceGrade compound="bpc-157" foldWhy />

BPC-157 has an unusual scientific history. Its evidence base is large, more than two hundred indexed papers over three decades. But it was built almost entirely by one research group: Predrag Sikirić, Sven Seiwerth and their colleagues at the School of Medicine of the University of Zagreb. They first described the peptide in the early 1990s.<Cite id="sikiric-1993-bpc-origin" /> This page sets out how dominant that group is, why that matters for how much we can trust the findings, and what would change the picture.

## Counting the literature

<Lead question="How much of the research comes from this one group?">Most of it. About 85 in 100 lab and animal papers come from the Zagreb group.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Three in four records name the group's leader as an author" value="175 of 235" label="PubMed records on BPC-157 list Sikirić as an author (74%)" counted="Every PubMed record on BPC-157 from 1992 to 2026 (our search, 20 October 2026)" source="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" />

<KeyNumber title="Before 2016 it was nearly nine in ten" value="85 of 96" label="of the records from before 2016 name Sikirić as an author (89%)" counted="The PubMed records on BPC-157 published from 1992 to 2015" source="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" />

<KeyNumber title="Most lab and animal studies come from the group" value="about 140 of 160" label="primary lab and animal papers come from the Zagreb group: about 85%" counted="The 177 records left after we set aside reviews, editorials, comments, letters and errata" source="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" />

That is well above the 70% threshold our rubric uses for "single-group dominant".

<InDetail title="How we counted, and who wrote the 177 records">

On 20 October 2026 we searched PubMed for every record that mentions BPC 157, or its code names PL 14736 and bepecin, in the title or abstract, or that is indexed under the substance "BPC 157".<Cite id="pubmed-bpc157-count-2026-10-20" /> We downloaded all records and checked the author lists and publication types with the National Library of Medicine's own tools. Each record outside the Zagreb group we then classified by hand.

The search returned **235 records**, published from 1992 to 2026. **Sikirić is an author on 175 of them (74%)**. Before 2016 the share was higher: 85 of 96 records (89%).

Reviews and commentaries inflate any count, so we then set aside everything PubMed tags as a review, editorial, comment, letter or erratum. That left 177 records, mostly primary studies:

<Diagram id="bpc-157-literature-by-source" caption="Who wrote the 177 PubMed records on BPC-157 that are not reviews. Our search of 20 October 2026.">

| Source | Records | What they are |
|---|---|---|
| Sikirić and/or Seiwerth as authors (Zagreb group, including collaborators elsewhere) | 140 | Almost all rat and mouse injury studies; some untagged reviews |
| Other Zagreb authors: the developer PLIVA (3) and one hospital department (1) | 4 | Animal and cell studies |
| Groups elsewhere: experimental studies in cells or animals | 19 | 17 with no Zagreb or PLIVA co-author, from about seven laboratories in China, Taiwan, Turkey, South Korea and the USA; 2 from Hungary co-authored with PLIVA staff |
| Groups elsewhere: human reports | 4 | Three small reports by one US clinician, and one dental case report |
| Groups elsewhere: other | 10 | Doping-detection methods (3), chemistry and production (3), commentaries or papers that mention it in passing (4) |

</Diagram>

Counted that way, the Zagreb group produced about 140 of roughly 160 primary experimental papers, about 85%.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Independent work is clustered too">

Independent work is also clustered. Five of the 17 independent experimental papers come from one laboratory in Taiwan, and five from one laboratory in Xi'an, China.<Cite id="chang-2011-tendon-fibroblasts,he-2022-pharmacokinetics" /> Collaborations add a further wrinkle. Papers by a gastroenterology group in South Korea, for example, include Sikirić as a co-author. We counted them as the group's work, because shared authorship means shared methods.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="What this count cannot tell you">

PubMed is not the whole literature. Conference abstracts, Croatian-language journals and studies indexed only in other databases are missing, and so is the developer's clinical programme, which survives only as abstracts.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> Author matching can miss name variants. Our split between "primary" and "review" relies partly on PubMed's tags, and some of the group's 140 records are review-like papers that PubMed did not tag. None of this changes the overall picture. It is why we give round numbers and describe the method.

</InDetail>

## Why volume is not the same as independence

<Lead question="Why does it matter that one group did most of the work?">Shared methods can make the same mistake every time. A second lab can catch it.</Lead>

<Compare title="Many studies from one lab are not many separate checks." detail="How repeats inside one research group differ from repeats by other laboratories.">

| | Many studies from one group | Studies from separate laboratories |
|---|---|---|
| Different people, animals and suppliers? | <Verdict is="no" /> The same people, injury models, scoring systems and source of peptide | <Verdict is="yes" /> Different people, animals, suppliers and habits |
| Would a hidden bias be caught? | <Verdict is="no" /> A bias would enter every study in the same direction | <Verdict is="yes" /> A second laboratory is unlikely to make the same mistake in the same direction |
| Can repeats tell a real effect from a shared bias? | <Verdict is="no" /> If there is a shared bias, repeats measure the bias again, very precisely<Cite id="ioannidis-2005-false-findings" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> If the effect appears again there, the most likely explanation is that it is real |

</Compare>

A bathroom scale that reads two kilograms heavy gives the same wrong answer however often you step on it. Only a second scale reveals the error.

<InDetail title="Replication is how science catches its own mistakes">

Any single experiment can be wrong for reasons that cannot be seen from inside it. Chance can produce a striking result in a small sample. A batch of reagent can be contaminated, or an animal strain can respond oddly. An outcome scored by someone who knows which animals were treated can drift towards the expected answer. A choice between several reasonable ways to analyse the data can tip a borderline result over the line.

A second laboratory with different people, animals, suppliers and habits is unlikely to make the same mistake in the same direction. If the effect appears again there, the most likely explanation is that it is real. That is why independent replication is the main error-correcting mechanism of experimental science.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Shared methods produce shared errors, and incentives are shared too">

Within one group, studies share far more than a topic. They use the same injury models and surgical techniques, the same scoring systems and the same source of peptide. The same people are trained the same way, and they share statistical habits and assumptions about how the peptide should behave. If any of these introduces a bias, it introduces it into every study in the same direction.

A hundred such studies then do not test the effect a hundred times. They measure the bias a hundred times, very precisely. John Ioannidis put it bluntly in 2005: in many fields, "claimed research findings may often be simply accurate measures of the prevailing bias".<Cite id="ioannidis-2005-false-findings" />

Incentives are shared too. A group whose reputation, funding and patents are tied to a compound has natural reasons to look hard for positive results and to publish them; Sikirić is named as an inventor on a 1998 international patent application for salts of the peptide.<Cite id="jozwiak-2025-review" /> This is not a special failing. It is true of every drug developer, and it is exactly why regulators rely on registered, independently monitored trials before they accept a claim.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Publication bias affects whole fields">

<KeyNumber title="Only 2 in 100 stroke papers in animals saw no clear effect" value="2%" label="of 525 publications reported no clear effect on the size of the stroke" counted="525 publications of animal stroke experiments, pooled from 16 systematic reviews (Sena 2010)" source="sena-2010-publication-bias" />

<KeyNumber title="A company could confirm only 6 of 53 key cancer studies" value="6 of 53" label="landmark lab studies of cancer could be confirmed by scientists at one company" counted="'landmark' preclinical cancer studies that scientists at one company tried to confirm (Begley 2012)" source="begley-2012-preclinical-standards" />

<KeyNumber title="Repeated effects were much smaller than the first ones" value="85%" label="smaller in the repeats than in the first papers, taking the middle value" counted="The positive effects in 50 experiments from high-profile cancer-biology papers, repeated systematically (Errington 2021)" source="errington-2021-replicability" />

Positive results are easier to publish than null ones, everywhere. A review of 525 animal stroke publications found that only 2% reported no significant effect. It estimated that about one in seven experiments went unpublished, and that publication bias alone accounted for roughly a third of the apparent benefit.<Cite id="sena-2010-publication-bias" /> When scientists at a company tried to confirm 53 "landmark" preclinical cancer studies, they succeeded with 6.<Cite id="begley-2012-preclinical-standards" /> A systematic replication of 50 experiments from high-profile cancer-biology papers found that effects first reported as positive were, on median, 85% smaller than the originals.<Cite id="errington-2021-replicability" /> Many animal studies also do not report randomisation or blinded assessment, the basic protections against these biases.<Cite id="macleod-2015-risk-of-bias" />

None of this is specific to Zagreb. But it shows what to expect when a large animal literature has not been independently repeated: some real effects, some overstated ones, and no way to tell them apart from inside the literature.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Breadth cuts both ways, and reviews are only echoes">

The group reports benefits from BPC-157 in an unusually wide range of conditions, from stomach ulcers and fistulas to tendon tears, nerve injury and cardiovascular damage.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> That breadth can be read in two ways. It might reflect a basic protective mechanism. Or it might reflect a consistent research method that tends to produce positive results in any model it touches. The existing literature cannot tell these apart. Independent replication can.

Recent years add another trap. Between 2024 and 2026 most new BPC-157 records were not from Zagreb. But more than half of those were reviews and commentaries, largely summarising the Zagreb data. A review that cites a study is an echo of it, not a second test.

</InDetail>

## What the independent work shows so far

<Lead question="What has independent work found?">Some effects in cells and in rats. Nothing has been confirmed in people.</Lead>

<Diagram id="bpc-157-independent-work" caption="What independent work has found so far. Nothing has been confirmed in people.">

| What was tested | Who tested it | What it showed |
|---|---|---|
| Signals in cells grown in a dish | One laboratory in Taiwan; two of its effects were later seen in Xi'an, China, and Ankara, Turkey (2026) | Plausible pro-healing signals |
| Toxicology and drug handling | One laboratory in Xi'an, China | Safety and handling, not whether it works |
| Gut damage in rats | An industry laboratory in the US (Parke-Davis), 1995 | Less chemically induced colon damage, with a dose–response |
| Tendon healing in rats | A group in Turkey, 2026: 32 rats in four groups, 8 given BPC-157 | Strength and tissue scores better, but not statistically significant; one collagen measure higher; 4 tendons per group in each analysis |
| People | The developer (its trial); one US clinician (three reports); a group of US dentists (one report, 2026) | The developer's trial was never published in full. Four small reports had no comparison group. |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="chang-2011-tendon-fibroblasts,hsieh-2017-vegfr2,hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos,zhang-2026-fbxo22,yildirim-2026-mammary-artery,xu-2020-preclinical-safety,he-2022-pharmacokinetics,veljaca-1995-colitis,bicer-2026-achilles,fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,poolton-2026-human-sr,froum-2026-nerve-cases" />

The independent studies are encouraging in places and sobering in others. So independent work supports the idea that BPC-157 does something to cells and to some rodent injuries. It has not confirmed how large the effects are, and it has not confirmed anything in people.

<InDetail title="Each independent finding, with its limits">

- **Cell signalling.** A laboratory in Taiwan found plausible pro-healing signals, mostly in cultured cells: <Claim level="in-vitro">more VEGF receptor 2, more nitric oxide, more activity in cell-migration proteins</Claim>.<Cite id="chang-2011-tendon-fibroblasts,hsieh-2017-vegfr2,hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos" /> Two of the same effects have since been reported by other groups, both in 2026. The laboratory in Xi'an saw <Claim level="in-vitro">more vessel-like tubes form in cultured human blood-vessel lining cells</Claim>.<Cite id="zhang-2026-fbxo22" /> A group in Ankara, Turkey, found that <Claim level="in-vitro">rings of human artery, left over from bypass surgery in 12 men, relaxed in a dish, mostly through nitric oxide</Claim>, as the Taiwanese rat arteries had.<Cite id="yildirim-2026-mammary-artery,hsieh-2020-src-cav1-enos" /> We found no independent repeat of the specific signalling pathways the Taiwanese laboratory described.
- **Toxicology and drug handling.** A laboratory in Xi'an produced the only formal toxicology and pharmacokinetic studies.<Cite id="xu-2020-preclinical-safety,he-2022-pharmacokinetics" /> These are useful, but they test safety and handling, not efficacy.
- **Gut protection.** An industry laboratory at Parke-Davis in the US reported in 1995 that <Claim level="animal">the peptide reduced chemically induced colon damage in rats, with a dose–response</Claim>. The abstract does not give the number of rats.<Cite id="veljaca-1995-colitis" />
- **Tendon healing.** The first independent test in living animals of the group's flagship tendon claim came from Turkey in 2026. It used a related but different model: 32 rats in four groups (8 given BPC-157) whose cut Achilles tendons were surgically repaired, while the Zagreb studies left them unrepaired. <Claim level="animal">Strength and overall tissue scores moved in the expected direction but were not statistically significant. One collagen measure was significantly higher</Claim>, but each analysis used only 4 tendons per group.<Cite id="bicer-2026-achilles" />
- **Humans.** The developer's randomised trial in ulcerative colitis, with 53 patients, was reported only as a meeting abstract and never published in full. Three small uncontrolled reports come from one US clinician.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,poolton-2026-human-sr" /> A fourth, on three dental patients given BPC-157 in a mixture with other drugs, came from a group of US dentists in 2026.<Cite id="froum-2026-nerve-cases" />

</InDetail>

## Being fair to the group

<Lead question="Does this mean the group did something wrong?">No. We found no sign of wrongdoing. This page describes the evidence, not the people.</Lead>

<Diagram id="what-single-group-dominance-means" caption="Who did the research tells you about the evidence. It is not a verdict on the people.">

| What this page says | What it does not say |
|---|---|
| Most BPC-157 research comes from one group | That the group did anything wrong |
| Other laboratories have rarely repeated its experiments | That the findings are false |
| How far the findings have been tested | Whether they are true |

</Diagram>

The volume of work is real. Over three decades the Zagreb group has run a very large number of experiments across many organ systems and published them in peer-reviewed journals, including established pharmacology and orthopaedic journals.<Cite id="staresinic-2003-achilles,klicek-2008-fistula,cerovecki-2010-ligament" /> Nothing on this page alleges misconduct, and nothing in our analysis suggests it.

Single-group dominance is a description of the evidence, not a verdict on the people who produced it. It tells you how far the findings have been tested, not whether they are true.

<InDetail title="How the group answers its critics, and why human evidence is missing">

The group also engages with its critics in print. When an independent review raised concerns about angiogenesis and tumours, the group published a detailed reply.<Cite id="sikiric-2025-comment,jozwiak-2025-review" /> Single-group dominance is not unusual for compounds that never reached late-stage development, and many medicines began in one laboratory. Nor is the missing human evidence simply the group's fault: in public records, the industrial development of PL 14736 never progressed beyond a phase 2 trial in the 2000s.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

</InDetail>

## What would change the picture

<Lead question="What would settle the question?">Other labs repeating the key tests, and trials in people, all published in full.</Lead>

<Diagram id="what-would-change-the-bpc-157-picture" caption="Five things would show whether the effects hold up. None of them needs new science.">

| What is needed | What it would add |
|---|---|
| Independent, pre-registered animal studies of the tendon, ligament and gut claims | Randomised and blinded tests, with the size set in advance and the peptide bought and analysed independently |
| Full publication of the old phase 2 trial data | The only controlled test of whether it works in people could be properly assessed |
| Registered human trials, reported in full whatever they find | One, in hamstring injury, is recruiting; results are expected after February 2027 |
| An identified and independently confirmed molecular target | Effects would become predictable and doses meaningful |
| Independent long-term toxicology | Including the carcinogenicity studies (tests of whether it causes cancer) that no one has yet published |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,ctgov-nct07437547" />

If these were done and the effects held up, BPC-157's evidence grade would rise, and this page would say so. If they shrank or vanished, that would be just as valuable to know. Until then, the honest reading is the one our grading gives: an extensive animal literature, single-group dominant, with almost no human data. The full picture is on the [BPC-157 page](/en/compounds/bpc-157/), and our grading method is explained under [evidence grading](/en/evidence/evidence-grading/).

<Callout type="tip" title="Reading any BPC-157 claim">
Ask four questions: in which species, how many subjects, which design, and from which group? A claim about people that rests on rat studies from one laboratory is a hypothesis, however many papers support it.
</Callout>

<InDetail title="Each of the five steps in full">

The fix is well known, and none of it requires new science:

- **Independent, pre-registered animal studies** of the key claims (tendon, ligament and gut healing). These should be randomised and blinded, use a sample size calculated in advance, and test several doses. Treatment should start after the injury is established, and the peptide should be bought and analysed independently. Ideally several laboratories would run the same study design in parallel.
- **Full publication of the old phase 2 trial data**, so the only controlled test of whether it works in people can be properly assessed.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />
- **Registered human trials reported in full whatever they find.** A randomised, placebo-controlled trial in hamstring injury is recruiting, with results expected after February 2027.<Cite id="ctgov-nct07437547" />
- **An identified and independently confirmed molecular target**, which would make effects predictable and doses meaningful.
- **Independent long-term toxicology**, including the carcinogenicity studies that no one has yet published.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" />

</InDetail>
