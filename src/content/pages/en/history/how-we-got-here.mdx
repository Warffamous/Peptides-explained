<Lead question="Why does a medicine need a licence before it is sold?">In the past, some medicines did great harm before anyone checked them.</Lead>

<Diagram id="three-checks-and-when" caption="Three questions every new medicine must answer before sale, and when US law first asked them.">

| Question | What it means | First required in the US |
|---|---|---|
| Is it safe enough? | Tests show the harms in normal use | 1938 |
| Does it work? | Well-controlled studies show the effect it claims | 1962 |
| Is it made properly? | Production under controls that keep its contents as declared | 1938 (formal manufacturing rules in 1962) |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="fda-history-milestones,us-fdc-act-1938,us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962,fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" />

Today a new medicine must answer these three questions before it can be approved in the US, the UK, Canada or Australia.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs,uk-hmr-2012-reg-58,ca-fdr-c08-004-notice-of-compliance,ca-food-and-drug-regulations,au-tg-act-artg-registration-listing" /> That was not always so. This page explains how the rules came about, what they protect against, and what they cost. Two disasters shaped them: a poisonous medicine in 1937 and thalidomide around 1960.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones,vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

## Before 1962: safe on paper, effect unproven

<Lead question="What did the law ask for before 1962?">In the US, from 1938, proof of safety. Nobody had to prove that a medicine worked.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="107 people died from one drug" value="107" label="people died, many of them children, after taking one drug" counted="Deaths from one US medicine dissolved in a poisonous solvent, 1937 (FDA history)" source="fda-history-milestones" />

The 1937 deaths led to the US Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938. From then on, a new drug had to be shown safe before marketing.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> Whether it did what its maker claimed was not checked.

<InDetail title="The 1938 law and its gap">

The medicine contained diethylene glycol, a poisonous solvent related to antifreeze. The law of 1906 did not ask for any information before a drug was sold, so nobody had to show it was safe first.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones,fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> The 1938 Act, approved on 25 June 1938, changed that. A company now had to send the FDA a new-drug application with reports of its safety tests and a description of how the drug was made. The FDA could refuse it if the tests were inadequate, if they did not show the drug was safe, or if the manufacturing controls could not keep the drug's identity, strength, quality and purity.<Cite id="us-fdc-act-1938" /> The law asked for "adequate tests by all methods reasonably applicable", but it did not say which tests.<Cite id="us-fdc-act-1938,junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" />

The gap was effect. Before 1962, US manufacturers did not have to prove to the FDA that their products worked.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> Senate hearings in the years before 1962 heard about "the sorry state of science supporting drug effectiveness" and exaggerated claims in labels and advertising, as a senior FDA official later put it.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" />

</InDetail>

## Thalidomide

<Lead question="What happened with thalidomide?">A sleeping pill taken early in pregnancy harmed more than 10,000 babies.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="More than 10,000 children were harmed" value="over 10,000" label="children born with severe birth defects, 1957–1962" counted="Children harmed by thalidomide worldwide, as estimated in a 2015 review of its history" source="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

<KeyNumber title="It was sold in dozens of countries" value="46" label="countries sold the drug, under different names" counted="Countries where the drug was marketed and distributed, from the same review" source="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

<KeyNumber title="Many of the most harmed babies died as infants" value="up to 40%" label="of the babies with the worst damage died in their first year" counted="Infant deaths among babies born with severe thalidomide damage, from the same review" source="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

{/* residue-ok: history (thalidomide) */}
Thalidomide went on sale in the late 1950s as a sedative and was soon given for morning sickness. In 1961 two doctors, Lenz in Germany and McBride in Australia, linked it to severe malformations in babies. Withdrawals began in November 1961, and by 1962 it was off the market in most of the world.<Cite id="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" /> In the US it had never been approved.<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history" />

<InDetail title="What went wrong, step by step">

{/* residue-ok: history (thalidomide) */}
Thalidomide was made by the German company Chemie Grünenthal and sold as Contergan in Germany, Softenon elsewhere in Europe and Distaval in the UK. It was advertised as free of risk, and doctors handed out free samples for morning sickness.<Cite id="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

The damage came from timing. The drug harmed the embryo in a short window early in pregnancy, between about day 20 and day 36 after fertilisation. Limbs, face, eyes, ears, heart and other organs could be affected, and many survivors live with severe disabilities today.<Cite id="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

The tests done before sale did not reveal the danger, and exactly what was tested is still debated. Mice, the usual species for screening drugs, are less sensitive to thalidomide than rabbits or primates. After the disaster, drug testing changed to use several species, and regulators developed systematic tests for toxic effects.<Cite id="vargesson-2015-thalidomide,kim-2011-thalidomide-review" /> The same lesson, that an animal result may not carry over to people, runs through our page on [the translation gap](/en/evidence/the-translation-gap/).

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Frances Kelsey and the US application">

In 1960 Frances Kelsey joined the FDA as one of a handful of medical officers. One of the first applications she was given was for thalidomide, already sold in dozens of countries. Despite constant pressure from the company, she refused to approve it because the evidence was inadequate. About a year later the link with birth defects was found, and the drug was never marketed in the US.<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history" />

The US did not escape entirely. The US sponsor had sent samples to thousands of doctors, who gave them to patients without telling them the drug was experimental. More than a dozen children with thalidomide damage are believed to have been born in the US as a result.<Cite id="junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" /> In 1962 President Kennedy gave Kelsey the highest award for a federal civil servant.<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history" />

</InDetail>

## 1962: proof that it works

<Lead question="What changed in 1962?">From 1962, US law asked for proof from well-controlled studies that a medicine works.</Lead>

<Compare title="After 1962, a new medicine had to show it works and be approved before sale." detail="What US law required of a new medicine before and after the Drug Amendments of 10 October 1962.">

| | Before 1962 (law of 1938) | After 1962 |
|---|---|---|
| Proof of safety before sale? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Required since 1938<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Still required<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> |
| Proof that it works? | <Verdict is="no" /> Not required<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Substantial evidence from adequate and well-controlled studies<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> |
| Must the FDA approve it first? | <Verdict is="no" /> Sales could start if the FDA did not act within 60 days<Cite id="us-fdc-act-1938,junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> The FDA must approve the application<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> |
| Must people in trials be told and asked? | <Verdict is="no" /> Not in the drug law; some were not even told<Cite id="junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> With exceptions<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> |
| Must older medicines show they work? | <Verdict is="no" /> Safety was enough<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> About 4,000 were re-assessed from 1966<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> |

</Compare>

The law defined "substantial evidence" as evidence from adequate and well-controlled investigations, including clinical ones, by qualified experts.<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> An FDA official later described this requirement as a radical change from what had gone before.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" />

<InDetail title="What 'adequate and well-controlled' means, from first principles">

People often get better or worse on their own, believing in a treatment can change how they feel, and an observer who knows who was treated can see what they expect. US regulations say a clinical study exists to "distinguish the effect of a drug from other influences" like these.<Cite id="ecfr-21-314-126" />

So a well-controlled study compares the treated group with a control group, such as a placebo or an established medicine. It assigns people to the groups by randomisation, as a rule, and uses blinding so that neither patients nor observers can tilt the result. Uncontrolled studies "are not acceptable as the sole basis" for a claim that a medicine works.<Cite id="ecfr-21-314-126" /> Our page on [how to read a study](/en/evidence/how-to-read-a-study/) explains each of these protections.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Consent, with the limits of 1962">

The 1962 law required investigators to tell people that they were receiving an experimental drug and to obtain their consent, or that of a representative. It also allowed exceptions where the investigator judged consent "not feasible" or not in the person's interest.<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> Later rules added more: a US policy of 1981, adopted across government in 1991 as the Common Rule, set requirements for documenting consent and for ethics review boards.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Re-checking the medicines already on the market">

The new rule also applied backwards. In 1966 the FDA asked the US National Academy of Sciences to evaluate the effectiveness of about 4,000 drugs approved on safety alone between 1938 and 1962.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> In 1970, during this review, new rules stated that "uncontrolled studies are not acceptable evidence to support claims of effectiveness".<Cite id="junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" />

</InDetail>

## The UK, Canada and Australia

<Lead question="How did the UK, Canada and Australia respond?">All three began to collect side-effect reports. The UK and Australia set up expert groups and new laws.</Lead>

<Diagram id="uk-canada-australia-after-thalidomide" caption="How three countries changed their rules after thalidomide.">

| Country | Soon after thalidomide | Later |
|---|---|---|
| United Kingdom | 1963: an expert committee to judge the tests and trials of new medicines, at first voluntary. 1964: the Yellow Card scheme for reports of side effects | 1968: the Medicines Act sets up a licensing system for medicines in law. 2003: today's regulator, the MHRA, is formed |
| Canada | 1962: thalidomide is taken off the market. A 1963 report counts 115 children harmed by it | 1965: reports of side effects are collected from this year on, today by Health Canada's Canada Vigilance Program. Today a new medicine needs substantial evidence that it works |
| Australia | 1961: an Australian doctor's letter links thalidomide to birth defects. 1963: an expert committee on the safety of medicines | 1964: a national scheme for side-effect reports starts. 1991: the Therapeutic Goods Act takes effect, run by the TGA |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="uk-hansard-1963-04-04-safety-of-drugs,uk-tna-mh171-csd-csm,uk-mhra-2014-yellow-card-50,uk-medicines-act-1968-enacted,uk-si-2003-1076-mhra-trading-fund,ca-hesa-2017-05-09-thalidomide-evidence,webb-1963-canadian-thalidomide,ca-hc-canada-vigilance,ca-fdr-c08-004-notice-of-compliance,mcbride-1961-thalidomide-letter,au-tga-mcewen-2007-history,au-tg-act-1989-asmade-details" />

Thalidomide was sold in the UK, Canada and Australia, and children were harmed there too.<Cite id="vargesson-2015-thalidomide,webb-1963-canadian-thalidomide,au-tga-mcewen-2007-history" /> The UK and Australia first set up expert committees on the safety of medicines. All three countries began to collect reports of side effects. The UK and Australia later wrote their licensing systems into new laws.<Cite id="uk-hansard-1963-04-04-safety-of-drugs,au-tga-mcewen-2007-history,uk-mhra-2014-yellow-card-50,ca-hc-canada-vigilance,uk-medicines-act-1968-enacted,au-tg-act-1989-asmade-details" />

<InDetail title="The United Kingdom: a committee, then a law">

In April 1963 the Minister of Health told Parliament, in a written answer, that the government accepted a recommendation for an independent expert Committee on Safety of Drugs. It would advise on whether the toxicity tests of a new drug were adequate before trials in people, and whether the trials were adequate before general use, and it would collect data about side effects found later. The scheme was voluntary, as the majority of the expert sub-committee had recommended; two members disagreed, saying a voluntary scheme would not meet the need. A review of drug law "with a view to legislation" was under way.<Cite id="uk-hansard-1963-04-04-safety-of-drugs" /> The committee was set up in 1963.<Cite id="uk-tna-mh171-csd-csm" />

In 1964, "in the wake of the thalidomide disaster", the Yellow Card scheme began to collect reports of suspected side effects, as an early warning of possible hazards.<Cite id="uk-mhra-2014-yellow-card-50" /> It was introduced by Sir Derrick Dunlop, who chaired the committee.<Cite id="uk-mhra-2015-yellow-card-digital" /> Anyone can still report a problem with a medicine this way.<Cite id="mhra-yellow-card" />

The Medicines Act 1968, which received Royal Assent on 25 October 1968, set up a licensing system for medicines in law: its Part II covers licences and certificates for medicinal products.<Cite id="uk-medicines-act-1968-enacted" /> Today the UK regulator may grant a licence only if the maker has shown that the medicine works, that its benefits outweigh its risks, and that its contents are as declared.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-58" /> Today's regulator, the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), was formed on 1 April 2003 by merging the Medicines Control Agency and the Medical Devices Agency.<Cite id="uk-si-2003-1076-mhra-trading-fund" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Canada: thalidomide withdrawn, then side-effect reports">

In Canada thalidomide was licensed as a prescription medicine and sold until 1962, when it was taken off the market.<Cite id="ca-hesa-2017-05-09-thalidomide-evidence" /> A 1963 report counted 115 children born in Canada in 1961 and 1962 with malformations linked to thalidomide taken by their mothers in early pregnancy. Eight were stillborn and 33 had died.<Cite id="webb-1963-canadian-thalidomide" />

Reports of suspected side effects have been collected in Canada since 1965. Today Health Canada does this through its Canada Vigilance Program, and both health professionals and patients can report.<Cite id="ca-hc-canada-vigilance" />

Today the approval of a new medicine in Canada, called a notice of compliance, needs reports of the tests that show its safety and "substantial evidence of the clinical effectiveness" for its intended use.<Cite id="ca-fdr-c08-004-notice-of-compliance" /> We found no official source we can cite for the year Canada first required this proof of effect, so this page does not give one.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Australia: a doctor's letter, then a national system">

In December 1961 William McBride, a doctor in New South Wales, wrote to the medical journal The Lancet. About 1.5% of all babies are born with abnormalities, he wrote, but he had seen multiple severe abnormalities in almost 20% of babies born to women given thalidomide in pregnancy.<Cite id="mcbride-1961-thalidomide-letter,au-tga-mcewen-2007-history" />

On 3 June 1963 the Australian Drug Evaluation Committee was appointed, a committee of independent experts to advise on the safety of medicines. A national scheme for reporting suspected side effects started in August 1964, and by December 1966 it had received 760 reports.<Cite id="au-tga-mcewen-2007-history" />

The national regulator was named the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) in August 1989, and the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 took effect on 15 February 1991.<Cite id="au-tga-mcewen-2007-history,au-tg-act-1989-asmade-details" /> Under it, a medicine must, with some exceptions, be on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG) before it is imported or supplied.<Cite id="au-tg-act-s19b-19d-offences" /> Before it registers a medicine, the TGA must judge whether its quality, safety and efficacy "have been satisfactorily established".<Cite id="au-tg-act-artg-registration-listing" />

</InDetail>

## What the checks give you

<Lead question="What do these rules give patients?">A known product, a tested effect, and risks that are watched after sale and written on the label.</Lead>

<Diagram id="what-each-check-protects-against" caption="What each check protects against.">

| Check | What it asks for | What it protects against |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Contents as declared; production under controls | The wrong substance, the wrong amount, contamination |
| Effect | Adequate and well-controlled studies | A medicine that does not work, or an effect that came from chance, placebo or bias |
| Safety | Animal tests and trials in people; refusal if the tests do not show safety | Harms that a small or uncontrolled test would miss |
| After sale | Reports of side effects; label updates | Rare harms that show up only when many people use it |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="us-fdc-act-1938,us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962,ecfr-21-314-126,fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs,uk-mhra-2014-yellow-card-50,ca-hc-canada-vigilance" />

The checks do not make a medicine harmless. They make its benefits and risks known, for one defined product. Rare harms often show up only after many people use a medicine, so reporting continues after sale and the label is updated.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs,uk-mhra-2014-yellow-card-50,ca-hc-canada-vigilance,au-tga-mcewen-2007-history" />

<InDetail title="Why the evidence belongs to a product, not to a name">

US regulations say that a medicine tested in a trial must be "standardized as to identity, strength, quality, purity, and dosage form to give significance to the results of the investigation".<Cite id="ecfr-21-314-126" /> The logic is simple: a trial measures what was in the trial. If a different product has a different content, amount or purity, the trial says little about it.

This is why a product from outside the legal supply chain that carries the name of an authorised medicine is not covered by that medicine's evidence. Its content has not been checked against the authorised product. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/) for what that means in practice and what you can check yourself.

</InDetail>


## What the caution costs

<Lead question="What does this caution cost?">Years of testing and a lot of money. Some medicines arrive late, and some substances are never tested.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Testing in people and review take about eight years" value="about 8 years" label="from permission to start tests in people to US approval, about the same from 1983 to 2017" counted="Time from the start of clinical testing to FDA approval, 1983–2017 (Darrow 2020)" source="darrow-2020-fda-approval-history" />

<KeyNumber title="Most drugs tried in people do not get approved" value="13.8%" label="of medicines tested in people for a disease were later approved for it" counted="41,040 company-run drug-and-disease programmes, 2000–2015 (Wong 2019)" source="wong-2019-clinical-trial-success" />

<KeyNumber title="By one count, a new medicine costs about a billion dollars" value="$985 million" label="spent on each new medicine, with the cost of the ones that failed (middle value, 2018 dollars)" counted="63 FDA-approved medicines, 2009–2018, with public cost data (Wouters 2020)" source="wouters-2020-rd-investment" />

These costs fall on people. A medicine that works but waits years for approval cannot help most patients in the meantime. A substance whose testing nobody expects to earn back may never be tested, so its benefits and risks stay unknown.

<InDetail title="The 'drug lag' debate">

Researchers have long compared how quickly new medicines reach patients in different countries.
{/* residue-ok: history (drug-lag research) */}
A 1992 review of this "drug lag" literature found that most studies put the United States, Sweden and Norway among the slowest countries to introduce new drugs, and the United Kingdom and West Germany among the fastest. The US and Norway had also introduced far fewer new drugs than other industrialised countries.<Cite id="andersson-1992-drug-lag" />

{/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}
The picture later reversed. For new medicines approved from 2001 to 2010, the first review took a median of 303 days at the FDA, 352 days at Health Canada and 366 days at the European Medicines Agency (EMA). Of the medicines approved in both the US and Europe, about two in three reached the US first; of those approved in both the US and Canada, about six in seven did.<Cite id="downing-2012-regulatory-review" /> FDA review times fell from more than three years in 1983 to less than one year in 2017, while the total time from permission to start tests in people to approval stayed at about eight years.<Cite id="darrow-2020-fda-approval-history" /> Most of the wait now goes into the studies themselves.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="How much one approval costs, and why estimates differ">

Estimates vary by a factor of more than two. A study based on a survey of ten companies put the average cost per approved new medicine at $2,558 million, including the cost of capital and of failed candidates (2013 dollars).<Cite id="dimasi-2016-rd-costs" /> A study using public data for 63 medicines put the median at $985 million and the mean at $1,336 million (2018 dollars); its data came mainly from smaller companies.<Cite id="wouters-2020-rd-investment" /> Both include the cost of the many candidates that fail.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Substances nobody pays to test">

Trials are paid for by whoever expects to earn the money back, usually through a patent. A 2021 study found that developers are "rarely interested" in research on approved medicines that have lost patent or regulatory protection, so new uses are mostly tested by academic groups that struggle to fund large trials.<Cite id="verbaanderd-2021-repurposing-funding" />
{/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}
The European Medicines Agency has said the same about old medicines and started a pilot in 2021 to help non-profit researchers, but only for medicines that are already authorised.<Cite id="ema-2021-repurposing-pilot" />

Our reading, not a finding of these sources: the same economics apply to many peptides sold as research chemicals. The FDA found no human trial of BPC-157 beyond a phase 2 study from 2005, reported only as a meeting abstract, and no approved product containing it in any country.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> When no one pays for trials, the questions a regulator would ask, about dose, effect and long-term safety, stay unanswered. People who use such products carry that uncertainty themselves. The history is on [the Croatian lab](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/) page.

</InDetail>

## Faster routes, and their limits

<Lead question="Are there faster routes?">Yes, mostly for serious illness. They let people use a medicine sooner, on less data, under strict rules.</Lead>

<Compare title="Each fast route still needs a known product and someone who takes responsibility." detail="Four routes in the US, the UK and Australia, compared on four questions. Checked on 24 October 2026.">

| | Accelerated approval (US) | Expanded access (US) | Early Access to Medicines Scheme (UK) | Special Access Scheme (Australia) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Is the medicine approved for this use? | <Verdict is="yes" /> It is an approval, based on an early sign of benefit<Cite id="fda-accelerated-approval-program" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> It is still being tested<Cite id="fda-expanded-access" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> It is not yet licensed for that use<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-167c" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> It is not approved in Australia<Cite id="au-tga-access-unapproved-goods-practitioners" /> |
| Can a doctor ask for it for one patient? | <Verdict is="no" /> It is an approval that the company applies for<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs,fda-accelerated-approval-program" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> A licensed doctor makes the request<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> A company applies; doctors can then prescribe it<Cite id="mhra-eams-overview" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Only a registered health practitioner can apply<Cite id="au-tga-access-unapproved-goods-practitioners" /> |
| Must someone make it and agree to supply it? | <Verdict is="yes" /> The company that applied<Cite id="fda-accelerated-approval-program" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> The company must agree; no law makes it supply<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> The company must be able to make and supply it<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-167c" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> The doctor must arrange the supply; no company has to supply<Cite id="au-tga-sas-individual-patients" /> |
| Only for serious illness? | <Verdict is="yes" /> For serious conditions with an unmet need<Cite id="fda-accelerated-approval-program" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Serious or immediately life-threatening, with no good alternative<Cite id="fda-expanded-access" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Life-threatening or seriously debilitating conditions<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-167c" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Not always: one category is for the seriously ill; another needs a clinical reason and TGA approval<Cite id="au-tga-sas-individual-patients" /> |

</Compare>

None of these routes is a shortcut for a product of unknown content. Our page on [treatment routes](/en/legal-routes/) explains the routes in each of our readers' countries, Canada included, and how they work for a patient.

<InDetail title="The routes in more detail">

- **Accelerated approval (US).** Formalised in 1992. A medicine for a serious condition can be approved on a marker that is thought to predict benefit, such as a shrinking tumour, but the company must then run studies to confirm the real benefit.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs,fda-accelerated-approval-program" />
- **Treatment use and expanded access (US).** Treatment use of drugs still in development, for life-threatening diseases, dates from 1987.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> Today expanded access, sometimes called compassionate use, is for a patient with a serious or immediately life-threatening condition when there is no comparable alternative and joining a trial is not possible.<Cite id="fda-expanded-access" /> The doctor, an ethics board and the company must all agree, and the FDA reviews the request.<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients" />
- **Right to Try (US, 2018).** For a person with a life-threatening condition who has tried the approved treatments and cannot join a trial. The drug must have finished a first, phase 1 trial and still be in development. The FDA does not review these requests, and no company has to supply.<Cite id="usc-21-360bbb-0a,fda-right-to-try" />
- **Early Access to Medicines Scheme (UK).** Set in law since 15 April 2022, for patients with life-threatening or seriously debilitating conditions. A medicine first needs a "Promising Innovative Medicine" designation and then a scientific opinion from the MHRA. The scheme is voluntary and does not replace a licence.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-167c,mhra-eams-overview" />
- **Special Access Scheme and Authorised Prescribers (Australia).** Category A is for a patient who is seriously ill, with death "reasonably likely to occur within a matter of months" or early death without early treatment; the doctor tells the TGA afterwards. Category B needs TGA approval first, with a clinical reason. An Authorised Prescriber can supply an unapproved product to a group of patients, usually with an ethics committee's approval.<Cite id="au-tg-regs-reg-12a-sas-category-a,au-tg-act-s19-access-approvals,au-tga-sas-individual-patients,au-tga-authorised-prescriber" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Faster routes have costs too">

Speed moves uncertainty from before approval to after it. A 2019 review looked at 93 cancer indications given US accelerated approval from 1992 to 2017. Confirmatory trials had shown longer survival for 19, about one in five. Another 39 had confirmed only an early marker, and others were still delayed, pending or running.<Cite id="gyawali-2019-accelerated-approval" />

{/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}
In a study of 68 cancer indications approved by the European Medicines Agency from 2009 to 2013, about half had still not shown a gain in survival or quality of life after a median of 5.4 years.<Cite id="davis-2017-ema-cancer-approvals" />

Requirements have also loosened over time. The share of new US approvals supported by at least two pivotal trials fell from 80.6% in 1995–1997 to 52.8% in 2015–2017, and the authors conclude that the FDA has "increasingly accepted less data and more surrogate measures".<Cite id="darrow-2020-fda-approval-history" />

{/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */}
The European Medicines Agency, for its part, found in a ten-year review that its conditional authorisation, a licence granted on less complete data, can speed up access to new medicines.<Cite id="ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Why these routes rarely help the peptides on this site">

Every route above needs a company that makes the medicine and agrees to supply it or, in Australia, a doctor who can arrange a lawful supply.<Cite id="fda-expanded-access-patients,uk-hmr-2012-reg-167c,au-tga-sas-individual-patients" /> On 23 October 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov listed expanded access for only two of the peptides on this site, retatrutide and elamipretide (SS-31), and none for BPC-157 or any other peptide on this site. A missing record does not prove that no one ever gets access, because not every arrangement is registered.<Cite id="ctgov-expanded-access-search-2026-10-23" /> None of the medicines in the UK's early access scheme is a peptide on this site.<Cite id="mhra-eams-scientific-opinions,mhra-eams-expired-opinions" /> For peptides, the TGA says an application should be supported by human studies of that peptide, in that form, by that route and for that use, and that the doctor is responsible for the product's quality and safety.<Cite id="au-tga-peptides-health-practitioners" /> See [treatment routes](/en/legal-routes/) and [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

</InDetail>

## Too strict, or not strict enough?

<Lead question="Is the system too strict or not strict enough?">There is evidence on both sides. This page describes it; our separate opinion piece takes a side.</Lead>

<Diagram id="caution-versus-speed-record" caption="What the record shows on each side of the debate.">

| For more caution | For more speed |
|---|---|
| Thalidomide was never approved in the US because a reviewer asked for better evidence | Studies reviewed in 1992 found the US slower than many countries to introduce new medicines |
| Of 93 US cancer approvals based on early signs of benefit, about 1 in 5 later showed longer survival | The European Medicines Agency found that its conditional authorisation can speed up access to new medicines {/* residue-ok: EMA evidence */} |
| A trial says little about a product with different contents | Substances that no company pays to test stay untested |

</Diagram>

Both sides describe real costs. Caution that is too slow leaves patients waiting; speed that is too loose approves medicines that do not deliver. The site's signed opinion on what should change is on [the case for reform](/en/the-case-for-reform/). This page does not argue it.
