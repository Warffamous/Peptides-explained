Peptide medicines are about a century old. Insulin, one of the first, lowered blood sugar in people with diabetes in 1922, and more than 80 peptide medicines have reached the market since.<Cite id="fda-history-100-years-insulin,muttenthaler-2021-peptide-trends" /> The rules that decide which medicines reach patients are younger. The US began to require proof that a medicine works in 1962. Canada has collected reports of side effects since 1965, the UK put the licensing of medicines into law in 1968, and Australia's Therapeutic Goods Act took effect in 1991.<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962,uk-medicines-act-1968-enacted,ca-hc-canada-vigilance,au-tg-act-1989-asmade-details" />

<InDetail title="How to read the dates">

- **Approvals** give the first approval of a product in that country: in the US the FDA's first approval date from Drugs@FDA; in Canada the date of Health Canada's notice of compliance; in Australia the date the medicine was registered; in the UK the date of the UK licence.<Cite id="fda-drugsatfda-ozempic,ca-sbd-ozempic,au-tga-pmr-search-status-a-2026-10-24,uk-mhra-ma-granted-2021" /> Later approvals for new uses are not listed.
- **Laws** give the date the act was adopted, unless the event says it came into force.
- **Events without a day** are known to the year only.
- **Regulatory status changes.** Each event describes the situation on the date shown. For the status today, see the compound pages and [what's changing](/en/legal/whats-changing/). We checked the older dates on 22 October 2026 and the UK, Canadian and Australian events on 24 October 2026.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="What this timeline leaves out">

It is a selection, not a complete history. It leaves out most peptide medicines that are not discussed on this site, most national laws outside the US, the UK, Canada and Australia, and most later approvals of the same molecule for new uses. A medicine that appears here is not recommended by this site, and a peptide that does not appear here is not safe or unsafe because of that.

</InDetail>

## Patterns worth noticing

<Lead question="What does the timeline teach?">Rules came after harm. Faster routes came after long waits. Copies and fakes came when demand was high.</Lead>

<Diagram id="timeline-cause-and-effect" caption="Three times, a problem led to a change in the rules.">

| What went wrong | What changed next |
|---|---|
| A toxic medicine killed 107 people in the US in 1937 | Proof of safety before marketing (US, 1938) |
| Thalidomide harmed more than 10,000 children, 1957–1962 | Proof that a medicine works (US, 1962); expert committees and side-effect reports (UK and Australia, 1963–1964) |
| Seriously ill patients pushed for earlier access, and US reviews were criticised as too slow | User fees to fund faster reviews and accelerated approval (US, 1992) |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="fda-history-milestones,vargesson-2015-thalidomide,us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962,uk-hansard-1963-04-04-safety-of-drugs,uk-mhra-2014-yellow-card-50,au-tga-mcewen-2007-history,junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history,fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" />

The pattern runs in both directions. Disasters made the rules stricter; long waits for new treatments made them faster in places. The page [how we got here](/en/history/how-we-got-here/) explains both pressures.

<InDetail title="The pattern, event by event">

**Harm, then rules.** The US Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 came after Elixir of Sulfanilamide killed 107 people, many of them children, in 1937.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> After thalidomide, the UK and Australia set up expert committees on the safety of medicines in 1963, and the UK, Australia and Canada began to collect reports of suspected side effects in 1964 and 1965.<Cite id="uk-hansard-1963-04-04-safety-of-drugs,au-tga-mcewen-2007-history,uk-mhra-2014-yellow-card-50,ca-hc-canada-vigilance" />

**Delay, then faster routes.** During the AIDS epidemic of the 1980s, very ill patients pushed for access to drugs still in development.<Cite id="junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" /> The FDA's review was long praised for its quality but, in the words of a former head of its drug centre, "criticized ... for being too slow". User fees to pay for more reviewers, and accelerated approval, followed in 1992.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> Much later, in 2022, the UK set its Early Access to Medicines Scheme in law, for patients with life-threatening or seriously debilitating conditions.<Cite id="uk-hmr-2012-reg-167c" />

**Demand, then unlicensed supply.** When demand for GLP-1 medicines outran supply in 2022, US pharmacies were allowed to compound copies during the shortage.<Cite id="fda-glp1-compounding-policy" /> Falsified pens followed in 2023, and by 2025 the UK regulator was seizing GLP-1 pens alongside other illegal medicines.<Cite id="mhra-2023-fake-weight-loss-pens,mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025" /> In 2026 Health Canada warned about unauthorised injectable peptides from the internet, the TGA made unapproved peptide products a priority for enforcement in Australia, and it found that a vial sold as retatrutide contained semaglutide instead.<Cite id="ca-hc-2026-04-think-twice-peptides,au-tga-2026-06-peptide-compliance-priority,au-tga-2026-09-retatrutide-counterfeit" />

**Advice is not approval.** One of the most recent events is an FDA advisory vote. On 23 July 2026 the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8–6, with one abstention, to recommend BPC-157 for the list of bulk substances US pharmacies may use in compounding (the 503A list). Over two days it considered seven peptides and, as reported from the public meeting, recommended six: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax and epitalon (not emideltide, also called DSIP). No fda.gov page yet gives the vote counts.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-materials,fda-pcac-2026-questions" />

The vote is advisory and non-binding, and it went against FDA staff's own recommendation.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" /> Legal compounding of these peptides remains prohibited until the FDA formally adopts the recommendation and completes rulemaking.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" /> None of the six is an approved medicine in the US, the UK, Canada or Australia; Semax is registered as a medicine in Russia, as the FDA's own briefing notes.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-semax-briefing,uk-mhra-substance-index-status-c-2026-10-23,mhra-products-search-status-d-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-status-c-2026-10-23,ca-dpd-search-canada-legal-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-c-2026-10-23,au-artg-search-status-d-2026-10-23" /> A 503A listing, if it ever happens, would allow pharmacy compounding on prescription in the US. It is not a marketing authorisation, it says nothing about whether a peptide works, and it changes nothing outside the US.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1" />

</InDetail>
