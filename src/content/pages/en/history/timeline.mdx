Peptide medicines are about a century old. Insulin, one of the first, lowered blood sugar in people with diabetes in 1922, and more than 80 peptide medicines have reached the market since.<Cite id="fda-history-100-years-insulin,muttenthaler-2021-peptide-trends" /> The rules that decide which medicines reach patients are younger: the US began to require proof that a medicine works in 1962, and Europe a licence before sale in 1965.<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962,eu-directive-65-65" />

<InDetail title="How to read the dates">

- **Approvals** give the first approval of a product in that jurisdiction: the FDA's first approval date from Drugs@FDA, and the date the European Commission granted the EU marketing authorisation.<Cite id="fda-drugsatfda-ozempic,ema-epar-ozempic" /> Later approvals for new uses are not listed.
- **Laws** give the date the act was adopted, unless the event says it came into force.
- **Events without a day** are known to the year only.
- **Regulatory status changes.** Each event describes the situation on the date shown. For the status today, see the compound pages and [what's changing](/en/legal/whats-changing/). We last checked every date on 22 October 2026.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="What this timeline leaves out">

It is a selection, not a complete history. It leaves out most peptide medicines that are not discussed on this site, most national laws outside the Netherlands, the EU and the US, and most later approvals of the same molecule for new uses. A medicine that appears here is not recommended by this site, and a peptide that does not appear here is not safe or unsafe because of that.

</InDetail>

## Patterns worth noticing

<Lead question="What does the timeline teach?">Rules came after harm. Faster routes came after long waits. Copies and fakes came when demand was high.</Lead>

<Diagram id="timeline-cause-and-effect" caption="Three times, a problem led to a change in the rules.">

| What went wrong | What changed next |
|---|---|
| A toxic medicine killed 107 people in the US in 1937 | Proof of safety before marketing (US, 1938) |
| Thalidomide harmed more than 10,000 children, 1957–1962 | Proof that a medicine works (US, 1962); a licence before sale (European Community, 1965) |
| Seriously ill patients pushed for earlier access, and US reviews were criticised as too slow | User fees to fund faster reviews and accelerated approval (US, 1992); conditional authorisation (EU, 2006) |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="fda-history-milestones,vargesson-2015-thalidomide,us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962,ec-legal-framework-medicines,junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history,fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs,ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation" />

The pattern runs in both directions. Disasters made the rules stricter; long waits for new treatments made them faster in places. The page [how we got here](/en/history/how-we-got-here/) explains both pressures.

<InDetail title="The pattern, event by event">

**Harm, then rules.** The US Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 came after Elixir of Sulfanilamide killed 107 people, many of them children, in 1937.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> The European Commission writes that much of the impetus behind Europe's first medicines law, in 1965, was the determination to prevent another thalidomide.<Cite id="ec-legal-framework-medicines" />

**Delay, then faster routes.** During the AIDS epidemic of the 1980s, very ill patients pushed for access to drugs still in development.<Cite id="junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" /> The FDA's review was long praised for its quality but, in the words of a former head of its drug centre, "criticized ... for being too slow". User fees to pay for more reviewers, and accelerated approval, followed in 1992.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> The EU added conditional marketing authorisation in 2006 and the PRIME scheme in 2016.<Cite id="ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation,ema-prime" />

**Demand, then unlicensed supply.** When demand for GLP-1 medicines outran supply in 2022, US pharmacies were allowed to compound copies during the shortage.<Cite id="fda-glp1-compounding-policy" /> Falsified pens followed in 2023, and by 2025 the UK regulator was seizing GLP-1 pens alongside other illegal medicines.<Cite id="ema-2023-falsified-ozempic,mhra-2023-fake-weight-loss-pens,mhra-2026-01-seizures-2025" />

**Advice is not approval.** The latest event is an FDA advisory vote. On 23 July 2026 the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8–6, with one abstention, to recommend BPC-157 for the list of bulk substances US pharmacies may use in compounding (the 503A list). Over two days it considered seven peptides and, as reported from the public meeting, recommended six: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax and epitalon (not emideltide, also called DSIP). No fda.gov page yet gives the vote counts.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-07-meeting,fda-pcac-2026-materials,fda-pcac-2026-questions" />

The vote is advisory and non-binding, and it went against FDA staff's own recommendation.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-briefing-intro" /> Legal compounding of these peptides remains prohibited until the FDA formally adopts the recommendation and completes rulemaking.<Cite id="fda-503a-bulks-page" /> None of them is an approved medicine in the EU, the UK or the US; Semax is registered as a medicine in Russia, as the FDA's own briefing notes.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-tb500-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-kpv-briefing,fda-pcac-2026-semax-briefing,ema-medicines-search" /> A 503A listing, if it ever happens, would allow pharmacy compounding on prescription in the US. It is not a marketing authorisation, it says nothing about whether a peptide works, and it changes nothing in the EU or the Netherlands.<Cite id="usc-21-353a,fda-pcac-2026-presentations-day1,nl-geneesmiddelenwet" />

</InDetail>
