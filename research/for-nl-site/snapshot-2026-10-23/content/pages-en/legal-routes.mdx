This page describes the legal channels. It does not help anyone get round the law. Your doctor is the starting point for all six routes. See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

## 1. A prescription for an approved medicine

<Lead question="How do I get an approved peptide medicine?">Through your own doctor, who decides if it suits you, and a pharmacy.</Lead>

<Diagram id="route-prescription" caption="The normal route for an approved medicine.">

1. **Check that it is approved.** The CBG's medicines database lists every medicine approved in the Netherlands.<Cite id="cbg-geneesmiddeleninformatiebank" />
2. **See a doctor who knows you.** No one may prescribe online to someone they have never met, do not know, or whose medicine history they cannot see.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-67-internet-prescribing" />
3. **The doctor decides.** A doctor must act as a good doctor would, in line with professional standards. So a doctor does not have to prescribe something just because you ask.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-453-good-care" />
4. **A pharmacy hands it over.** Prescription medicines may only be handed over on a prescription, by a pharmacist or a dispensing GP.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-61-prescription" />

</Diagram>

Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro) are approved in the EU.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy,ema-epar-mounjaro" /> A "research" vial with the same name is a different, unlicensed product. See [semaglutide](/en/compounds/semaglutide/).

## 2. Off-label prescribing

<Lead question="Can a doctor prescribe a medicine for another use?">Sometimes. Only if doctors' own rules allow it, with extra care and your consent.</Lead>

<Diagram id="off-label-four-situations" caption="When a Dutch doctor may prescribe a medicine for a use it is not approved for.">

| Situation | Allowed? |
|---|---|
| A professional standard allows it | Yes, following that standard |
| A professional standard advises against it | No |
| A standard is being written | Only after doctor and pharmacist talk it through, with extra care |
| There is no standard, and none is being written | In principle, no. Rare exceptions for one patient with no other option, after talking with the pharmacist |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="igj-2026-off-label-standpunt,nl-gmw-art-68-off-label" />

Off-label means an approved medicine, used for something else. It cannot make an unapproved peptide available.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-68-off-label" />

<InDetail title="The extra care the IGJ expects">

The IGJ updated its position on off-label prescribing in February 2026, and adjusted it in May 2026. Outside a standard, the doctor must weigh benefit against side effects on current science. The doctor must check your up-to-date medicine history. You must be told more than usual, and your explicit consent goes in your file. You are watched more closely for side effects.<Cite id="igj-2026-off-label-standpunt" />

</InDetail>

## 3. A doctor's declaration (artsenverklaring)

<Lead question="Can my doctor ask for an exception?">Yes, for one patient, when the approved drugs cannot help you for a medical reason.</Lead>

<Diagram id="artsenverklaring-steps" caption="The three steps of supply on a doctor's declaration.">

1. **Your doctor signs a declaration.** You cannot be treated with medicines approved here, for medical reasons. The doctor accepts responsibility and the risk, and has told you the product was not checked for quality, safety and effect.<Cite id="igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring" />
2. **A pharmacy, wholesaler or manufacturer applies.** Only a supplier based in the Netherlands can ask the IGJ for an exemption. You cannot apply yourself.<Cite id="igj-qa-zonder-handelsvergunning" />
3. **The IGJ decides.** It has up to 8 weeks, about 4 on average. It may say no.<Cite id="igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring" />

</Diagram>

The IGJ calls this an exception for individual treatment "in extreme cases". It is not meant as a way round the licence rule.<Cite id="igj-qa-zonder-handelsvergunning" />

<InDetail title="What the application must contain">

The legal basis is article 40(3)(c) of the Medicines Act and article 3.17 of the Medicines Regulation. The IGJ can grant the exemption only if all of these are true:<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning,nl-regeling-gnw-art-3-17" />

- the doctor finds the treatment necessary;
- no adequate medicine is available in the Netherlands;
- the supplier keeps records of amounts, patients and side effects.

In practice the IGJ asks for:<Cite id="igj-qa-zonder-handelsvergunning" />

- a declaration signed by a doctor in the Dutch BIG register, with a patient code instead of your name, valid for one year;
- on page 2, the treatments you already had or that were considered, and why approved medicines cannot be used;
- the official product information from the country of origin, or, for a product approved nowhere, the Investigator's Brochure or IMPD;
- a valid GMP certificate when the product comes from outside the EEA, MRA countries and the UK, or comes from there without a licence.

Money, convenience or a different form of the same medicine are not accepted as reasons. The supplier must record side effects and report them to Lareb.<Cite id="igj-qa-zonder-handelsvergunning" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Why this route does not fit BPC-157 and similar peptides">

Two documents are the problem. For a product approved nowhere, the IGJ asks for the trial files a company writes about it: the Investigator's Brochure or the IMPD. It also asks for proof that the product was made under GMP.<Cite id="igj-qa-zonder-handelsvergunning" /> A vial sold for "research" comes with neither. A supplier would need a manufacturer that makes the peptide to medicine standards and can show it.

There must also be no adequate medicine for your condition on the Dutch market.<Cite id="nl-regeling-gnw-art-3-17" /> The route is real for medicines approved in another country but not sold here.<Cite id="igj-qa-zonder-handelsvergunning" />

</InDetail>

## 4. A pharmacy preparation (magistrale bereiding)

<Lead question="Can a pharmacy make a peptide for me?">Only if the raw material meets an official standard. The pharmacist must also be sure it is good.</Lead>

<Diagram id="pharmacy-preparation-checks" caption="What a pharmacy must be sure of before it makes a medicine itself.">

1. **A prescription first.** The prescription must exist before the medicine is made, for a patient who is known.<Cite id="cjeu-2015-abcur" />
2. **Small scale, in the pharmacy.** The pharmacist makes it, or has it made, in small amounts in the pharmacy, and hands it over from there.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" />
3. **Raw material of known quality.** It must meet the European Pharmacopoeia, or another official one.<Cite id="igj-eigen-bereidingen-apotheek" />
4. **The pharmacist must judge it.** No regulator checks the preparation in advance, so the pharmacist must check the raw material's file and the whole process.<Cite id="igj-eigen-bereidingen-apotheek" />

</Diagram>

If a substance is in no official pharmacopoeia, the IGJ says it "cannot simply be used". If the pharmacist is not sure about origin, quality and final checks, a pharmacy preparation is not a good choice.<Cite id="igj-eigen-bereidingen-apotheek" />

## 5. A clinical trial

<Lead question="Can I join a trial?">Perhaps. A trial is the main legal way to use a medicine still being tested, if you qualify.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="No trial of BPC-157 runs in the Netherlands" value="0" label="trials of BPC-157 with a site in the Netherlands" counted="ClinicalTrials.gov search of 22 October 2026. None running for TB-500, thymosin beta-4 or the other experimental peptides on this site either; elamipretide (SS-31) had three Dutch trials, all finished" source="ctgov-nl-be-peptide-search-2026-10-22" />

<KeyNumber title="But there are two trials of retatrutide here" value="2" label="trials of retatrutide with Dutch sites; one was still recruiting" counted="ClinicalTrials.gov search of 22 October 2026" source="ctgov-nl-be-peptide-search-2026-10-22,ctgov-nct06383390,ctgov-nct07165028" />

Taking part is always voluntary. A medical ethics committee (METC) must approve the study first. You can stop at any time without saying why, and you are insured for harm caused by the research.<Cite id="ccmo-ivo-meedoen" />

<InDetail title="How to look for a trial, and what to expect">

**Where to look.** Ask your GP or specialist, or a patient organisation. You can also search the Dutch register Onderzoekmetmensen.nl, the EU's public trial website, or ClinicalTrials.gov. The CCMO does not match people with studies.<Cite id="ccmo-achtergrond-meedoen,ema-clinical-trials-regulation" />

**What it involves.** You get written information and time to decide. You sign a consent form. There is often a check first to see if you can take part.<Cite id="ccmo-ivo-meedoen" /> In early trials (phase 1 and 2), patients usually do not get better from taking part. Patients usually get travel costs only, and do not pay for the study medicine.<Cite id="ccmo-achtergrond-meedoen" />

**Your rights under EU law.** The expected benefits must justify the risks. You can leave at any time without giving a reason and without any harm to you.<Cite id="eu-regulation-536-2014" />

**The insurance has limits.** Harm must be reported within four years. Harm you were told about in advance is usually not covered.<Cite id="ccmo-ivo-meedoen" />

</InDetail>

## 6. A compassionate use programme

<Lead question="What is compassionate use?">A company gives a new medicine to very ill people before it is approved. The CBG must agree.</Lead>

<Diagram id="compassionate-use-criteria" caption="What must be true before a company may give a new medicine early.">

1. **A group of seriously ill patients.** The disease is long-term, seriously disabling or life-threatening.<Cite id="cbg-compassionate-use-criteria" />
2. **No good approved treatment.** Approved medicines do not treat it well enough.<Cite id="cbg-compassionate-use-criteria" />
3. **A medicine in real development.** A licence application has been filed, or trials are still running.<Cite id="cbg-compassionate-use-criteria,eu-regulation-726-2004" />

</Diagram>

The manufacturer applies, not the patient. The CBG's list of approved programmes includes no peptide on this site.<Cite id="cbg-compassionate-use-criteria,cbg-compassionate-use-list" />

## What this means for you

<Lead question="So what can I do?">Start with your doctor. Ask about approved medicines for your goal. If none fits, ask about trials.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- Tell your doctor what you hope to achieve. There may be an approved treatment for it.
- Ask whether an approved medicine could be prescribed, on-label or within a professional standard.
- If approved medicines did not work for you, ask whether a doctor's declaration is possible.
- Ask your doctor or a patient organisation about trials that you might join.
- If you use something already, say so. Your doctor can still help you check your health.

</Checklist>

<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-448-information,igj-2026-off-label-standpunt,igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring,ccmo-achtergrond-meedoen" />

A doctor must explain your options, including other treatments, and their risks.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-448-information" /> See [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

These routes are kept narrow on purpose, to protect the licence system.<Cite id="cjeu-2012-commission-v-poland" /> For someone whom approved treatments did not help, that can be a real cost. We discuss this on [the case for reform](/en/the-case-for-reform/).
