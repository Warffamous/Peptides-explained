<Lead question="Why does a medicine need a licence before it is sold?">In the past, some medicines did great harm before anyone checked them.</Lead>

<Diagram id="three-checks-and-when" caption="Three questions every new medicine must answer before sale, and when the law first asked them.">

| Question | What it means | First required |
|---|---|---|
| Is it safe enough? | Tests show the harms in normal use | US 1938; European Community 1965 |
| Does it work? | Well-controlled studies show the effect it claims | US 1962; European Community 1965 |
| Is it made properly? | Production under controls that keep its contents as declared | US 1938 (formal manufacturing rules in 1962); European Community 1965 |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="fda-history-milestones,us-fdc-act-1938,us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962,fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs,eu-directive-65-65" />

Today a new medicine must answer these three questions before it can be authorised in the EU or approved in the US. That was not always so. This page explains how the rules came about, what they protect against, and what they cost. Two disasters shaped them: a poisonous medicine in 1937 and thalidomide around 1960.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones,vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

## Before 1962: safe on paper, effect unproven

<Lead question="What did the law ask for before 1962?">In the US, from 1938, proof of safety. Nobody had to prove that a medicine worked.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="107 people died from one drug" value="107" label="people died, many of them children, after taking one drug" counted="Deaths from one US medicine dissolved in a poisonous solvent, 1937 (FDA history)" source="fda-history-milestones" />

The 1937 deaths led to the US Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938. From then on, a new drug had to be shown safe before marketing.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> Whether it did what its maker claimed was not checked.

<InDetail title="The 1938 law and its gap">

The medicine contained diethylene glycol, a poisonous solvent related to antifreeze. The law of 1906 did not ask for any information before a drug was sold, so nobody had to show it was safe first.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones,fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> The 1938 Act, approved on 25 June 1938, changed that. A company now had to send the FDA a new-drug application with reports of its safety tests and a description of how the drug was made. The FDA could refuse it if the tests were inadequate, if they did not show the drug was safe, or if the manufacturing controls could not keep the drug's identity, strength, quality and purity.<Cite id="us-fdc-act-1938" /> The law asked for "adequate tests by all methods reasonably applicable", but it did not say which tests.<Cite id="us-fdc-act-1938,junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" />

The gap was effect. Before 1962, US manufacturers did not have to prove to the FDA that their products worked.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> Senate hearings in the years before 1962 heard about "the sorry state of science supporting drug effectiveness" and exaggerated claims in labels and advertising, as a senior FDA official later put it.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The Netherlands in 1958: a register for packaged medicines">

On paper, the Netherlands already asked about effect. The Wet op de Geneesmiddelenvoorziening of 28 July 1958 set up a board to register packaged medicines. A product could be registered if it could reasonably be assumed to have the claimed effect and not to harm health when used as directed; unregistered packaged medicines could not be sold. The act asked for a reasonable assumption, not for a particular kind of study. Its article 37 left the date it came into force to a later royal decree, possibly at different times for different parts.<Cite id="nl-wog-1958" /> We could not confirm when the register started to work.

</InDetail>

## Thalidomide

<Lead question="What happened with thalidomide?">A sleeping pill taken early in pregnancy harmed more than 10,000 babies.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="More than 10,000 children were harmed" value="over 10,000" label="children born with severe birth defects, 1957–1962" counted="Children harmed by thalidomide worldwide, as estimated in a 2015 review of its history" source="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

<KeyNumber title="It was sold in dozens of countries" value="46" label="countries sold the drug, under different names" counted="Countries where the drug was marketed and distributed, from the same review" source="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

<KeyNumber title="Many of the most harmed babies died as infants" value="up to 40%" label="of the babies with the worst damage died in their first year" counted="Infant deaths among babies born with severe thalidomide damage, from the same review" source="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

Thalidomide went on sale in the late 1950s as a sedative and was soon given for morning sickness. In 1961 two doctors, Lenz in Germany and McBride in Australia, linked it to severe malformations in babies. Withdrawals began in November 1961, and by 1962 it was off the market in most of the world.<Cite id="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" /> In the US it had never been approved.<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history" />

<InDetail title="What went wrong, step by step">

Thalidomide was made by the German company Chemie Grünenthal and sold as Contergan in Germany, Softenon elsewhere in Europe and Distaval in the UK. It was advertised as free of risk, and doctors handed out free samples for morning sickness.<Cite id="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

The damage came from timing. The drug harmed the embryo in a short window early in pregnancy, between about day 20 and day 36 after fertilisation. Limbs, face, eyes, ears, heart and other organs could be affected, and many survivors live with severe disabilities today.<Cite id="vargesson-2015-thalidomide" />

The tests done before sale did not reveal the danger, and exactly what was tested is still debated. Mice, the usual species for screening drugs, are less sensitive to thalidomide than rabbits or primates. After the disaster, drug testing changed to use several species, and regulators developed systematic tests for toxic effects.<Cite id="vargesson-2015-thalidomide,kim-2011-thalidomide-review" /> The same lesson, that an animal result may not carry over to people, runs through our page on [the translation gap](/en/evidence/the-translation-gap/).

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Frances Kelsey and the US application">

In 1960 Frances Kelsey joined the FDA as one of a handful of medical officers. One of the first applications she was given was for thalidomide, already sold in dozens of countries. Despite constant pressure from the company, she refused to approve it because the evidence was inadequate. About a year later the link with birth defects was found, and the drug was never marketed in the US.<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history" />

The US did not escape entirely. The US sponsor had sent samples to thousands of doctors, who gave them to patients without telling them the drug was experimental. More than a dozen children with thalidomide damage are believed to have been born in the US as a result.<Cite id="junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" /> In 1962 President Kennedy gave Kelsey the highest award for a federal civil servant.<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history" />

</InDetail>

## 1962: proof that it works

<Lead question="What changed in 1962?">From 1962, US law asked for proof from well-controlled studies that a medicine works.</Lead>

<Compare title="After 1962, a new medicine had to show it works and be approved before sale." detail="What US law required of a new medicine before and after the Drug Amendments of 10 October 1962.">

| | Before 1962 (law of 1938) | After 1962 |
|---|---|---|
| Proof of safety before sale? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Required since 1938<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Still required<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> |
| Proof that it works? | <Verdict is="no" /> Not required<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Substantial evidence from adequate and well-controlled studies<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> |
| Must the FDA approve it first? | <Verdict is="no" /> No. Sales could start if the FDA did not act within 60 days<Cite id="us-fdc-act-1938,junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> The FDA must approve the application<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> |
| Must people in trials be told and asked? | <Verdict is="no" /> Not in the drug law; some were not even told<Cite id="junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Yes, with exceptions<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> |
| Must older medicines show they work? | <Verdict is="no" /> No, safety was enough<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> About 4,000 were re-assessed from 1966<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> |

</Compare>

The law defined "substantial evidence" as evidence from adequate and well-controlled investigations, including clinical ones, by qualified experts.<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> An FDA official later described this requirement as a radical change from what had gone before.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" />

<InDetail title="What 'adequate and well-controlled' means, from first principles">

People often get better or worse on their own, believing in a treatment can change how they feel, and an observer who knows who was treated can see what they expect. US regulations say a clinical study exists to "distinguish the effect of a drug from other influences" like these.<Cite id="ecfr-21-314-126" />

So a well-controlled study compares the treated group with a control group, such as a placebo or an established medicine. It assigns people to the groups by randomisation, as a rule, and uses blinding so that neither patients nor observers can tilt the result. Uncontrolled studies "are not acceptable as the sole basis" for a claim that a medicine works.<Cite id="ecfr-21-314-126" /> Our page on [how to read a study](/en/evidence/how-to-read-a-study/) explains each of these protections.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Consent, with the limits of 1962">

The 1962 law required investigators to tell people that they were receiving an experimental drug and to obtain their consent, or that of a representative. It also allowed exceptions where the investigator judged consent "not feasible" or not in the person's interest.<Cite id="us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962" /> Later rules added more: a US policy of 1981, adopted across government in 1991 as the Common Rule, set requirements for documenting consent and for ethics review boards.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Re-checking the medicines already on the market">

The new rule also applied backwards. In 1966 the FDA asked the US National Academy of Sciences to evaluate the effectiveness of about 4,000 drugs approved on safety alone between 1938 and 1962.<Cite id="fda-history-milestones" /> In 1970, during this review, new rules stated that "uncontrolled studies are not acceptable evidence to support claims of effectiveness".<Cite id="junod-2008-fda-clinical-trials-history" />

</InDetail>

## Europe's answer

<Lead question="How did Europe respond?">From 1965, a ready-made medicine needed a licence, based on test results, before it went on sale.</Lead>

<TimeStrip title="Europe's licence rule dates from 1965. The Dutch law changed form in 2007." detail="The main European and Dutch medicines laws, and the European agency, by year." min={1955} max={2030} step={10} axis="Year" source="nl-wog-1958,nl-geneesmiddelenwet-2007-version,eu-directive-65-65,eu-directive-2001-83-original,eu-regulation-2309-93,ema-history" checked="2026-10-22" rows={[
  { label: "Netherlands: Wet op de Geneesmiddelenvoorziening", spans: [{ from: 1958, to: 2007, text: "act of 1958, replaced in 2007" }] },
  { label: "Netherlands: Geneesmiddelenwet", spans: [{ from: 2007, to: 2026.8, text: "since 1 July 2007" }] },
  { label: "European Community: Directive 65/65/EEC, a licence before sale", spans: [{ from: 1965, to: 2001, text: "1965 to 2001" }] },
  { label: "EU: Directive 2001/83/EC, the medicines code", spans: [{ from: 2001, to: 2026.8, text: "since 2001" }] },
  { label: "European Medicines Agency", spans: [{ from: 1995, to: 2026.8, text: "since 1995" }] },
]} />

The European Commission writes that much of the impetus for Europe's medicines law was the determination to prevent another thalidomide: no medicine should ever again be marketed without prior authorisation.<Cite id="ec-legal-framework-medicines" /> Directive 65/65/EEC of 26 January 1965 made that the rule in the member states of the European Economic Community.<Cite id="eu-directive-65-65" />

<InDetail title="What Directive 65/65 required">

The directive covered ready-made ("proprietary") medicines, sold under a special name in a pack. None could be placed on the market in a member state without an authorisation, and the member states had 18 months to put the rule into their own law. The application had to include the results of chemical and microbiological tests, pharmacological and toxicological tests, and clinical trials. The authorisation was refused if the medicine was harmful in normal use, if its therapeutic effect was lacking or "insufficiently substantiated by the applicant", or if its composition was not as declared. A decision was due within 120 days, and an authorisation lasted five years before renewal.<Cite id="eu-directive-65-65" />

The directive named its own balance. Its first aim was "to safeguard public health", but by "means which will not hinder the development of the pharmaceutical industry or trade in medicinal products".<Cite id="eu-directive-65-65" /> In 2001 the EU gathered its medicines laws into one code, Directive 2001/83/EC, which repealed Directive 65/65 and kept the same grounds for refusal.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83-original" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The Dutch line: from 1958 to today">

The Wet op de Geneesmiddelenvoorziening of 1958 was replaced almost fifty years later. On 1 July 2007 the Geneesmiddelenwet, the Dutch Medicines Act of 8 February 2007, came into force and withdrew it.<Cite id="nl-wog-1958,nl-geneesmiddelenwet-2007-version" /> The core rule today is article 40: no medicine may be placed on the Dutch market without an EU or Dutch marketing authorisation, and a medicine without one may not be sold, supplied or imported, with a few exceptions.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning" /> Our page on [Dutch law](/en/legal/nl/) explains what this means for peptides.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="One agency for Europe">

In 1993 the European Community created what is now the European Medicines Agency (EMA), with an EU-wide authorisation procedure that started on 1 January 1995.<Cite id="eu-regulation-2309-93,ema-history" /> Today medicines made by biotechnology and medicines with a new active substance for diabetes, among other groups, must go through this central route.<Cite id="eu-regulation-726-2004" /> That is why semaglutide and tirzepatide were authorised by the European Commission for the whole EU at once.<Cite id="ema-epar-ozempic,ema-epar-mounjaro" />

</InDetail>

## What the checks give you

<Lead question="What do these rules give patients?">A known product, a tested effect, and risks that are watched after sale and written on the label.</Lead>

<Diagram id="what-each-check-protects-against" caption="What each check protects against.">

| Check | What it asks for | What it protects against |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Contents as declared; production under controls | The wrong substance, the wrong amount, contamination |
| Effect | Adequate and well-controlled studies | A medicine that does not work, or an effect that came from chance, placebo or bias |
| Safety | Animal tests and trials in people; refusal if harmful in normal use | Harms that a small or uncontrolled test would miss |
| After sale | Reports of side effects; label updates | Rare harms that show up only when many people use it |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="eu-directive-65-65,us-pl-87-781-drug-amendments-1962,ecfr-21-314-126,fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs,ema-history" />

The checks do not make a medicine harmless. They make its benefits and risks known, for one defined product. Rare harms often show up only after many people use a medicine, so reporting continues after sale and the label is updated.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs,ema-history" />

<InDetail title="Why the evidence belongs to a product, not to a name">

US regulations say that a medicine tested in a trial must be "standardized as to identity, strength, quality, purity, and dosage form to give significance to the results of the investigation".<Cite id="ecfr-21-314-126" /> The logic is simple: a trial measures what was in the trial. If a different product has a different content, amount or purity, the trial says little about it.

This is why a product from outside the legal supply chain that carries the name of an authorised medicine is not covered by that medicine's evidence. Its content has not been checked against the authorised product. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/) for what that means in practice and what you can check yourself.

</InDetail>


## What the caution costs

<Lead question="What does this caution cost?">Years of testing and a lot of money. Some medicines arrive late, and some substances are never tested.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Testing in people and review take about eight years" value="about 8 years" label="from permission to start tests in people to US approval, about the same from 1983 to 2017" counted="Time from the start of clinical testing to FDA approval, 1983–2017 (Darrow 2020)" source="darrow-2020-fda-approval-history" />

<KeyNumber title="Most drugs tried in people do not get approved" value="13.8%" label="of medicines tested in people for a disease were later approved for it" counted="41,040 company-run drug-and-disease programmes, 2000–2015 (Wong 2019)" source="wong-2019-clinical-trial-success" />

<KeyNumber title="By one count, a new medicine costs about a billion dollars" value="$985 million" label="spent on each new medicine, with the cost of the ones that failed (middle value, 2018 dollars)" counted="63 FDA-approved medicines, 2009–2018, with public cost data (Wouters 2020)" source="wouters-2020-rd-investment" />

These costs fall on people. A medicine that works but waits years for approval cannot help most patients in the meantime. A substance whose testing nobody expects to earn back may never be tested, so its benefits and risks stay unknown.

<InDetail title="The 'drug lag' debate">

Researchers have long compared how quickly new medicines reach patients in different countries. A 1992 review of this "drug lag" literature found that most studies put the United States, Sweden and Norway among the slowest countries to introduce new drugs, and the United Kingdom and West Germany among the fastest. The US and Norway had also introduced far fewer new drugs than other industrialised countries.<Cite id="andersson-1992-drug-lag" />

The picture later reversed. For new medicines approved from 2001 to 2010, the FDA's first review took a median of 303 days and the EMA's 366 days, and of the medicines approved in both, about two in three reached the US first.<Cite id="downing-2012-regulatory-review" /> FDA review times fell from more than three years in 1983 to less than one year in 2017, while the total time from permission to start tests in people to approval stayed at about eight years.<Cite id="darrow-2020-fda-approval-history" /> Most of the wait now goes into the studies themselves.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="How much one approval costs, and why estimates differ">

Estimates vary by a factor of more than two. A study based on a survey of ten companies put the average cost per approved new medicine at $2,558 million, including the cost of capital and of failed candidates (2013 dollars).<Cite id="dimasi-2016-rd-costs" /> A study using public data for 63 medicines put the median at $985 million and the mean at $1,336 million (2018 dollars); its data came mainly from smaller companies.<Cite id="wouters-2020-rd-investment" /> Both include the cost of the many candidates that fail.

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Substances nobody pays to test">

Trials are paid for by whoever expects to earn the money back, usually through a patent. A 2021 study found that developers are "rarely interested" in research on approved medicines that have lost patent or regulatory protection, so new uses are mostly tested by academic groups that struggle to fund large trials.<Cite id="verbaanderd-2021-repurposing-funding" /> The EMA has said the same about old medicines and started a pilot in 2021 to help non-profit researchers, but only for medicines that are already authorised.<Cite id="ema-2021-repurposing-pilot" />

Our reading, not a finding of these sources: the same economics apply to many peptides sold as research chemicals. The FDA found no human trial of BPC-157 beyond a phase 2 study from 2005, reported only as a meeting abstract, and no approved product containing it in any country.<Cite id="fda-pcac-2026-bpc157-briefing" /> When no one pays for trials, the questions a regulator would ask, about dose, effect and long-term safety, stay unanswered. People who use such products carry that uncertainty themselves. The history is on [the Croatian lab](/en/history/the-croatian-lab/) page.

</InDetail>

## Faster routes, and their limits

<Lead question="Are there faster routes?">Yes, for serious diseases. They let people use a medicine sooner, on less data, under strict rules.</Lead>

<Compare title="Each fast route still needs a known product and someone who takes responsibility." detail="Four routes in the EU, the Netherlands and the US, compared on three questions.">

| | Conditional authorisation (EU) | Accelerated approval (US) | Compassionate use (EU) | Named-patient use (NL) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Can it be prescribed like a licensed medicine? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Yes, a licence renewed each year<Cite id="ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> Yes, an approval<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Only in a programme for a group of patients<Cite id="ema-compassionate-use" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Only for one patient, with a doctor's declaration<Cite id="igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring" /> |
| Must a company be developing it? | <Verdict is="yes" /> The company applies<Cite id="ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> The company applies<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> An application or trials must be under way<Cite id="cbg-compassionate-use-criteria" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No, but a supplier needs an exemption from the inspectorate<Cite id="igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring" /> |
| Must more evidence follow? | <Verdict is="yes" /> The missing studies must be delivered<Cite id="ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> The real benefit must be confirmed<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> The medicine is still being studied<Cite id="ema-compassionate-use" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No study; the doctor takes responsibility<Cite id="igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring" /> |

</Compare>

None of these routes is a shortcut for a product of unknown content. Our page on [legal routes](/en/legal-routes/) explains how they work for a patient in the Netherlands.

<InDetail title="The routes in more detail">

- **Accelerated assessment (EU).** A review of up to 150 days instead of 210 for medicines of major interest to public health.<Cite id="ema-accelerated-assessment" />
- **PRIME (EU, 2016).** Early EMA advice to developers of medicines for unmet needs, so their studies answer the right questions.<Cite id="ema-prime" />
- **Conditional marketing authorisation (EU, 2006).** Granted on less complete data when having the medicine now outweighs the risk of missing data; valid one year at a time.<Cite id="ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation" />
- **Authorisation under exceptional circumstances (EU).** For medicines whose full benefits cannot be shown, because the disease is rare or full data cannot be collected.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83-original,ema-epar-scenesse" /> Afamelanotide (Scenesse), for a rare disease that makes light painful, was authorised this way in 2014 and is reviewed every year.<Cite id="ema-epar-scenesse" />
- **Compassionate use (EU).** A medicine still in development for a group of seriously ill patients who cannot join a trial. In the Netherlands only a manufacturer can apply.<Cite id="ema-compassionate-use,cbg-compassionate-use-criteria" />
- **Named-patient use (in the Netherlands, the artsenverklaring).** A doctor requests an unauthorised medicine for one patient, on the doctor's own responsibility, and a pharmacy or wholesaler asks the health inspectorate for an exemption.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83-original,igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring" />
- **In the US.** Treatment use of investigational drugs for life-threatening diseases from 1987; accelerated approval on an early sign of benefit from 1992, if the company later confirms a real benefit.<Cite id="fda-2006-promoting-safe-effective-drugs" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Faster routes have costs too">

Speed moves uncertainty from before approval to after it. A 2019 review looked at 93 cancer indications given US accelerated approval from 1992 to 2017. Confirmatory trials had shown longer survival for 19, about one in five. Another 39 had confirmed only an early marker, and others were still delayed, pending or running.<Cite id="gyawali-2019-accelerated-approval" /> Of 68 cancer indications approved by the EMA from 2009 to 2013, about half had still not shown a gain in survival or quality of life after a median of 5.4 years.<Cite id="davis-2017-ema-cancer-approvals" />

Requirements have also loosened over time. The share of new US approvals supported by at least two pivotal trials fell from 80.6% in 1995–1997 to 52.8% in 2015–2017, and the authors conclude that the FDA has "increasingly accepted less data and more surrogate measures".<Cite id="darrow-2020-fda-approval-history" /> The EMA, for its part, found in a ten-year review that conditional authorisation can speed up access to new medicines.<Cite id="ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Why these routes rarely help the peptides on this site">

Every route except named-patient use needs a company that is developing the medicine, with trials running or an application filed.<Cite id="cbg-compassionate-use-criteria,ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation" /> For most peptides sold as research chemicals, the EMA has no record of an EU authorisation or application, and none has an approved Dutch compassionate-use programme, so there is little for these routes to work with.<Cite id="ema-medicines-search,cbg-compassionate-use-list" /> Named-patient use needs a doctor willing to take responsibility and a supplier that can deliver a product lawfully.<Cite id="igj-leveren-op-artsenverklaring" /> See [legal routes](/en/legal-routes/) and [talking to your doctor](/en/talking-to-your-doctor/).

</InDetail>

## Too strict, or not strict enough?

<Lead question="Is the system too strict or not strict enough?">There is evidence on both sides. This page describes it; our separate opinion piece takes a side.</Lead>

<Diagram id="caution-versus-speed-record" caption="What the record shows on each side of the debate.">

| For more caution | For more speed |
|---|---|
| Thalidomide was never approved in the US because a reviewer asked for better evidence | Studies reviewed in 1992 found the US slower than many countries to introduce new medicines |
| Of 93 US cancer approvals based on early signs of benefit, about 1 in 5 later showed longer survival | The EMA found that conditional authorisation can speed up access to new medicines |
| A trial says little about a product with different contents | Substances that no company pays to test stay untested |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="fda-kelsey-history,andersson-1992-drug-lag,gyawali-2019-accelerated-approval,ema-conditional-marketing-authorisation,ecfr-21-314-126,verbaanderd-2021-repurposing-funding" />

Both sides describe real costs. Caution that is too slow leaves patients waiting; speed that is too loose approves medicines that do not deliver. The site's signed opinion on what should change is on [the case for reform](/en/the-case-for-reform/). This page does not argue it.
