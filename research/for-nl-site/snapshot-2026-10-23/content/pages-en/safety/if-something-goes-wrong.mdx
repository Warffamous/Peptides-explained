This page is for when you feel unwell after using a peptide product, or think something has gone wrong. It is written for the Netherlands. Short notes for other countries are at the end.

<Callout type="stop" title="Call your local emergency number now if" id="stop-now">

- someone is not breathing, or cannot be woken up;
- the lips, tongue or throat swell, or breathing is hard;
- there is chest pain or pressure that does not go away;
- one side of the face or body suddenly becomes weak, or speech is slurred;
- someone is confused, very drowsy or has a fit, for example with very low blood sugar.

Do not drive yourself to hospital.

</Callout>

## Where to get help

<Lead question="Who do I call?">It depends on how fast you need help: the emergency number, your GP, or the huisartsenpost.</Lead>

<Diagram id="where-to-get-help-nl" caption="Where to get help in the Netherlands, from most to least urgent">

| How urgent is it? | Who to contact |
|---|---|
| A life may be in danger | Your local emergency number, straight away, day or night |
| It cannot wait until tomorrow, during office hours | Your own GP (huisarts) |
| It cannot wait until tomorrow, in the evening, at night or at the weekend | The out-of-hours GP service (huisartsenpost). Call before you go |
| You think you need a hospital | Call your GP or the huisartsenpost first, unless a life is in danger. They send you to the emergency department (SEH) if needed |
| It can wait a few days | Make an appointment with your GP |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="rijksoverheid-urgent-medical-help,rijksoverheid-huisartsenpost-seh" />

If you are not sure which fits, call your GP or the huisartsenpost. They will tell you what to do.<Cite id="rijksoverheid-urgent-medical-help" />

<JurisdictionCard code="NL" />

<InDetail title="Costs, and the poison centre">

- **Costs.** Care from your GP and the huisartsenpost does not use your deductible (eigen risico). Care in a hospital emergency department does.<Cite id="rijksoverheid-huisartsenpost-seh" />
- **The poison centre (NVIC)** gives advice to doctors, pharmacists and other professionals, day and night. If you think you have been poisoned, call your GP, or the huisartsenpost outside office hours. Your doctor can then call the NVIC.<Cite id="nvic-umcutrecht" />

</InDetail>

## What to tell the doctor

<Lead question="What do I tell the doctor?">What you used, when, how much and where, and everything else you take.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- **The name** on the label, and what it was sold as.
- **Where it came from**: a pharmacy, a website, a gym, a friend or a clinic.
- **When** you last used it, and since when you have used it.
- **How much and how often.** The doctor needs this to understand what happened.
- **How you took it**, and where on your body you injected.
- **Everything else you take**: medicines, supplements, alcohol and drugs.
- **Allergies** you know of.

</Checklist>

<Cite id="nhs-poisoning,vic-health-2026-retatrutide-liver,thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" />

<InDetail title="Why each detail matters">

- **The product may not be what the label says.** Some fake semaglutide held insulin, which makes blood sugar fall. If the doctor knows your product did not come from a pharmacy, they can think of this. Then they can check your blood sugar.<Cite id="stammers-2026-insulin-falsified-semaglutide,antonacci-2026-falsified-semaglutide-coma" />
- **Doctors are asked to record these details.** In Australia, liver injuries were linked to a product sold as retatrutide. The health department of Victoria then asked doctors to note the product name, its ingredients, the dose and where it was bought.<Cite id="vic-health-2026-retatrutide-liver" />
- **Say it even if it does not feel like a medicine.** In a Swiss case, a woman was in hospital with days of vomiting. Only careful questions found the likely cause: weekly injections from a "life coach". She had not thought of them as medication. Her doctors advise asking patients directly about injections and peptides.<Cite id="buzzi-2026-retatrutide-case-report" />
- **Other medicines can change the risk.** For example, GLP-1 type products together with insulin can push blood sugar too low.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy" />

</InDetail>

## Bring the product, and keep it

<Lead question="Should I bring the vial?">Yes. Bring the vial or pen with its label and box. A lab may test it.</Lead>

<Diagram id="keep-the-product" caption="Why the vial or pen helps, from your bag to the lab">

1. **Pack it safely.** Put the vial, pen or box in a closed bag or container. A used needle goes in a sharps container, not in your bag.<Cite id="nhs-poisoning,fda-sharps-disposal-steps" />
2. **Show it to the doctor.** The label tells them what the product was meant to be.<Cite id="nhs-poisoning" />
3. **A lab can test what was in it.** In Italy, a lab found insulin in a vial sold as semaglutide. In Germany, a state lab found insulin in fake pens.<Cite id="antonacci-2026-falsified-semaglutide-coma,bfarm-ozempic-falsification" />
4. **What is found can protect others.** Dutch doctors stress that quick reports to the regulator help stop fake products from spreading.<Cite id="stammers-2026-insulin-falsified-semaglutide" />

</Diagram>

Keep the rest of the product, unused, until you have recovered and decided whether to report it. See [sharps disposal](/en/safety/sharps-disposal/) for used needles.

## Why you can be open with a doctor

<Lead question="Will the doctor report me?">Dutch doctors must keep what you say secret. The exceptions are few and rare.</Lead>

<Compare title="Your doctor must keep what you say secret, apart from a few rare exceptions." detail="Medical secrecy in the Netherlands. Checked on 22 October 2026. This is not legal advice.">

| | Your doctor in the Netherlands |
|---|---|
| Must your doctor keep what you say secret? | <Verdict is="yes" /> By law, apart from a few exceptions.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-457-confidentiality,knmg-beroepsgeheim" /> |
| May your doctor refuse to answer police questions about you? | <Verdict is="yes" /> Doctors have the right to stay silent towards the police and the courts.<Cite id="knmg-beroepsgeheim-doorbreken" /> |
| Is using a product on yourself one of the exceptions? | <Verdict is="no" /> The Dutch medical association (KNMG) does not name it. A doctor may break secrecy to prevent serious harm, but only under strict rules.<Cite id="knmg-beroepsgeheim-doorbreken" /> |
| Can a doctor act if you put someone else in danger? | <Verdict is="yes" /> For example, if a drunk patient wants to drive.<Cite id="knmg-beroepsgeheim-doorbreken" /> |
| Does Lareb pass your side-effect report on to your doctor? | <Verdict is="no" /> Lareb does not share it with your doctor or pharmacist.<Cite id="lareb-bijwerkingen-melden" /> |

</Compare>

<InDetail title="The rules behind this">

- **The law.** The Dutch law on medical treatment is called the WGBO. Under it, your care provider may not give information about you to others without your consent, unless a law requires it. People directly involved in your treatment are not counted as "others".<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-457-confidentiality" />
- **The exceptions.** The Royal Dutch Medical Association (KNMG) lists when a doctor may break secrecy:
  - with your consent;
  - with consent that can be assumed, such as a referral letter;
  - when a law says the doctor must or may report something, such as some infectious diseases;
  - to prevent serious harm to you or someone else, under strict rules and only if there is no other way;
  - in a few cases of weighty interest to someone else.

  The doctor must be able to explain such a choice.<Cite id="knmg-beroepsgeheim-doorbreken" />
- **What we checked.** Some products are not authorised medicines. We looked for a Dutch rule that makes a doctor report a patient to the police for using one. We found none (checked 22 October 2026). For the law on these products, see [Dutch law](/en/legal/nl/).
- **Your file.** Your doctor will probably write down what you used. That is normal. The file is private, and you may see it and get a copy.<Cite id="nl-wgbo-art-454-456-records" />

GPs are used to hearing about difficult subjects. All doctors in the Netherlands have a duty of secrecy.<Cite id="thuisarts-naar-de-huisarts" />

</InDetail>

## Report what happened

<Lead question="Should I report it?">Yes, if you can. Report the side effect to Lareb, and a seller you doubt to the IGJ.</Lead>

<Diagram id="where-to-report-nl" caption="Where to report in the Netherlands, and what happens next">

| What to report | Where | Who can report | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| A side effect | Lareb, the Dutch side-effect centre | Anyone. Also people who used illegal medicines or bought them online | Reports help find patterns. Lareb gives no medical advice and does not tell your doctor |
| A website or seller you doubt | The Health and Youth Care Inspectorate (IGJ), through its national reporting point (Landelijk Meldpunt Zorg) | Anyone | The IGJ checks online sellers of medicines |
| A product you think is fake | Your pharmacist, if it came from a pharmacy. Otherwise the IGJ | Anyone | Regulators can warn others |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="lareb-bijwerkingen-melden,lareb-2021-illegal-medicines,igj-2025-illegal-online-medicines,igj-online-medicijnen,ema-2023-falsified-ozempic,igj-2023-falsified-ozempic" />

<InDetail title="Why reports matter">

- **They can stop a fake product from spreading.** Dutch doctors treated two people for insulin poisoning from fake semaglutide. They stress that quick reports to the regulator are essential.<Cite id="stammers-2026-insulin-falsified-semaglutide" />
- **They show patterns no single doctor can see.** The EU side-effect database held 234 reports about possible fake semaglutide. In them, low blood sugar was reported more often than with the real medicine.<Cite id="zinzi-2026-counterfeit-semaglutide-eudravigilance" />
- **The World Health Organization asks everyone** to stop using suspicious medicines and to report them.<Cite id="who-2024-falsified-semaglutide" />

</InDetail>
