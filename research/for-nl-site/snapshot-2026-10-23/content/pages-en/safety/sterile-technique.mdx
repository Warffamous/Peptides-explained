Sterile means free of living germs. Aseptic technique is the set of habits that keeps a sterile thing sterile while you use it. Health workers are taught to use these habits for every injection.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit,cdc-2024-safe-injection-practices" /> This page explains them for someone at home.

This page gives no amounts, volumes or sums. It is about keeping germs out, not about what to inject.

## How germs get under the skin

<Lead question="How do germs get in?">A needle carries germs from hands, a table, the skin or the vial into your body.</Lead>

Germs are too small to see. They live on skin, on tables and in the air. A needle that touches them carries them through the skin, past the body's first defence.

The short animation shows how germs get in and spread, one step at a time.

<Explainer id="contamination-infection" />

<InDetail title="The same chain in words, with every warning sign">

<Diagram id="contamination-to-infection" caption="How germs travel from a vial or the skin into the body">

1. **Germs are present** on the hands, the table, the rubber top of the vial, the skin, or already inside the liquid.
2. **The needle picks them up** and carries them into the tissue under the skin.
3. **Under the skin they can grow.** The skin around the spot becomes red, hot and swollen: this is a skin infection called cellulitis. Sometimes the body walls the germs off in a pocket of pus: an abscess.
4. **The germs can spread** along the lymph vessels and into the blood. In the blood they can cause sepsis, which damages organs and can kill.
5. **Warning signs** are spreading redness, heat, swelling, pus, red lines, fever or feeling very ill. Then you need a doctor quickly.

</Diagram>

<Cite id="nhs-cellulitis,nhs-skin-abscess,medlineplus-lymphangitis,nhs-sepsis,who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

</InDetail>

Every step below breaks this chain at one point. Leaving out one step leaves one way in.

## Check the product first

<Lead question="What do I check before I start?">The seal, the label, how it looks and how it was kept. Then prepare it cleanly.</Lead>

<ProductCheck />

## Before you start

<Lead question="What do I do before I start?">Wash your hands, clean a table, and lay out new equipment in sealed packs.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- **Wash your hands** with soap and running water for at least 30 seconds. Include the wrists and the spaces between the fingers. Dry them with a clean paper towel. If your hands are not visibly dirty, an alcohol hand rub also works.
- **Clear and clean a table.** Remove clutter. Wipe the surface with 70% alcohol and let it dry.
- **Lay out everything you need** on the clean surface: a new needle and syringe in sealed packs, alcohol swabs, and a sharps container within reach.
- **Check every pack.** Throw away a needle or syringe whose pack is torn, wet or past its expiry date.

</Checklist>

<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit,rivm-hygiene-piercing,ema-epar-wegovy" />

## Handling the vial

<Lead question="How do I keep the vial clean?">Wipe the rubber top, then do not touch it. Use a new needle and syringe every time.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- **Wipe the rubber top** (the stopper) with a fresh alcohol swab. Let it dry in the air before the needle goes in.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />
- **Do not touch the stopper** after you have wiped it. Do not let the needle touch anything except the stopper and, later, your skin.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />
- **Use a new sterile needle and syringe every time** you go into a vial. Never put a needle back into a vial after it has been in your skin. Germs and blood from your skin would end up in the whole vial.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit,cdc-2024-preventing-unsafe-injection,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />
- **Never leave a needle in the stopper** to use it again later.<Cite id="cdc-2024-preventing-unsafe-injection,who-2010-injection-toolkit" />
- **Never share** a vial, a syringe, a needle or the water. Hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV can pass this way, even when no blood is visible.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit,mainline-steroids-safe-injecting,drugsinfo-injecteren" />

</Checklist>

A needle is also only really sharp the first time. A reused needle is blunter and makes infection more likely.<Cite id="drugsinfo-injecteren" />

## Single-dose and multi-dose vials

<Lead question="How long can I use an opened vial?">A single-dose vial: once. A multi-dose vial: usually no more than 28 days after first use.</Lead>

<TimeStrip title="A single-dose vial is used once. A multi-dose vial is usually thrown away 28 days after first use." detail="Days after first use. Write the date on the vial. A pharmacy medicine may give its own date in the leaflet." min={0} max={30} step={7} axis="Days after first use" source="dailymed-sterile-water-label,who-2010-injection-toolkit,cdc-2024-preventing-unsafe-injection,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" rows={[
  { label: "Single-dose vial", spans: [{ from: 0, to: 0.5, text: "open once, then throw away the rest" }] },
  { label: "Vial without a preservative", spans: [{ from: 0, to: 1, text: "within 24 hours of opening, unless the maker says otherwise (WHO)" }] },
  { label: "Multi-dose vial with a preservative", spans: [{ from: 0, to: 28, text: "throw it away 28 days after first use, unless the maker gives another date (CDC)" }] },
  { label: "Compounded GLP-1 medicine in a multi-dose vial (US)", spans: [{ from: 0, to: 28, text: "never past 28 days, even with liquid left or if the instructions say longer (FDA)" }] },
]} />

<InDetail title="Why a multi-dose vial carries more risk, and how to date it">

A **single-dose vial** usually has no preservative. It is meant to be opened once. Whatever is left must be thrown away, because germs that get in can grow freely.<Cite id="dailymed-sterile-water-label,cdc-2024-preventing-unsafe-injection" />

A **multi-dose vial** is used several times. It usually contains a preservative that slows the growth of bacteria. But the preservative does not work against viruses, and it does not fully protect against bacteria either.<Cite id="cdc-2024-preventing-unsafe-injection" /> Every time a needle goes through the stopper, there is a new chance for germs to get in. That is why health workers use multi-dose vials only when there is no other option.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

If you use a multi-dose vial:

- Write the date on it when you first use it.
- Throw it away 28 days after first use. A medicine from a pharmacy may have its own date in the leaflet; follow that.<Cite id="cdc-2024-preventing-unsafe-injection" />
- For compounded GLP-1 medicines in multi-dose vials, the FDA is stricter. Throw the vial away 28 days after first use, even if there is liquid left. Do this even if the instructions say you can use it for longer.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />
- A vial without a preservative should be thrown away much sooner. WHO advises within 24 hours of opening, unless the maker says otherwise.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

</InDetail>

## Bacteriostatic water and sterile water

<Lead question="What water can I mix a powder with?">Only water made for injection. One kind may be opened more than once; the other may not.</Lead>

Some products come as a powder that must be dissolved before use. Only use water made for injection, from a sealed container. Never use tap water or bottled drinking water.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit,mainline-steroids-safe-injecting" />

There are two kinds:

<Compare title="With a preservative, the water can be opened more than once. It cannot make a dirty vial clean." detail="The two kinds of water made for injection, from their US labels and the CDC.">

| | Sterile water for injection | Bacteriostatic water |
|---|---|---|
| What is in it: is there a preservative? | <Verdict is="no" /> Only sterile water | <Verdict is="yes" /> Sterile water with a preservative (benzyl alcohol) |
| How often you may open it: more than once? | <Verdict is="no" /> Once. Throw away the rest. | <Verdict is="yes" /> Several times, as a multi-dose container |
| What the preservative does: does it kill viruses or clean a dirty vial? | <Verdict is="no" /> Not applicable: there is no preservative | <Verdict is="no" /> Slows the growth of bacteria. Does not kill viruses and does not make a dirty vial clean. |

</Compare>

<Cite id="dailymed-sterile-water-label,dailymed-bacteriostatic-water-label,cdc-2024-preventing-unsafe-injection" />

Benzyl alcohol is harmful for newborn babies, so bacteriostatic water must never be used for them.<Cite id="dailymed-bacteriostatic-water-label" />

## Mixing a powder with water

<Lead question="How do I mix a powder without harming it?">Let the water run down the inside wall, then roll or swirl gently. Do not shake.</Lead>

The same rules apply as for any vial. Wipe both stoppers. Use a new sterile needle and syringe.<Cite id="who-2010-injection-toolkit" />

<Diagram id="mix-gently" caption="Mix by rolling or swirling the vial gently, never by shaking it">

- Let the water run slowly down the inside wall of the vial.
- Mix by gently rolling or swirling the vial. **Do not shake it.**
- When it is dissolved, the liquid should be clear and without bits. If it is not, do not use it.

</Diagram>

<Cite id="dailymed-egrifta-sv-label,mainline-steroids-safe-injecting" />

Mixing your own vial also means working out amounts. Mistakes happen, and some have been serious.<Cite id="fda-2024-compounded-semaglutide-dosing-errors" /> This site gives no amounts. A pharmacist or doctor can check your understanding of a prescribed medicine.

<InDetail title="Why shaking can harm a peptide">

Why not shake? Peptides are fragile chains. Shaking and changes in temperature can make them clump together (aggregate).<Cite id="zapadka-2017-peptide-aggregation" /> <Claim level="in-vitro">Clumped peptide can lose its activity</Claim>.<Cite id="zapadka-2017-peptide-aggregation" /> <Claim level="human-limited">Clumps in protein medicines have also been linked to immune reactions in patients</Claim>.<Cite id="rosenberg-2006-aggregates-immunology" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Dosing mistakes the FDA has seen">

The FDA has reports of people who injected 5 to 20 times the intended amount of compounded semaglutide from multi-dose vials. Most were not used to measuring with a syringe. Others had been given wrong conversions between milligrams, millilitres and "units". Some needed hospital care.<Cite id="fda-2024-compounded-semaglutide-dosing-errors" />

</InDetail>

## Storage and the cold chain

<Lead question="How should it be stored?">Follow the leaflet. Changes in temperature and shaking can break a peptide down.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Wegovy, for example, belongs in the fridge and must not freeze" value="2–8 °C" label="in the fridge, says the Wegovy label. Keep it away from the cooling element." who="EU product information for Wegovy, one example of a peptide medicine label" source="ema-epar-wegovy" level="established" />

The cold chain means keeping a medicine cold at every step, from the factory to your fridge.

For products from outside a pharmacy, nobody knows how they were stored or shipped. The FDA has received complaints about compounded GLP-1 products that arrived warm or with too few ice packs. It advises not to use an injectable GLP-1 product that arrives warm.<Cite id="fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />

<InDetail title="What the Wegovy label says, and why temperature matters">

Authorised peptide medicines come with clear storage rules. The Wegovy label is an example. Store it in the fridge (2–8 °C), away from the cooling element. Out of the fridge, it may be kept below 30 °C only for the time the leaflet states. Do not freeze it. Protect it from light. Do not use it if it has been frozen or left in direct sunlight.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy" />

The reason is chemistry. <Claim level="in-vitro">Changes in temperature and shaking can break peptides down or make them clump</Claim>.<Cite id="manning-2010-protein-stability,zapadka-2017-peptide-aggregation" /> A freeze-dried powder is usually more stable than a liquid. But freeze-drying itself can make some peptides clump more after mixing.<Cite id="zapadka-2017-peptide-aggregation" />

</InDetail>

## When to throw a vial away

<Lead question="When should I throw a vial away?">When it looks wrong, was stored wrong, is too old, or may have germs in it.</Lead>

<Checklist>

- It should be clear, but it is cloudy, coloured, or has bits or flakes in it.
- The vial is cracked, or the seal or cap is broken or missing.
- It has been frozen, or left warm or in the sun.
- It is a multi-dose vial more than 28 days after first use, or it has no date.
- You touched the stopper, or the needle touched something else.
- A used needle went into it, or someone else used it.
- You do not know how old it is or how it was stored.

</Checklist>

<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy,dailymed-egrifta-sv-label,dailymed-sterile-water-label,who-2010-injection-toolkit,cdc-2024-preventing-unsafe-injection,fda-unapproved-glp1-concerns" />

When in doubt, throw it out.

## Why a powder is not proof of sterility

<Lead question="Is a dry powder sterile?">Not by itself. Freeze-drying does not kill germs.</Lead>

<KeyNumber title="Germs were found in vials that had never been opened" value="749" label="people had infections by 1 July 2013, and 61 of them died" who="US outbreak investigation, 2012–2013: fungal infections after steroid injections from one pharmacy" source="smith-2013-fungal-methylprednisolone" level="human-limited" />

Many people trust a freeze-dried powder because it looks dry and clean. But dry and clean-looking does not mean free of germs.

So sterile technique protects you from germs **you** might add. It cannot remove germs that were already in the product. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

<InDetail title="What freeze-drying does, and how a factory makes a powder sterile">

Freeze-drying removes water from a frozen product. It does not kill germs. In fact, laboratories use freeze-drying to keep collections of bacteria alive.<Cite id="morgan-2006-drying-microorganisms" />

In a proper factory, sterility comes from other steps. The liquid is passed through a filter that holds back bacteria. The vials are filled in germ-free conditions. The freeze-dryer itself is sterilised. Regulators inspect these steps.<Cite id="fda-1993-lyophilization-guide" /> If they are skipped, the powder can carry germs and bacterial toxins (endotoxins), and you cannot see them.<Cite id="fda-1993-lyophilization-guide" />

Germs are not the only thing that can be wrong. The plate shows six problems a vial from outside a pharmacy may have.

<Infographic id="online-vial-risks" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="The 2012 outbreak: germs inside unopened vials">

A real example shows what this means. In 2012, US health officials traced an outbreak of fungal infections to steroid injections from one pharmacy. Fungus was found in vials that had never been opened. By 1 July 2013, 749 people had infections and 61 had died.<Cite id="smith-2013-fungal-methylprednisolone" /> The problem was not how the injections were given. The germs were inside before anyone used a needle.

</InDetail>
