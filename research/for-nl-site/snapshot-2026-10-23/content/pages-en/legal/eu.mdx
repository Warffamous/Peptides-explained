<Lead question="Why does EU law matter for peptides?">It sets the rules that Dutch, Belgian and German medicines law all follow.</Lead>

<Diagram id="eu-law-layers" caption="EU medicines law works in layers. The EU sets common rules; each country applies and enforces them.">

| Layer | Examples | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| EU directives | Directive 2001/83/EC, the EU code for medicines | Sets the definition of a medicine, the licence rule and the advertising rules. Each country writes them into its own law.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> |
| EU regulations | Regulation 726/2004 (EU-wide licences), Regulation 536/2014 (clinical trials) | Apply directly in every EU country.<Cite id="eu-regulation-726-2004,eu-regulation-536-2014" /> |
| EU Court of Justice | Rulings on what a "medicine" is, or what counts as advertising | Explains what EU rules mean; national courts must follow it.<Cite id="cjeu-2014-d-and-g,cjeu-2009-damgaard" /> |
| National bodies | CBG and IGJ (Netherlands), FAMHP (Belgium) | Grant national licences, check the market, fine and seize.<Cite id="nl-gmw-art-40-handelsvergunning,nl-gnw-art-100-supervision,nl-gnw-art-101-fines,be-medicines-law-1964,famhp-postal-seizure-qa" /> |

</Diagram>

So the core rules on this page apply in every EU country. Penalties, enforcement and some exceptions differ by country. See [the Netherlands](/en/legal/nl/) and [Belgium](/en/legal/be/).

## What counts as a medicine

<Lead question="What makes a product a medicine in EU law?">Being offered as a treatment, or being able to change how the body works through a drug-like effect.</Lead>

<Compare title="A product can be a medicine in two ways. A 'not for human use' label settles neither." detail="Directive 2001/83/EC, article 1(2), as explained by the EU Court of Justice.">

| | By presentation (article 1(2)(a)) | By function (article 1(2)(b)) |
|---|---|---|
| Can claims, pictures or a website make it a medicine? | <Verdict is="yes" /> What counts is the impression an ordinary, well-informed person gets, even from hints.<Cite id="cjeu-1983-van-bennekom" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> This test looks at what the substance can do in the body.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-hecht-pharma" /> |
| Must science show that it works? | <Verdict is="no" /> A product that does little can still be offered as a medicine.<Cite id="cjeu-1983-van-bennekom" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> It must be able to change body functions in a way that really matters.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-hecht-pharma" /> |
| Does a "research use only" label decide it? | <Verdict is="no" /> A label is only one part of how a product is presented.<Cite id="cjeu-1983-van-bennekom" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> The label does not change what the substance does.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-hecht-pharma,cjeu-2014-d-and-g" /> |

</Compare>

Sometimes a product could be a medicine and also something else, such as a food supplement. Then medicines law wins, once its medicine status is clear.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83,cjeu-2009-hecht-pharma" /> Authorities decide case by case.<Cite id="cjeu-2014-d-and-g" /> More on labels: [research use only](/en/legal/research-use-only/).

<InDetail title="What the EU Court of Justice has said">

**Van Bennekom (1983).** A Dutch case about vitamin pills. "Presentation" must be read broadly. A product is presented as a medicine when it is recommended as such on labels, in leaflets or by word of mouth. It is also presented as one when an ordinary, well-informed person gets that clear impression, even if it is only implied. The pill or capsule form can be strong evidence, but not the only evidence.<Cite id="cjeu-1983-van-bennekom" />

**Hecht-Pharma (2009).** A product is not a medicine "by function" if, used as intended, it cannot change body functions in a way that really matters. The "in doubt, medicines law wins" rule only applies once its medicine status is shown.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-hecht-pharma" />

**D. and G. (2014).** A case about synthetic cannabinoids. Authorities decide case by case, looking at what the product contains and what it does as far as science can show. They also look at how it is used, how widely it is sold, how well people know it, and its risks. The "function" test covers substances that can benefit health, not substances taken only to get high.<Cite id="cjeu-2014-d-and-g" />

</InDetail>

## The licence rule

<Lead question="Can a medicine be sold without a licence?">No. Every medicine needs a licence, from the EU or from one country.</Lead>

<Compare title="Some medicines must have an EU-wide licence. Others can have a national one." detail="Directive 2001/83/EC article 6 and Regulation (EC) No 726/2004 article 3 and annex I.">

| | EU-wide licence (centralised procedure) | National licence |
|---|---|---|
| Valid in every EU country? | <Verdict is="yes" /> One licence for the whole EU.<Cite id="eu-regulation-726-2004" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Valid in the country that granted it.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> |
| Required for biotech medicines, and for new medicines for diabetes, cancer and some other serious diseases? | <Verdict is="yes" /> These must go through the EU route.<Cite id="eu-regulation-726-2004" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Not possible for these.<Cite id="eu-regulation-726-2004" /> |
| Can a product with neither licence be sold legally? | <Verdict is="no" /> Not as a medicine.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Not as a medicine.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> |

</Compare>

Semaglutide (Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro) have EU-wide licences.<Cite id="ema-epar-wegovy,ema-epar-mounjaro" /> BPC-157 and TB-500 have no EU-wide licence.<Cite id="ema-medicines-search" /> We know of no EU country that has licensed them either.

<InDetail title="What a licence brings with it">

A licence is more than permission to sell. It comes with duties that protect the people who use the medicine:

- **Quality.** Makers need a manufacturing licence.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> They must follow good manufacturing practice (GMP), and use active substances made under GMP too.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> For injections there are extra rules for sterile production.<Cite id="eudralex-volume-4-gmp" />
- **Safety monitoring.** The marketing authorisation holder must run a system to collect, assess and act on side effects.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" />
- **Information.** The product comes with an official summary of product characteristics (SmPC) and a leaflet.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" />

A product sold without a licence carries none of these duties. See [research use only](/en/legal/research-use-only/).

</InDetail>

## The narrow exceptions

<Lead question="Are there exceptions to the licence rule?">Yes, a few narrow ones. Mainly: one patient with a medical need, very ill groups, and trials.</Lead>

<Compare title="Each exception is narrow and needs a doctor or a formal programme." detail="Directive 2001/83/EC article 5(1); Regulation (EC) No 726/2004 article 83; Regulation (EU) No 536/2014.">

| | Named patient (Directive, article 5(1)) | Compassionate use (Regulation 726/2004, article 83) | Clinical trial (Regulation 536/2014) |
|---|---|---|---|
| For one named patient? | <Verdict is="yes" /> On a doctor's order, under the doctor's personal responsibility.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> For a group of seriously ill patients.<Cite id="eu-regulation-726-2004" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> For the people taking part in the trial.<Cite id="eu-regulation-536-2014" /> |
| Must the medicine be in formal development? | <Verdict is="no" /> Not in EU law; national rules add conditions.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83,nl-regeling-gnw-art-3-17" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> A licence application or trials must be under way.<Cite id="eu-regulation-726-2004" /> | <Verdict is="yes" /> The trial itself is the development.<Cite id="eu-regulation-536-2014" /> |
| Can cost or convenience be the reason? | <Verdict is="no" /> Only medical reasons count.<Cite id="cjeu-2012-commission-v-poland" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> Only for patients who cannot be treated well with approved medicines.<Cite id="eu-regulation-726-2004" /> | <Verdict is="no" /> No one may be pushed to take part, also not with money.<Cite id="eu-regulation-536-2014" /> |

</Compare>

A pharmacy may also make a medicine itself for one patient, on a prescription. The licence rule does not cover that either.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83,cjeu-2015-abcur" /> How these work in practice in the Netherlands: [legal routes](/en/legal-routes/).

<InDetail title="The named-patient exception, as the EU Court reads it">

Article 5(1) lets a country make an exception "to fulfil special needs". The medicine must be supplied on a genuine order from a health professional, for one patient under their direct personal responsibility.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" />

In 2012 the EU Court of Justice explained how narrow this is. Exceptions are read strictly. "Special needs" means individual cases with medical reasons. The doctor must have examined the patient and prescribed on purely medical grounds. It only applies when no approved equivalent is on the market. Money is not enough of a reason.<Cite id="cjeu-2012-commission-v-poland" />

</InDetail>

<InDetail title="Compassionate use and clinical trials">

**Compassionate use.** A country may give an unlicensed medicine to a group of very ill patients. Their disease is long-term, seriously disabling or life-threatening. They cannot be treated well with an approved medicine. This route is for medicines that can get an EU-wide licence. The medicine must have an EU licence application, or be in clinical trials. The country must tell the EMA.<Cite id="eu-regulation-726-2004" />

**Clinical trials.** Since 31 January 2022 one EU law covers trials of medicines, through one online system, CTIS.<Cite id="ema-clinical-trials-regulation" /> A trial may only run if its expected benefits justify the risks. Everyone in it must give informed consent after a clear explanation. You can leave at any time, without giving a reason and without any harm to you.<Cite id="eu-regulation-536-2014" />

</InDetail>

## Advertising

<Lead question="Can unlicensed medicines be advertised?">No. And prescription medicines may not be advertised to the public at all.</Lead>

<Diagram id="eu-advertising-rules" caption="What EU law treats as advertising for medicines, and what it does not.">

| Counts as advertising | Does not count |
|---|---|
| Any information, canvassing or inducement meant to promote prescribing, supply, sale or use of a medicine | The label and the patient leaflet |
| Advertising to the public, or to doctors and pharmacists | Answers to a specific question about a medicine, without promotion |
| Free samples, gifts and paid events meant to promote prescribing | Factual notices, such as a pack change or a side-effect warning, without product claims |
| | Information about health or disease that does not refer, even indirectly, to a medicine |

</Diagram>

<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" />

Countries must forbid all advertising for a medicine without a licence. They must also forbid advertising prescription medicines to the public.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> An independent writer can be advertising too, if the purpose is to promote.<Cite id="cjeu-2009-damgaard" />

## Fake medicines and online pharmacies

<Lead question="How does the EU protect people from fake medicines?">With a code on each pack of prescription drugs, and a logo for legal sellers online.</Lead>

<TimeStrip title="The EU's rules against fake medicines took effect step by step from 2013." detail="When each rule started to apply across the EU. Belgium, Greece and Italy had until 2025 for the safety code." min={2011} max={2027} step={2} axis="Year" source="eu-directive-2011-62,eu-regulation-699-2014,eu-regulation-2016-161" checked="2026-10-22" rows={[

  { label: "EU logo for legal online sellers", spans: [{ from: "2015-06-25", to: "2026-10-22", text: "in every EU country by mid-2015" }] },
  { label: "Safety code and seal on prescription packs", spans: [{ from: "2019-02-09", to: "2026-10-22", text: "since 9 February 2019; in Belgium, Greece and Italy by 2025" }] },

A legal online seller must be allowed to supply medicines, be registered, and show the EU logo. The medicines it sells must be legal in the buyer's country.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> So no seller can legally offer an unlicensed peptide with that logo. Most prescription medicines carry a unique code and a seal that pharmacies check.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83,eu-regulation-2016-161" />

<InDetail title="How the logo and the safety code work">

The logo is the same across the EU, and shows which EU country the seller is based in. Clicking it should lead to the national list of registered sellers.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83,eu-regulation-699-2014" />

The safety code is a unique number on each pack, plus a seal that shows if the pack was opened. Pharmacies scan the code to check that the pack is genuine.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83,eu-regulation-2016-161" /> Belgium, Greece and Italy already had their own pack checks. They could wait, but had to switch by 9 February 2025 at the latest.<Cite id="eu-regulation-2016-161" /> A vial sold outside the legal supply chain has no such check. See [product risk](/en/safety/product-risk/).

</InDetail>

## What is changing

<Lead question="Is EU medicines law about to change?">Yes. A reform was agreed in December 2025. On 22 October 2026 it was not yet adopted.</Lead>

<Diagram id="eu-pharma-reform-steps" caption="The new EU medicines law: where the reform stood on 22 October 2026.">

1. **26 April 2023.** The European Commission proposes a new directive and a new regulation. The directive would replace Directive 2001/83/EC.<Cite id="ep-oeil-2023-0132-cod,ep-oeil-2023-0131-cod" />
2. **11 December 2025.** Parliament and Council reach political agreement.<Cite id="ema-pharma-reform-page" />
3. **1 October 2026.** The Council publishes its formal position.<Cite id="ep-oeil-2023-0132-cod,ep-oeil-2023-0131-cod" />
4. **Next step.** The file is waiting for Parliament's second reading. It lists 23 November 2026 as an indicative date for the plenary vote.<Cite id="ep-oeil-2023-0132-cod,ep-oeil-2023-0131-cod" />
5. **After that.** The EMA expects the new acts to enter into force in 2026, with time until 2028 for countries to change their own laws.<Cite id="ema-pharma-reform-page" />

</Diagram>

Until then, the rules on this page apply. We follow the reform on [what's changing](/en/legal/whats-changing/).

## Contacts

<Lead question="Who can I contact at EU level?">The EMA for EU-wide medicines. Side effects are reported in your own country.</Lead>

<JurisdictionCard code="EU" />

The EMA assesses medicines for an EU-wide licence. Its public list shows which medicines have one.<Cite id="ema-medicines-search" /> Every EU country must make it easy for patients to report side effects to their national authority.<Cite id="eu-directive-2001-83" /> In the Netherlands you report to Lareb; in Belgium, to the FAMHP.<Cite id="lareb-bijwerkingen-melden,famhp-adverse-reaction-qa" />
